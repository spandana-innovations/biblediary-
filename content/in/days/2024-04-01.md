    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Matthew 28:8–15"
    audio: null
    body: |
      There are not two wrong attitudes possible with regard to the resurrection of Jesus. The first is illustrated by the decision of the chief priests in today’s gospel to bribe the soldiers to report that the disciples stole Christ’s body while they, the soldiers, were asleep: they want *to explain away*the resurrection. </span></span></span></span>



      The other wrong attitude is in the conviction of those overzealous and rationally inclined Christians who attempt to prove the resurrection by reason. I am convinced that the resurrection is not a doctrine to be proved, but a truth *to be believed*and to be experienced in our lives now. As Thomas Merton put it: “We are called not only to believe that Christ rose from the dead, thereby proving that he was God: we are called upon to experience the Resurrection in our own lives by entering into this dynamic movement, by following Christ who lives in us” *(He Is Risen,*Argus Communications 8).</span></span></span></span>



      But how do we experience the resurrection, how does it give new meaning to our lives now? This was the problem of the people of Jerusalem to whom Peter spoke on that first Pentecost, and we shall see in future readings how they came to find an answer. For now we can say that at least part of the answer was *conversion,*wholehearted conversion to Christ and his way. And if that was good enough for them, it should be good enough for us.</span></span></span></span>



      The resurrection was a new beginning for Jesus: his life in this new, resurrected body of his is no longer constrained by the former limitations of space, matter, or time. It surely was a new beginning for the apostles, as we shall see in the weeks ahead. How different their lives after the resurrection and especially after Christ sends them the Holy Spirit from the naive, misinformed thinking, speaking, and acting they manifested before Jesus died! That difference gives us a real insight into the nature of conversion.</span></span></span></span>

      Today’s Gospel portrays two kinds of people. One represented by Mary Magdalene and the other by the chief priests. Both the groups receive the message about the resurrection of Jesus. But their responses are different. While the women are filled with ‘fear and great joy,’ and ‘ran to tell his disciples’ the news of the resurrection, the chief priests and elders plot a way to hide the truth by paying money to the guards. The risen Jesus meets the women and they respond to him by taking hold of his feet and worshipping him. Jesus reassures them by saying, ‘Do not be afraid...’ To which group do I belong? Those who encountered the risen Lord and ran to proclaim him to others or those who try to hide the truth with the power of money?</span></span></span></span>

      Saint Hugh </span></span>- *Bishop Confessor (1053-1132)*</span></span></span></span>



      It was at the early age of 25 and while still a layman that Hugh, a native of the Dauphine (south-eastern France), was made Canon at Valence. An active defendant of the reforms promulgated by Pope Saint Gregory VII, he was consecrated Bishop of Grenoble two years later and laboured zealously, though unsuccessfully, to eradicate the evils of simony and concubinage in his diocese. In 1082, convinced of his own ineffectiveness, he resigned from the Bishopric and proceeded to become a Benedictine monk at the austere abbey of La Chaise-Dieu in Auvergne; at the express orders of the Pope, however, he returned to his post the following year and there remained until he was called to his reward on 1 April 1132, what with each of the four succeeding Popes turning down his plea to be relieved of his duties. He was canonized two years later by Innocent III.</span></span></span></span>



      In 1084 Hugh personally conducted Saint Bruno and his six companions to the wild Alpine solitude near Grenoble, called Chartreuse, and presented them with the land on which they went on to construct their first Carthusian monastery, the Grande Chartreuse (Great Chartre-house). Here he frequently shared in the </span></span>monks’ life of poverty, prayer and solitary study. His personal austerity and excessive fasts that affected his health were only moderated at the insistence of Saint Bruno, his spiritual director. </span></span></span></span>



      To relieve the distress of the poor, Hugh, who always travelled on foot, is said to have disposed of his golden chalice and Episcopal ring and was famed for his equitable judgements, which were readily accepted by both litigants. </span></span></span></span>
