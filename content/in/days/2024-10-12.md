  - key: homily

    ref: "Luke 11:27–28"

    body: |
      When the woman praises Jesus and his mother, declaring her “blessed” for having borne such a great prophet, Jesus again redirects that praise to God, and to those whose lives praise Him: “Rather, blest are those who hear the word of God and keep it.” Jesus could read the woman’s thoughts, as He had read His disciples’ thoughts. To the disciples Jesus had warned, “I saw Satan fall from heaven like lightning.” In other words, don’t take credit as if you did the work yourselves. It was God’s work, so give the praise to Him. To the woman Jesus has the same advice: “If your meaning is, ‘too bad I couldn’t be the Messiah’s mother,’ just remember, you can be close to God, too. You can hear the word of God and keep it, too. And that is what made Mary, my Mother, the great woman she is. Mary has always turned her privileges and honours, as well as her trials, her poverty and hardships into praise of God. You can do the same, and you will be blessed. Most happy and blessed are those who listen to God’s message, and respond to it, obey it, keep faithful to it in their own lives.



      *(Pray Today’s Gospel, Bernard C. Mischke O.S.C. and Fritz Mischke O.S.C.)*
  - key: reflection

    body: |
      Obedience is an infallible guide of spiritual life. Obedience to authority is looked at as our duty. Obedience to equals is seen as courtesy. Obedience to those junior to ourselves is a sign of nobility. Obedience to God’s will is our supreme responsibility. Jesus indirectly, presents Mary as an example of such obedience to God’s word. The woman in the crowd could appreciate Mary’s physical motherhood. But Jesus draws the attention of the crowd to Mary’s spiritual motherhood. Such motherhood includes obedience to God’s will. Mary’s readiness to accept Jesus in her womb was preceded by her willingness to accept him in her heart. *In which areas of my life do I find it difficult to obey authority and submit to the will of God?*

    audio: null
    body: |
      St Wilfrid, a native of Northumbria (England), who was to become one of the great men of his age, is best remembered for his part in championing the supreme ecclesiastical authority of Rome. Born of noble stock and educated at the court of King Osway, at Lindisfarne and at Canterbury, he accompanied St Benedict Biscop to Rome as a young man. On his return he founded the monastery of Ripon and, though aged only 24, became its first Abbot. Here he introduced the Benedictine rule, and five years later was ordained priest. At the famous Synod of Whitby, in 664, he was able to persuade the majority to accept for all of England the Roman usage concerning Easter and the tonsure, in preference to the customs which had been introduced by the Scottish monks from Iona.



      Shortly after, Wilfrid was made Archbishop of York and received his consecration in France. His energetic administration witnessed the founding of a large number of Benedictine monasteries and the further extension of Roman usage and church chant. But when King Egfrid and St Theodore proceeded to divide his archdiocese into three parts, St Wilfrid went in appeal to Rome — the first known instance of an English Prelate’s appeal. He won his case, but upon his return was imprisoned and then exiled by the King. He thus preached the faith for five years in Sussex and Wessex, and there converted King St Ceadwalla. Not until 686, was a reconciliation effected so that he could return to York. He died on 24 April c.709.
