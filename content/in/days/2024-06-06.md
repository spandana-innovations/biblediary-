  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Mark 12:28b–34"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Jesus was asked several questions about how we should relate to God. One of these is in today’s gospel. A lawyer comes up and asks him, “Which is the first of all the commandments?” There were a lot of rules and regulations in the Jewish religion at that time. He wanted to know which one was the most important.



      Our Lord’s answer went well beyond the question. He was asked about the first commandment; but his reply includes both the first and the second commandment. The first is to love God with all our heart, soul, mind and strength, and the second is to love our neighbour as ourselves. In that way he shows that these two commandments are inseparable. We cannot love God without loving our neighbour, and in loving our neighbour we are, at the same time, loving God. Yet, the two commandments are not on the same level, one is first and one is second. It is the love of God which is to be the primary love in our lives. We owe the greatest devotion to God. As Jesus says in one of the other gospels, “Seek first the kingdom of God.” God as revealed in Jesus is to be our greatest love. If we are caught up into a loving relationship with God, it will overflow into a love of all those whom God loves, and our various human loves for other people will reflect something of God’s love for them.

    title: Reflection
    ref: null
    audio: null
    body: |
      The call to love is the common element in the two commandments that Jesus proffers. The difference lies in the object of that love. The first commandment reminds us to love God above everything. Our love of God is always a response to God’s love for us. The more we give of ourselves to God, the more we will love Him. And the second commandment, “love of neighbour’’ flows from our love for God. The journey in love to God leads us to the other; in loving God we are caught up in God’s love for others. When we live by these two commandments of love, we are on the cusp of the kingdom of God that Jesus came to establish here on Earth.

      St Norbert (Bishop, Confessor) (c.1060-1134)

      St Norbert was related to the German imperial house and was a Canon at Xanten Rhineland (Germany), his birthplace. He nevertheless led a worldly life as almoner at the court of Emperor Henry V until, in 1115, he narrowly escaped death by lightning that had thrown him off his frightened horse. An utterly changed man, he thenceforth led a life of severe penance, received Holy Orders and attempted to reform the Chapter at Xanten. But meeting with persecution and ridicule, he renounced his canonry, gave all his possessions to the poor and wandered barefoot and penniless to St Gilles in France, where Pope Gelasius II gave him faculties to preach wherever he went.

      In 1121, at the request of Pope Callixtus II and Bartholomew, the Bishop of Laon, Norbert founded the Relgious Order of Canons Regular of Strict Observance at Premontre under the reformed rule of St Augustine, for parochial, educational and missionary work. It grew with such rapidity that by 1131, there were already 500 Priests, Clerics and Lay-brothers.

      Through his zealous preaching, Norbert had the people of the Low Counties abandon the Sacramentarian heresy, i.e., the denial of the Real Presence. As chancellor and adviser to the King, he accompanied the latter’s army to Rome to aid the cause of the rightful Pope, Innocent II against the pretensions of the anti-pope Pietro di Leoni. Soon after his return to the north, Norbert fell critically ill and died in 1134 and was canonized in 1852.



      *<span class="subhead">Reflection:</span>* “He was a most eloquent preacher; after long meditation, he would preach the Word of God and with his fiery eloquence, he purged vices, refined virtues and filled souls of goodwill with the warmth of wisdom” (Biography of St Norbert).
