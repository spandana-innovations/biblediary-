    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Luke 10:1-12"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Today we celebrate the Feast of St. Francis Xavier, one of the greatest missionaries of the 1500s, who was instrumental in the spread of Christianity in India.</span></span></span></span></span>



      The readings today are all linked to evangelization. The First Reading speaks of Jeremiah’s commissioning as a Prophet; in the Second Reading Paul speaks of the afflictions & persecutions that come with the job; and finally in the Gospel Jesus sends out the 70 to proclaim with express instructions on how to go about it.</span></span></span></span></span>



      The young prophet Jeremiah, in a close encounter with God, is commissioned to be a prophet who will carry God’s message of doom to the people of Judah. “The word of the Lord” comes to Jeremiah: “Before I formed you in the womb I knew you. Before you were born I consecrated you; I appointed your prophet to the nations,” says the Lord to Jeremiah (cf. Ex.3:1-6, 17; Is.6:1-5; Ez.1-3).</span></span></span></span></span>



      From the earliest scripture, we have seen the power of the Word of God in creation, and in the shaping of human history. The word “formed” is first used in Gen.2:7, where God creates man from the dust of the earth. It is also related to the Hebrew word for “potter”. The term “knew” (“yada” in Hebrew) suggests a deep intimacy – like that of a man and a woman (cf.Gen.4:1). It is a relationship that comes with commitment. Here it refers to Yahweh’s deep commitment to Jeremiah, from the very “beginning”.</span></span></span></span></span>



      And so Jeremiah is “consecrated” - the Hebrew term “hiqdis” means to be “consecrated” or “set apart” for religious purposes. Like the Temple and the Tabernacle is set apart for the worship of God, so also Jeremiah is “set apart” for his ministry of being a “prophet to the nations.”</span></span></span></span></span>



      The Biblical prophetic ministry involved much more than prophesizing the future – it involved confronting social injustice, calling the people to repentance and in Jeremiah’s case, predicting the coming doom to a nation if the people failed to change their ways. Naturally the young prophet resists “I am only a boy!” he exclaims; but God dismisses it “you shall speak what I command you” and a reassurance “I am with you to deliver you”.</span></span></span></span></span>



      In the Gospel, Jesus “appointed 72 others” to proclaim the Kingdom. He sends them out “two by two ahead of him”; their job is to proclaim the Kingdom (cf.Lk9:1-6). It is not an easy job, and as carriers of the Good News, they are bound to face severe opposition; they are sent “like lambs among wolves”. However, “Whoever listens to you listens to me; whoever rejects you rejects me; but whoever rejects me rejects him who sent me.” (Lk.10:16)</span></span></span></span></span>



      Jesus came to restore wholeness; the disciples will do the same. To those who accept their message of salvation, wish them peace; with those who reject the message, they are to distance themselves. The disbelievers condemn themselves much like Chorazin and Bethsaida who have seen and yet not believed. </span></span></span></span></span>



      In the Second Reading, Paul speaks of the ministry as carrying “treasure in clay jars”. Clay jars were very insignificant in the Near East; if they broke, they were simply discarded. Paul compares Christians to “clay jars” - insignificant and clearly replaceable. The “treasure”, in contrast, is the greatness of the Gospel of Christ, which is held in the weak clay jars.</span></span></span></span></span>



      So why does God put such an invaluable treasure in a weak jars? So that it is the treasure and not the jar that becomes the focus of the proclamation: “to show this all surpassing power is from God and not from us” i.e. the power is in the treasure (message) and not the jar (proclaimer). </span></span></span></span></span>



      Yet the power of the Word gives power to the proclaimer; and hence we are “afflicted but not crushed; perplexed, but not driven to despair; persecuted, but not forsaken; stricken but not destroyed; always carrying in our bodies the death of Christ, so that the life of Jesus (treasure) may be made visible in our mortal flesh (clay jar)”.</span></span></span></span></span>



      Authentic evangelizing comes with true conviction “I believed and so I spoke”. Paul’s conviction comes from his experience of the Risen Christ: “the one who raised the Lord Jesus Christ will raise us also with Jesus”. Thus he proclaims so that more & more people may know & experience Grace, and gives thanks to the glory of God.</span></span></span></span></span>



      By virtue of our Baptism, we are all called to be a Prophet, Priest & King – all three roles are focussed on giving glory to God. Today, on the feast of St. Francis Xavier, we are reminded that as a Church, our primary job is evangelization; to proclaim Christ at all times – if & when possible, with words, but always with our lives.</span></span></span></span></span>

      The mission of the disciples in today’s Gospel and that of Francis Xavier, whose solemnity we celebrate today, has one epicentre: Proclamation of the Kingdom of God, that is, leading all people to accept God’s unconditional love. Francis Xavier often prayed to God: “Give me souls; take away everything else.” The disciples are forbidden from carrying anything for themselves. Thus, both relied on God for their missionary success. Every Christian is mandated with the same mission. The required strength for the mission is provided by God, who strengthens every Christian in his/her weakness to be strong like Jeremiah, to manage with the minimum like the disciples in the Gospel, and to bear the afflictions, perplexities, despair, and various other sufferings as did St Paul and other apostles.</span></span></span>

    title: Saint of the Day
    saintName: "Saint Francis Xavier - Confessor (1506 -1552)"
    ref: "Saint Francis Xavier - Confessor (1506 -1552)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Born on 7 April 1506 to Don Juan de Jasu and Doña Maria de Azpilcueta y Aznárez, Francis Xavier was one of the six who, with Ignatius de Loyola, took their vows as the first Jesuits on 15 August 1534. Ordained priest on 24 June 1537, he celebrated his first mass after a rigorous 40-day fast and spiritual preparation. He was Ignatius’ secretary in Rome when he was sent by King John III of Portugal to the East Indies. At only a day’s notice, Francis set out with the Portuguese ambassador for Lisbon. The 11,000-mile sea voyage having wintered at Mozambique, he landed in Goa, the centre of Portuguese activity in the East, on 6 May 1542 and, learning the local language, nursed the sick, preached, and taught catechism, before proceeding to the Fishery Coast and baptizing the pearl fishers. </span></span></span></span></span>



      In 1544 Francis proceeded to Ceylon, thence to Malacca and the Spice Islands and, some believe, even the Philippines. Despite language constraints and Buddhist opposition in Japan where he had landed on 15 August 1549, his preaching, accompanied by numerous miracles, was so effective that within some 40 years, the faithful in Japan numbered 400,000!</span></span></span></span></span>



      Francis succumbed to fever on 3 December 1552 during a fruitless three month-long wait on the Sancian island of Canton for Chinese pirates who, for a tidy sum, were to smuggle him into China. In 1553, his body, found incorrupt and fresh after over two months of burial, is venerated till today in the Bom Jesus Basilica in Old Goa, even as his right arm, severed at the elbow at the instance of the then Jesuit General Claudio Acquaviva, lies enshrined in the Church of the Gesù in Rome. </span></span></span></span></span>



      Beatified in 1619 and canonized in 1622, Francis has been named Apostle of the Indies and patron of foreign missions.</span></span></span></span></span>
