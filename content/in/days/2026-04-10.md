      Reflection: 1



      Even after meeting the risen Jesus, Peter and companions are still confused and uncertain about what to do; so they return to their old, familiar profession. Jesus does not shame or blame them. He now meets them again where he met them when they were busy with their fishing activity and called them to follow him. We may be tempted to give up what we have begun with Jesus. Like Peter and companions, are we ready once again to get busy with the mission of Jesus? With the risen Lord come the new day and sunshine into the lives of his disciples. Jesus invites his tired followers to “come and have breakfast.” As Pope Francis said, the simple invitation from Jesus is a profound reminder that he meets us in our everyday lives, offering us sustenance and fellowship, and communicates his desire to restore and nourish us after our struggles.



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      Today’s Gospel story is about our risen Lord’s persistent search for the apostles, even when they had gone back to their fishing profession, trying, perhaps, to forget the disastrous events leading to the crucifixion of their Master. John presents this incident as the third of Jesus’ post-Resurrection appearances. The incident proves that Jesus’ post-Resurrection appearances were not mere hallucinations. In the first part of today’s Gospel, the risen Jesus appears to the apostles and gives them a symbol of their mission in a miraculous catch of fish, followed by a grilled-fish breakfast which Jesus had prepared for them. The second part is a dialogue between Jesus and Simon where Simon is asked three times whether he loves Jesus and answers that he does, as if in reparation for his triple denial of Jesus. As his primary mission, Peter is given the care of the vulnerable lambs and sheep, and he is told that fidelity to this mission will lead him to martyrdom.



      <span class="subhead">Life messages:</span> We need to open our eyes, ears, minds, and hearts wide to see, hear and experience the risen Lord coming into our lives in various forms, circumstances and events. These include: 1) The risen Lord blesses us with success and achievements: We often fail to acknowledge the presence of the risen Lord behind our unexpected successes, great achievements, promotions at work, miraculous healings, and success in relationships. But the risen Lord is right there at our parties, celebrations, and occasions of rejoicing. 2) The risen Lord is present in our pains and suffering: Acts 9:1-13 tells us how the risen Lord transformed the life of Saul by flattening him on the Damascus Road, and by making him temporarily blind, so that he would come to see that Jesus was the Christ and the Son of God and believe. The same Jesus often visits us in the form of accidents, illnesses, the loss of dear ones, pain, suffering, and problems in relationships. 3) The risen Lord visits us through our friends and well-wishers: He is present in those who visit us and encourage us in our sad and desperate moments. The risen Lord visits us in our dire needs in the form of unexpected help from the least expected persons. 4) The risen Lord is present in our Christian worship: Jesus is present on our altars during the Holy Mass to share his Divine life with us, in the words of the Holy Scripture, in the Sacraments, especially the Eucharist and the Sacrament of Reconciliation and wherever two or three are gathered in his name (Matthew 18:20).

    title: Reflection
    ref: null

    body: |
      The disciples of Jesus were disheartened and discouraged, and they go back to their trade of fishing. After a night of catching nothing, Jesus tells His disciples where to cast their nets, leading to a miraculous catch. This encounter reveals that when we rely on God rather than our own strength, He provides in ways we cannot imagine. The disciples’ humility and trust allow them to recognise Jesus, who feeds and strengthens them for the mission ahead. In Acts 4:1-12, Peter and John, once simple fishermen, speak boldly about Jesus to religious leaders. Filled with the Holy Spirit, they proclaim that salvation comes through Jesus alone. These passages remind us that God doesn’t require us to be powerful or perfect; He asks only for faith and willingness. As we trust in Him, He equips us to share His love and to become instruments of His work in the world.

    ref: "Saint Fulbert - Bishop, Confessor (c 960-1029)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Born in the Picardy region of France, Saint Fulbert studied at the Benedictine abbey at Reims where he had as his instructor the famous Gerbert of Aurillac, who in 999 was to become Pope Sylvester II. Fulbert was the founder of the cathedral school at Chartres, which before long became the most renowned institution of its kind in France, attracting as he did students even from Germany, Italy and England. In appreciation of his wisdom and learning, his pupils styled him the “Venerable Socrates”.



      For the last 22 years of his life he was Bishop of Chartres and as such laboured at the reform of the clergy and against the practice of assigning church benefices to laymen; he did not hesitate to even rebuke some of his fellow Bishops for their worldly-minded, military adventures. A personal friend of Saint Odilo of Cluny, of Blessed Richard of Saint Vannes, and of Saint Abbo of Fleury, he wholeheartedly endorsed their great monastic reforms.



      Saint Fulbert is also hailed as a poet. He composed a number of hymns to Our Lady, for whom he had a special devotion, and introduced the feast of her Nativity in his diocese. He died on 10 April 1029 and was buried in the monastery church of Saint Pere-de-Chartres.
