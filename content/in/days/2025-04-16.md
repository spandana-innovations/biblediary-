      Reflection: 1



      The Son of Man is going as it is written of him, but woe to that man who betrays him. Jesus knew who that man was, but that man feigned ignorance and pretended to be innocent of the betrayal. In our life too, we may experience betrayal from totally unexpected persons, from our close relations and intimate friends. There is no assurance that all of them will stand by you and with you always. Everyone of us, therefore, who belong to Christ should re-examine our commitment and loyalty to him. We should also learn not to retaliate to those who are not loyal to us. The words of Jesus show how sad he felt about the fate of his betrayer. We should also feel sorry for those who go against us in spite of whatever good we do; we should wish them well and pray for them.</span></span></span></span>



      *— Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP</span></span>*</span></span>



      Reflection: 2



      Today is traditionally known as “Spy Wednesday,” and was called by Christians as early as AD 250 the “the day of betrayal.” Today’s Gospel describes how Judas secretly planned to betray Jesus for thirty silver pieces (the price of a slave), and how he was seeking an opportunity to betray his master. We also learn how Jesus had his plans for celebrating his last Passover supper with his disciples carried out in a house prearranged by him. In Jesus’ time, the Passover supper was celebrated on the first day of the week of Unleavened Bread. During the meal, Jesus dramatically declared that one of the disciples was planning to betray Him and hinted that it was Judas.</span>



      Bible scholars suggest three reasons why Judas betrayed Jesus. The primary reason seems to have been his greed for money — John tells us that Judas used to steal from the common fund. Bitter hatred due to disillusionment might have been a second reason. As a zealot who hated the Romans, Judas might have expected that his master would overthrow the Romans using his Divine power. Instead, Jesus preached love and peace. A third reason may have been that Judas supposed that his betrayal would give Jesus an occasion to fight back and conquer the Romans. That may be the reason why he committed suicide when he saw Jesus arrested without any resistance from Him.</span>



      <span class="subhead">Life messages: </span></span>



      1) We, too, can betray Jesus and his ideals for money, for power and influence, or for selfish pleasure. Let us examine our conscience when we wrongly think that our God disappoints us by promoting chastity, poverty, justice, and righteousness in the Holy Bible, by not answering our prayers as and when we want, by allowing suffering and premature death in our families and communities, and by not punishing the wicked right here, right now. Let us be on our guard not to betray Jesus as Judas did.</span>



      2) Holy Week is the time to assess our life, examine our conscience, do penance for our infidelity, and make practical resolutions to reform our lives with God’s help.</span>



      3) We are little Judases—we fall to small temptations we can’t seem to overcome. These little things can have a negative impact on our spiritual life over time. </span>

      Today’s gospel passage invites us to reflect on the themes of betrayal, temptation, loyalty, and the sovereignty of God. Judas was a member of Jesus’ inner circle. His decision to betray Jesus is a stark reminder that betrayal and unfaithfulness can come from unexpected sources, even from those who are closest to us. We are challenged today to examine our loyalty and commitment to Christ and consider the potential for betrayal in each of us. We need to examine our own choices, seeking faithfulness and integrity in our relationship with Christ and with others. In the end, we are reminded of the profound grace and forgiveness that Christ offers, even in the face of betrayal.

    body: |
      The eldest of 9 children of Francoise [a miller] and Louise Soubirous, Sister Marie-Bernarde Soubirous, popularly known as Bernadette, was born at Nevers in Lourdes, at the foothills of the French Pyrenees.



      In 1858, Bernadette, together with her sister Marie and a friend named Jeanne, were out looking for firewood for the family hearth when, in a grotto of Massabielle along the river Gave, she beheld a vision of Our Lady which she described to her companions thus: “A Lady of unspeakable beauty, dressed in white, drawn in at the waist by a blue sash. She had a white veil on her head and falling down her back.... Her feet were bare and there was a rose on each of them. On her right arm, a rosary of white beads... whose chain was golden, all shining like the two roses on her feet.”



      Mary, who appeared to and conversed with Bernadette on 17 other occasions, said: “I promise to make you happy, if not in this world, at least in the next” (*18 February*); “Penance! Penance! Penance!” (*24 February*); “Go and tell the Priests that a chapel must be built here” (*27 February*); “I am the Immaculate Conception” (*25 March*).



      Bernadette endured much opposition from family, civil authorities and clergy alike, all of whom refused to believe her. The Bishop of Tarbes appointed a Commission of ecclesiastical and medical experts to inquire into the reality and nature of the apparitions and miracles that had occurred at Lourdes. Before the reports of the commission were even approved by the Bishop three years later, the fame of Lourdes had spread worldwide, its position as a place of pilgrimage firmly established!



      In 1866, Bernadette commenced her novitiate at the motherhouse of the Sisters of Nevers, and in 1878, the year she made her perpetual vows, she renewed before the representatives of the Bishops of Tarbes and Nevers the depositions she had made 20 years before, regarding the grotto apparitions. Bernadette died of Asthma and TB in 1879 with a smile on her face at her impending encounter with her “Lady in White” and was canonized in 1933.
