  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Luke 6: 6-11."
    audio: null
    body: |
      Today’s Gospel describes a miraculous healing performed by Jesus one Sabbath as a public violation of Sabbath laws, in order to prove that God’s intention for the Sabbath was for His people *to do good and to save life* rather than *to do evil or to destroy life*. The incident and the reaction: Ex 20:8 and Dt 5:12 instructed the Jews to *keep the Sabbath holy.*But the scribes and the Pharisees had amplified God’s law on the Sabbath, misinterpreting it and making it burdensome for the common people through man-made laws. Jesus wanted to demonstrate in public the original intention of God in declaring Sabbath holy. For Jesus, the Sabbath was a day of rest on which Israelites were meant to adore God, to learn and teach His laws, and to do good to/for others. Hence, Jesus took the liberty of healing a man with a withered hand in the local synagogue immediately after the worship service, thus infuriating the scribes and the Pharisees.



      <span class="subhead">Life messages:</span> 1) Our Catholic “Sabbath” observance of participating in the Eucharistic celebration on Sunday is meant to recharge our spiritual batteries for doing good to/for others and avoiding evil. 2) Our Sunday observance is further meant to be an offering of our lives to God on the altar, to praise God, to thank Him for His blessings, to ask God’s pardon and forgiveness for our sins, to present our needs before the Lord, and to participate in the Divine Life by receiving Holy Communion. 3) It is finally a day to spend with the members of the family and to help our neighbours in the activities of our parish and neighbourhood.

  - key: reflection
    title: Reflection
    ref: null
    audio: null
    body: |
      Jesus heals a man with a withered hand on the Sabbath, despite knowing the Pharisees are watching and waiting to accuse Him. Jesus asks them, “Is it lawful to do good on the Sabbath?” He challenges their rigid interpretation of the law, showing that compassion and mercy should be at the heart of religious observance. By healing the man openly, Jesus demonstrates that love and human need take precedence over strict legalism. We are challenged to examine our own attitudes: do we prioritise rules over people? Jesus calls us to let love guide our actions, especially when it means standing up against judgement or rigidity. In following His example, we are encouraged to act with courage and compassion, and recognise that true faith honours God by caring for others, even when it challenges established norms or expectations.

    audio: null
    body: |
      Born in Milan, then a French city, Frederic Antoine Ozanam was the second of the three surviving children. Early in life, he imbibed from his parents the humane touch of concern for the poor and the needy.



      In time Frederic’s humaneness was to find due expression when a friend named Jules Deveaux introduced him to Sr Rosalie Rendu, the Superior of a Convent of the Daughters of Charity, located in the poorest district of Paris. Working with this organization founded by St Vincent de Paul brought him in close contact with the derelicts of society, eventually inspiring him to found the first Conference of Charity in May 1833 for the purpose of visiting and helping the poor both materially and spiritually, placing his new, fledgeling apostolate under the protection and patronage St Vincent de Paul (SVP). Today the SVP has nearly 50,000 conferences spread across the five continents!



      The grounding he had received in the Catholic Faith stood him in good stead when he found himself having to defend the Church and the Faith in any debate; it even enabled him to come out the stronger through a phase of doubt and disbelief.



      In 1841 Frederic married Amelie, the highly intelligent and well educated daughter of Monsieur Soulacroix, Rector of Lyons. A Christian to the core, Frederic proved himself to be both a caring spouse and an ideal father, as also a spiritual father to his followers in and through the SVP.



      Frederick, whose last words when dying were “My God, My God, have mercy on me!” was beatified in 1997.
