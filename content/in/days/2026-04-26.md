      Reflection: 1



      Jesus is the good shepherd who has come hat all his sheep may have life in its fulness. In Palestine, various shepherds put their flocks together for the night under one caretaker. At dawn each shepherd would call his own sheep and lead them out. In Jesus, God Himself becomes the shepherd of His people. Unlike the robbers (s the Jewish leaders were), the true shepherd enters through the door. Jesus is the loving, caring and self-sacrificing good shepherd. He is the gate that leads to salvation; no one goes to the Father except through him (Jn 14:6). He sacrifices himself to protect his sheep. However, if it not enough that the shepherd is good; the sheep also need to be good. They must listen to the voice of their shepherd and follow him faithfully. Am I a good sheep of Jesus? Do I listen to his word, accept his teaching and faithfully follow his guidance? If I am a good sheep, he will lead me to abundant life.



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      Today is called Good Shepherd Sunday, and, appropriately, this day is also the *World Day of Prayer for Vocations.* Today, the Church calls us to reflect on the meaning of God’s call for each of us and to pray for generous respondents God’s personal call (vocation), to the priesthood, the diaconate, and the consecrated life, because the entire Christian community shares the responsibility for fostering vocations. Both the Old and New Testaments use the image of a Shepherd and His flock to describe the unique relationship of God with Israel and Christ with Christians. The first reading is taken from St. Peter’s first sermon, given on Pentecost. Here, he exhorts his listeners, Jewish people gathered for the Feast of Weeks – the “Sabbath” of the seven weeks that have elapsed since Passover — to know beyond any doubt that the One they have allowed to be crucified is the true Shepherd, whom God has made both Lord and Messiah. Peter then proclaims that the proper response to the Good News about Jesus is to repent and be baptized “in the name of Jesus Christ,” and thus to become members of the Good Shepherd’s flock. Through the Sacrament of Baptism, they will receive forgiveness for their sins. The Responsorial Psalm (Ps 23), introduces Yahweh as the Good Shepherd of Israel and describes all of the things the Lord does for us, His sheep, in providing for our needs. The second reading, taken from Peter’s First Letter to the Church, continues the “shepherd” imagery. Peter encourages the suffering Christians to follow in footsteps of their shepherd (“the suffering servant”), and to remember that they have been claimed by him. Peter also explains how Jesus, the innocent sufferer, was a model of patience and trust in God, and he reminds us that it is Jesus’ suffering which has enabled us to become more fully children of God. In today’s Gospel, two brief parables about sheep reveal Jesus as our unique means to salvation. He is the selfless, caring “shepherd” who provides protection and life itself, and he is the “sheep gate,” the one gateway to eternal life.



      <span class="subhead">Life Messages:</span> 1) We need to be good shepherds and good leaders: Everyone who is entrusted with the care of others is a shepherd. Hence, pastors, parents, teachers, doctors, nurses, government officials, and caregivers, among others, are all shepherds. We become good shepherds by loving those entrusted to us, praying for them, spending our time, talents, and blessings for their welfare, and guarding them from physical and spiritual dangers.



      2) We need to be good sheep in the fold of Jesus, the Good Shepherd: Our local parish is our sheepfold, and our pastors are our shepherds. Jesus is the High Priest, the Bishops are the successors of the Apostles, the pastors are their helpers and the parishioners are the sheep. Hence, as the good sheep of the parish, parishioners are expected to a) Hear and follow the voice of our shepherds through their homilies, Bible classes, counseling, and advice. b) Receive the spiritual food given by our pastors by regular participation in the Holy Mass, by frequenting the Sacraments and by participating in prayer services, renewal programs and missions as far as we are able to do so.

      Today, the Gospel reminds us that Jesus, the Good Shepherd, gives us life – life in abundance. In John 10:1-10, Jesus speaks of Himself as the ‘gate’ through which we enter into the fullness of life. He is the Shepherd who calls His sheep by name and leads them to safety and green pastures. Through His death and resurrection, Jesus opens the way to eternal life, offering us peace, joy, and security. In the first reading, Apostle Peter, filled with the Holy Spirit, proclaims the message of Christ, and many are drawn to this life-giving truth. And the same Peter reminds us in the Second reading: “By his wounds we have been healed. For you were straying like sheep, but have now returned to the Shepherd and overseer of your souls.” As followers of Christ, we are called to embrace this abundant life He offers, to live in His care, and to share this life with others, leading them back to the Shepherd.

    body: |
      It is now generally conceded that the name Anacletus (or Anencletus), which is given in all the ancient lists of Popes from the second to the fourth century was probably abbreviated to Cletus. It was later erroneously believed that Cletus and Anacletus were two distinct figures, and they were therefore commemorated on 26 April and 13 July respectively.



      Anacletus succeeded Saint Linus to the papal throne about the year 76 and reigned for twelve years. The 6th century *Liber Pontificalis* states that he had a small chapel or “Memoria” erected over the resting place of the Prince of the Apostles atop Vatican Hill and another one over the tomb of Saint Paul on the property of the matron Lucina on the Ostian Way. Aside from this, little is known of the reign of this second successor of Saint Peter.



      Anacletus was believed to have been martyred under the cruel Emperor Domitian, the “Nero-come-to-life-again”.
