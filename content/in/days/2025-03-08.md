      Reflection: 1



      What is our attitude towards people who are generally considered sinful? Do we look at them with contempt, considering ourselves righteous and so superior to them? Jesus teaches that such an attitude is entirely wrong. When Jesus saw Levi the tax collector at work, he called him and Levi followed him immediately. Jesus showed love and compassion for the man, and he responded with great joy and enthusiasm. He was one in need of healing, and Jesus the physician offered him the help he needed without even being requested. If the Lord had not done so, Levi would have remained what he was, and we would not have St Mathew, Apostle and Evangelist.



      It is the compassion of Jesus that brought about such a radical change in him. Once he was collecting tax; now he is going on spreading the good news of salvation through his Gospel, and millions, including us, are his beneficiaries.



      — Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP



      Reflection: 2



      Today’s Gospel episode of Matthew’s call as Jesus’ Apostle reminds us of God’s love and mercy for sinners and challenges us to practice this same love and mercy in our relations with others.



      The call and the response: Jesus went to the tax collector’s post to invite Matthew to become his disciple. Since tax collectors worked for a foreign power and extorted more tax money from the people than the area owed, and thus made themselves rich by extortion, they were hated and despised as traitors by the Jewish people, and considered public sinners by the Pharisees. But Jesus could see in Matthew a person who needed Divine love and grace. While everyone hated Matthew, Jesus was ready to offer him undeserved love, mercy and forgiveness. Hence, Matthew abandoned his lucrative job, because for him, Christ’s call to follow him was a promise of salvation, fellowship, guidance and protection.



      Scandalous partying with sinners: It was altogether natural for Matthew to rejoice in his new calling by celebrating with his friends. Jesus’ dining with outcasts in the house of a traitor scandalized the Pharisees for whom ritual purity and table fellowship were important religious practices. Hence, they asked the disciples, “Why does your master eat with tax collectors and sinners?” Jesus Himself answered their question, stressing his ministry as healer: “Those who are well do not need a physician; the sick do.” Then, in Matthew’s account, quoting Hosea, Jesus challenged the Pharisees, “Go and learn the meaning of the words, ‘I desire mercy, not sacrifice’ (Hos 6:6)” Finally, Jesus clarified his position, “I did not come to call the righteous, but sinners.”



      Life messages:



      1) Jesus calls you and me for a purpose: Jesus has called us, through our Baptism, has forgiven our sins and has welcomed us as members of the Kingdom. He calls us through the Word and through his Church to be his disciples and to turn away from all the things that distract us and draw us away from God.



      2) Just as Jesus did, and Matthew did, we, too, are expected to preach Christ through our lives, by reaching out to the unwanted and the marginalized in society with Christ’s love, mercy and compassion.

      Jesus’ approach to sinners is presented in today’s Gospel passage. When the Pharisees and the Scribes looked at tax collectors as public sinners, as those to be treated with contempt and disdain, Jesus looked at them with compassion and considered them as “the sick who need a doctor”. Matthew was a tax collector. The compassionate approach of Jesus made him an Apostle and an Evangelist. We can save many sinners through a compassionate approach. As the saying goes, “You can catch more flies with honey than with vinegar”. It is often our self-righteous attitude that scorns others. Such a self-righteous attitude is self-destructive. A true Christian can never be self-righteous in his approach towards others.

    audio: null
    body: |
      St John, though born in central Portugal, grew to manhood in the service of the chief shepherd of a nobleman of Oropeza, Spain, and was noted for his faithfulness and piety. After spending some time as a soldier in France and at the siege of Vienna, he worked for a while as a shepherd at Seville and even offered himself for the ransom of Christian captives from the Moors.



      For a while he peddled religious books and pictures in Gibraltar, but when he was about 45, the Infant Jesus appeared to him one day in a vision and said: “John of God, Granada will be your cross!” In that city, to which he now repaired, he was so stirred by a sermon of St John of Avila that he distributed his possessions and gave himself up to such public lamentations over his wasted youth that he was considered demented. John, however, counselled and absolved him, whereupon he devoted himself entirely to alleviating the lot of the sick and the poor.



      Renting a small house in Granada, he would carry to it the infirm who could not reach it by themselves. Immediately on their arrival, he would wash their feet and offer them a Crucifix to kiss, after which he would put them to bed and urge them to have a priest hear their confession.



      The Archbishop fully supported his work, and his sanctity was attested by numerous miracles. He died on 8 March 1550 while praying on his knees before the altar. He was canonized in 1690.



      The Order which he founded, viz., the “Brothers Hospitallers” — in Italy they are called “Fatebene Fratelli” (*Do-Good-Brethren*) — now maintain over 100 hospitals of their own in all parts of the world. St John is the Patron of hospitals, of the sick, of nurses, printers and book-sellers.
