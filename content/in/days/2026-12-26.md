  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Mathew 10: 17-22"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Matthew’s Judeo-Christian community had experienced much persecution. Jesus’ words “*You will be dragged before governors andkings*” and “*brother will deliver up brother to death, and the father his child, and children will rise against parents and have them put to death,”* were beginning to come true. James the apostle had been martyred by King Herod and the lives of other apostles were also in danger. Hence, in repeating Jesus’ warning to his apostles, Matthew encouraged his Judeo-Christians to rely on Jesus’ promise of the protective power of a providing God for themselves as they persevered in the Faith and its practice.



      Persecutions past and present: Jesus gave his frank warning to the apostles that their lives and their future followers’ lives were not going to be beds of thorn less roses. Jesus foretold three sources of persecution awaiting Christians: The Roman government, the local Jewish synagogues and their Jewish or pagan family members. The main accusations against the first-century Christians were that they were cannibals and incendiaries practicing immorality during worship services, that they caused a split in their families, and that they considered slaves as equals (in an empire with 60 million slaves!).



      <span class="subhead">Life messages:</span>



      Although we have freedom to practice the religion of our choice, the extreme interpretation of the *“separation of Church and state”* policy eliminates the religious instruction and moral training of children in public schools, allowing youngsters not given this training at home to grow up as pagans. The secular media, run by atheists and agnostics, ridicule all religious beliefs and practices, inflicting a type of “white martyrdom” on believers. Hence, the duty of parents to see that their children receive religious and moral instruction from their parishes and families becomes more important daily.

  - key: reflection

    ref: null

    body: |
      We have just celebrated Christmas, the festival of love, which brings the Christian families and friends together like no other feast. Today we celebrate St Stephen, who reminds us that Christian love cannot be limited only to family and friends but should be extended to enemies, as Stephen prayed for those who were about to stone him to death. His love is not afraid of death. His journey of serving Jesus began when he was appointed deacon to care for the neglected Greek widows. What inspired and motivated him to risk his life was his ‘gaze’ into heaven, where he saw the ‘glory of God and Jesus standing at the right hand of God’ (Acts 10:55). As Christians, we know that our home is in heaven, and our faith in the future fulfilment of our lives is our motivating factor to love our enemies and face the persecutions in this life.
  - key: saint
    title: Saint of the Day
    saintName: "Saint Stephen, Proto-Martyr - (-c. 36)"
    ref: "Saint Stephen, Proto-Martyr - (-c. 36)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Within a year of Jesus’ death and resurrection the young Church at Jerusalem already numbered over 5000, and the Apostles felt the need to delegate some of their duties—like preaching, baptizing, and the fair distribution of alms—to ‘deacons’ (helpers). The first of the seven to be ordained for this purpose was Stephen, “a man full of faith and the Holy Spirit”.



      Stephen, “full of grace and fortitude”, performed “great wonders and signs” in the name of Jesus. A pupil of the great Gamaliel, he preached fearlessly in the “synagogue of the Freedmen” (descendants of Jews who had been carried off to Rome as children in 63 B.C. and there freed), and in the Synagogues of the Hellenist Jews from Cyrene, Alexandria, Cilicia (Beirut) and Asia, who preferred to worship by themselves. He insisted that Jesus came to save all and not merely the “Chosen People”, and that the Church was separate and distinct from the synagogue, reproving the Pharisees for their obstinacy that prevented them from recognizing the long awaited Messiah and, instead, crucifying him.



      Accused of blasphemy before the Sanhedrin, his fiery and eloquent defence of the Church, his enumeration of God’s past mercies toward Israel and the latter’s ingratitude, and finally his declaration that he saw “the heavens opened and the Son of Man standing at the right hand of God”, so raised their fury that they dragged him outside the city and stoned him to death. All through the fracas, Stephen’s thoughts, as a true follower of Jesus, were for his persecutors—“Lord, lay not this sin to their charge.” He earned himself the distinction of becoming the first martyr of the fledgling Church, exclaiming, “Lord Jesus, receive my spirit!” thus living up to the meaning of his name which, in Greek, signifies “crown”!



      <span class="subhead">Reflection:</span> *“Love was Stephen’s weapon by which he gained every battle....” (Saint Fulgentius of Ruspe).*
