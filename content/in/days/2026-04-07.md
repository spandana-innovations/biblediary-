      Reflection: 1



      Today’s Gospel tells us about Mary Magdalene’s visit to the tomb and her weeping because she did not find the body of Jesus. She was once under the clutches of demons and Jesus liberated her. Thereafter Jesus was the only Master of her life. As a true devotee she is now looking for the body of Jesus. She stayed at the tomb, and Jesus rewards her perseverance and reveals himself to her first. Jesus invites her to ascend to amore divine communion with the Father. She is asked not to cling to the risen Lord but o rise with him to the new, heavenly life, to regain a place in God’s family. Mary had the courage to go in search of Jesus even in the midst of her sadness, and she was made an apostle of the good news of Jesus. If we love Jesus, we shall look for him, meet him and proclaim him. </span></span></span></span>



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      Today’s Gospel presents the great recognition scene in the New Testament in which Mary Magdalene, at the tomb early in the morning, was not able to recognize the Risen Jesus until Jesus called her by name. Gradual recognition, or misunderstanding, as a stage on the path to belief and understanding, frequently occurs in the narratives of John’s Gospel. [See, for example, the conversations Jesus had with Nicodemus (ch. 3),

      and the Samaritan woman (ch. 4).] In today’s passage, we find it once again: Mary thought at first that Jesus was the gardener.</span></span></span></span></span>



      Mary Magdalene failed to recognize Jesus because of her false assumption that his body had been stolen. Her attention was concentrated on the empty tomb. Her tears of intense grief could also have blurred her vision. Once Mary had recognized Jesus, he gave her a message to be conveyed to his Apostles about His plan to leave them and ascend to his Father. She was the first to see the Risen Christ and the first to tell the apostles about the Lord’s resurrection. Mary’s message to Jesus’ disciples, “*I have seen the Lord,” (Jn 20:18),* became the basis and essence of later preaching and Christians’ witness-bearing. St Thomas Aquinas said that one old lady (*una vetera*), might have more Faith than a host of learned theologians.</span></span></span></span></span>



      <span class="subhead">Life messages:</span></span></span> 1) Just like Mary Magdalene, we too may fail to recognize the presence of the risen Lord in our neighbor because of our preoccupations, spiritual blindness, or evil habits. But we can be open to experience the presence of the Risen Lord in our lives through our prayer, our Sacramental life, and our meditative reading of the Bible. These all enable us to bear witness to the Risen Lord in our daily lives.</span></span></span></span></span>



      2) It is our powerful conviction of the Real Presence of the Risen Lord, both in the Eucharist and in our lives, which gives us the strength to fight temptations and to serve our brothers and sisters in corporal and spiritual works of mercy. </span></span>

      Today we find a profound moment of restoration and reunion. In Mary Magdalene’s encounter with the risen Jesus, she experiences the joy of regaining what was lost – our place in God’s family, lost in the Garden of Eden. When Jesus says, “I am going back to my Father and your Father, to my God and your God” (v.18), He reaffirms that we are restored to this divine relationship. Each time we approach the sacrament of reconciliation, we hear this same invitation to return home, just like the father’s embrace of the prodigal son. Pope John Paul II reminded us in Dives in Misericordia: “God is always ready to welcome, to forgive, to restore.” This encounter invites us to trust in God’s constant, loving invitation to re-join His family, no matter how far we’ve strayed. Through reconciliation, we return to the family where we truly belong.</span></span></span>

    saintName: "Saint John Baptist De La Salle - Confessor (1651-1719)"
    ref: "Saint John Baptist De La Salle - Confessor (1651-1719)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      John Baptist de la Salle was made a canon of the cathedral of Reims at age 16 and ordained priest at 27. A model of piety and humility, he was gradually drawn through his love for the poor, into what was to become his life-work.</span></span></span></span>



      At that time Jansenism—whose twin focus was the denial of man’s ability to resist temptation and the rejection of the doctrine that Christ died for all men—had taken firm root in France; the constant foreign and internal wars had brought people famine, ruin and untold suffering; education was neglected, ignorance and vice abounding. Faced with such desperate conditions, John de la Salle rejoiced at being asked to help found two free schools for the poor. He took personal and active interest in the teachers and their methods, encouraged them, housed and fed them under his own roof, thus guaranteeing them against excessive expenditure which might have consumed their meagre earnings.</span></span></span></span>



      To John the education of the poor was an urgent need. The Institute of the Brothers of Christian Schools, founded about 1680 on simplicity, mortification and absolute reliance upon Divine Providence, finally blossomed into the flourishing and worldwide religious congregation it is today. </span></span>There were to be no priests in his institute but only brothers, their lives fully consecrated as “educators of the mind” and “apostles of the soul”. Above all he insisted on the teaching of the Faith in Catholic Schools and the use of the vernacular instead of Latin in school texts, so that through the pupils’ reading ability the parents at home might also receive an education in Christian doctrine and morals. </span></span></span></span>



      His writings embrace the fundamentals of scientific pedagogy, Christian behaviour and manners, Christian doctrine, treatises on the religious life, method of mental prayer and numerous meditations — all simple, direct and vigorous in approach.</span></span></span></span>



      John Baptist de la Salle died in 1719, was canonized in 1900, and declared patron of all teachers in 1950.</span></span></span></span>
