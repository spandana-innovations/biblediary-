      Reflection: 1



      The missionaries of Jesus have to work in a hostile world. Jesus predicts that they will face persecution and martyrdom for his sake. People will hate them because of Jesus. Yet, they should go ahead without fear or worry; the Holy Spirit will speak through them. In all the difficulties and struggles they undergo, God will guide them and protect them. They should trust in the Lord who sends them and not be anxious about their lack of resources, power or protection. The Son of Man will come even before they complete all their work in different places. Everyone who endures to the end will be saved. The mission Jesus has given and the salvation he has promised us require endurance.



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      Matthew’s Judeo-Christian community had experienced much persecution. Jesus’ prophetic words, “*You will be dragged before governors andkings*” and “*brother will deliver up brother to death, and the father his child, and children will rise against their parents and have them put to death,”* were beginning to be fulfilled. The Apostle James had been martyred by King Herod, and the lives of other apostles were also in danger. Hence, by repeating Jesus’ warning to the apostles, Matthew encouraged his Judeo-Christians to rely on Jesus’ promise of the protective power of a providing God as they persevered in Faith and its practice.



      Persecutions, past and present: Jesus gave his frank warning to the apostles that their lives and their future followers’ lives were not going to be beds of roses. Jesus foretold three types of persecution awaiting Christians: by the Roman government, by the local Jewish synagogues, and by their Jewish or pagan family members. The main accusations against the first-century Christians were that they were cannibals, atheists, and incendiaries, that they practiced immorality during worship services, that they caused their families to split, and that they considered slaves as equals –in an empire with 60 million slaves!



      Life message: Parents have a duty to see that their children receive religious and moral instruction from their parishes and families.

    body: |
      As Jesus sends out his disciples on a mission, he points to the inevitability of the opposition they will have to face. The disciples of Jesus are sent out like “sheep among wolves”, clearly implying that while carrying out their missionary activities they will be encountering difficult situations with narrow-minded and hard-hearted people. At times, the disciples might think that they are weak, lacking as they do, the resources needed to counter such situations and people. Secondly, Jesus cautions the disciples that their activities will be strictly monitored by the powerful leaders of the place, both secular and religious, and that they would need to stand firmly against the opposition unleashed by them. Thirdly, the disciples will be displaced by those who dislike them. In all these situations, Jesus assures the disciples that God’s protective hands will always guide them.

    ref: "Saint Benedict of Nursia, Abbot, Confessor (c.480 - c.547)."
    audio: null
    body: |
      Born of noble parents in Nursia in Italy, Benedict spent his youth in Rome, but the dissolute lives of his fellow students and the state of the country, then under the Arian Theodoric the Goth, prompted him at age 16 to seek salvation as a lone hermit in a hidden cave. In time, maturing rapidly in mind and character, his sanctity, miracles, and his gift of directing souls attracted numerous earnest disciples whom he organized into 12 monasteries, each comprising 12 monks, with its own superior.



      Accepting a donation of land on Monte Cassino, he established the great abbey which became the motherhouse of the Benedictine Order for the reclamation and regeneration of Christian Europe, an accomplishment that was to earn him the title “Father of Europe” from Pope Pius XII, and “Patron Saint of all Europe” in 1964 from Pope Paul VI.



      His monks vested manual labour with a new dignity, adding to it prayer, the “Opus Dei”, the humble acknowledgement of God’s presence throughout the day by brief public acts of faith, praise and adoration. Their vows comprised poverty, chastity, obedience and the stable attachment to one monastery for life. In contrast to the later Franciscan idea of poverty, the Benedictine monastery was to be not only self-supporting, but in a position to succour the poor, the sick, the afflicted and give shelter to the stranger.



      By their teaching, studying and copying of manuscripts, the Benedictines preserved the Greek and the Roman classics through the Dark Ages. Though not a priest himself, Benedict’s Order has given the Church no less than 24 popes, 4,600 bishops and over 5,000 saints!
