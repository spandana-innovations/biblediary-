      Reflection: 1



      The text is part of what Jesus said to his disciples after he washed their feet at the Last Supper. The foot-washing was a deeply significant gesture of the Messiah as servant. The disciples were not able to understand or accept this at that time because they had no intention to be servants themselves. But Jesus reminded them that no servant is greater than his master and no messenger greater than the one who sent him. He also stated that one who ate his bread had turned against him. Jesus is going to send his disciples as his principal messengers. Whoever receives the one he sends actually receives him, and whoever received him actually received the Father who sent him. It is a blessing to have an opportunity to offer humble service to anyone, because it is an opportunity to imitate the Master. In imitating him we should be constantly aware that Jesus offered not merely philanthropic service, but service unto salvation.</span></span></span></span>



      *— Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP</span></span>*</span></span>



      Reflection: 2



      Today’s Gospel is the second part of the explanation Jesus gave to his disciples after washing their feet before the Last Supper. He promised his disciples that that whoever listened to them would be listening to him as well, provided his preaching disciples became the humble servants of others.



      Gospel lessons: In the first part of today’s Gospel, Jesus emphasizes the fact that the hallmark of his disciples must be their readiness and generosity in offering humble and sacrificial service to others, because that was the model Jesus had given them by his life and especially by washing their feet. It is by serving others that we become great before God. In the second part of today’s Gospel, Jesus shows his apostles how to treat people who are unfaithful and disloyal. Jesus hints at the betrayal of Judas by quoting Psalm 4:9: “*He who ate my bread has lifted his heel against me.” *Instead of distancing himself from Judas, Jesus offers him reconciliation, showing him more affection by washing his feet and by giving him a morsel of bread dipped in sauce with his own hand. In the third part, Jesus gives the basis for apostolic succession, stating that one who receives his apostles and messengers receives him, thereby receiving God the Father who sent Jesus.



      <span class="subhead">Life messages: </span>



      1) Let us prove that we are true disciples of Jesus by rendering others humble and loving service today.



      2) Let us learn to be reconciled with those who offend us by unconditionally pardoning them, by wishing them the very best and by keeping them in our prayers.



      *(Fr Tony: https://frtonyshomilies.com/)*

    body: |
      The Gospel is in the context of the symbolic gesture of the washing of the feet which calls us to accept Jesus as a Messiah Servant, who gives Himself for others, and to reject the idea of a glorious king. The disciples could not easily accept this identity of Jesus, as they did not want to be servants. Being at service is the essence of Christianity synthesized in the virtue of charity. Being a servant is not easy, as it entails humility. If we have the love for souls as our motivation to serve, every opportunity to live as servants becomes a blessing to live like the Master who came to serve and not be served and to give his life as a ransom for many.

      St. Isidore was born at Madrid, Spain, in the latter half of the 12th century. For the greater part of his life he was employed as a laborer on a farm outside the city. Many marvelous happenings accompanied his lifelong work in the fields and continued long after his holy death. He was favored with celestial visions and, it is said, the angels sometimes helped him in his work in the fields. St. Isidore was canonized in 1622.</span>
