      Reflection: 1



      To the people who were admiring the beauty and riches of the Temple Jesus said that days would come when this glorious temple would be destroyed. Years later, in 70 AD, the Romans destroyed both Jerusalem and the Temple. Jesus warned them not to be deceived by false prophets, and not to be frightened by news of wars and troubled times; he assured them that the end would not come soon. The end-times will certainly come, but we cannot calculate as to when that will happen. Rather than worry about such things, we should grasp the present and live it faithfully, trusting in God amidst troubles and challenges. </span></span></span></span>



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      Today’s Gospel begins with Jesus’ reaction to the comments the disciples had been making about the splendour of the Temple in Jerusalem. The forty-foot-tall pillars supporting the beams of the front porch were made of solid marble. Most of the decorations and the large vine on the front porch with six-foot long grape clusters were made of solid gold plates, while the dome was gold-plated. But Jesus prophesied this Temple’s total destruction. In AD 70, the Roman army invaded the city, plundered everything valuable, set fire to the Temple, pulled down the City’s walls, killed one million Jews, and took 97,000 healthy Jews as captives. Jesus also gave the disciples warnings about false military messiahs and their deceptive doctrines about overthrowing the Romans. Then Jesus listed some signs of the end of the world, like wars between nations, earthquakes, famines, plagues, and unnatural movements of the heavenly bodies.</span>



      <span class="subhead">Life message:</span> We need to learn from the signs of the times, like crises in morality, a culture of death, an increase in violence and terrorism, the “normalization” of sexual deviations, the breaking down of families, and the moral degradation of society. We need to prepare ourselves for the end times by living ideal Christian lives, helping others, sharing our blessings with others, getting and staying reconciled with God and our neighbors, and trusting in the living presence of Jesus in the Church.</span>

      In today’s Gospel, Jesus reminds us to be cautious about predictions of the future, urging us to focus on living faithfully in the present. He warns against deception by false prophets and emphasizes the unpredictability of the end times. Instead of fixating on the unknown, we are called to remain steadfast in our faith, practise repentance, and find hope in God’s presence, even in turbulent times. This passage encourages us to trust God’s plan, endure life’s challenges, and seek peace and justice while holding on to Christian hope. It is a powerful reminder that, no matter how difficult things may seem, there is always a glimmer of hope, a ray of light, and a source of strength to guide us through the darkest times. How can I nurture my hope during uncertain times?

    ref: "Saint Catherine of Alexandria, Virgin, Martyr (4th cent)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Numbered among early women martyrs, Catherine holds a special place among the prophetic saints in the Church because of her role as philosophical defender of the Faith. In Church art, she is depicted wearing a crown, for she was of royal birth; holding a book, for she was extremely learned; and steering a spiked wheel, indicating her martyrdom.



      Converted by a vision of Our Lady, she boldly protested the persecution of Christians under the Roman emperor Maxentius who, unable to refute the arguments she raised with him directly, had a battery of some fifty eminent philosophers assembled to counter her views. However, their admittance of defeat before her prowess rung the death-knell both for them and for her—they were burned alive while she was put in prison where she succeeded in converting not only the gaolers but also the emperor’s wife, Faustina! Any wonder then why she is considered patroness of philosophers, too! Incidentally, the spiked wheel to which she was fastened mysteriously broke apart, leaving Catherine unscathed, thus emerging as patroness of wheelwrights. Finally beheaded, angels are said to have taken her remains atop Mount Sinai, where they were discovered (c 800).
