      The Greek word Epiphany (*&epsilon;&pi;&iota;&phi;ά&nu;&epsilon;&iota;&alpha;*), means *appearance* or *manifestation*. First, the angels revealed Jesus to the shepherds. In the Western Church, the Feast of the Epiphany celebrates Jesus’ first manifestation to the Gentiles, represented by the Magi, while in the Eastern Church, the Feast commemorates the baptism of Christ, at which the Father and the Holy Spirit gave combined testimony to Jesus’ identity as Son of God. Later, in the synagogue at Nazareth, Jesus revealed Himself in words as the promised Messiah, and at Cana Jesus revealed His Divinity by transforming water into wine. These multiple revelations are all suggested by the Feast of the Epiphany.



      Scripture lessons summarized:Today’s Gospel teaches us how Christ enriches those who bring Him their hearts and offer their lives to Him. The adoration of the Magi fulfills the oracle of Isaiah (first reading), prophesying that the nations of the world would travel to the Holy City following a brilliant light, bringing gold and incense to contribute to the worship of God. Today’s Responsorial Psalm (Ps 72) verses 10-11 picture kings from foreign lands bringing gifts to pay homage to a just king in Israel. Paul’s letter to the Church of Ephesus (today’s second reading), expresses God’s secret plan in clear terms: *“the Gentiles are…co-heirs … co-partners in the promise, in Christ Jesus through the Gospel.”* Today’s Gospel reminds us that if God brought the Magi – foreigners and pagans – to recognize and give Jesus proper respect as the King of Jews, we should know that there is nothing in our sinful lives that would keep God from bringing us to Jesus. There were three groups of people who reacted to the Epiphany of Christ’s birth. The first group, headed by King Herod, tried to eliminate Jesus, the second group, priests and scribes, ignored Jesus, and the third group, represented by the shepherds and the Magi, came to adore Jesus.



      <span class="subhead">Life Messages: </span>



      1) Let us make sure that we belong to the third group: a) by actively worshiping Jesus at Mass with the gold of our love, the myrrh of our humility and the frankincense of our adoration; b) by giving a new direction to our lives. Just as the Magi chose another route to return to their homes, let us choose a better way of life, abstaining from proud, unjust, and impure thoughts, words, and actions, evil habits, and selfish behaviour; c) by becoming stars leading others to Jesus, as the star led the Magi to Jesus. Let us remove the darkness of the evil around us by radiating the light of Jesus’ love through selfless service, unconditional forgiveness, and compassionate care.



      2) Like the Magi, let us offer Jesus our gifts on this feast of Epiphany and every day: (a) the Gift of our life by offering it on the altar during the Holy Mass and every morning as soon as we get up, asking for the strengthening anointing of the Holy Spirit to do good and avoid evil during the course of the day. b) the Gift of responsive relationship with God by talking to Him in personal and family prayers and listening to Him through reading the Holy Bible every day. c) the Gift of friendship with God by recognizing Jesus’ presence in, and offering loving, humble service to, everyone we encounter, and by getting reconciled to God every night, asking His pardon and forgiveness for our sins and failures during the day.

      Today we celebrate the Epiphany, the revelation of Christ to the world. The wise men from the East came to Jerusalem, looking for the King of the Jews, expecting to find him perhaps in the palace of Herod. But to their surprise, the star led them to a stable. Although they ended up in a place that they did not expect, this did not stop them from making the most of it. We will be disappointed if we always expect God to be found only in good times or our beautiful chapels; rather we should be prepared to find God even in the marketplace, in events expected and unexpected, or in the darkest caves of despair. After having encountered the newborn King, ‘they departed to their own country by another way’ (v 12). It was the divine encounter that changed their lives forever, and they began to travel on a ‘new’ road of adventure. If we truly seek God and give ourselves fully to Him, we will also lead a new life.

      (1) Solemnity of the Epiphany of Our Lord and Feast of the Holy Magi



      This Feast is a threefold commemoration of Our Lord’s ‘manifestation’ amongst us: first to the Gentiles as represented by the Magi; next at his baptism in the Jordan when his divinity is manifested; and then at the wedding feast at Cana in Galilee when he performed his first miracle, changing water into wine.



      In the Eastern tradition where the feast of the Epiphany originated in the third century, the emphasis is on the Baptism of the Lord, whereas in the Latin rite it is on the coming of the Magi, commonly known as the Three Wise Men or ‘Three Kings’ or perhaps members of the priestly clan of the Medes, the star they sighted miraculously guiding them on their long journey to Jerusalem and Bethlehem. While rendering the Infant King in the manger their homage, they offer him gold in keeping with his royal status, myrrh to venerate him as man in preparation for his burial, and incense with which they adore him as God.



      Saint Paul avers that Isaiah’s prophecy concerning Jerusalem refers to Christ—the true Light of the world—and his Church, to which those seeking the true light turn, stepping out of the darkness of error into the light of the Truth.



      <span class="subhead">Reflection:</span> *“To know” Jesus Christ means to study him, and to “acknowledge” him means to believe. What an abyss stretches between “knowledge” and “faith”!”*(Blessed James Alberione)



      (2) Saint Simon Stylite - Confessor (388-459)



      We have here the first and probably the best known case of a Stylite, i.e., a “pillar-hermit”. An oriental ascetic who passed long years perched atop a high column, Saint Simon was born of well-to-do peasants near the northern border of Syria. At 15 he entered a monastery, where his self-imposed austerity was found to be so extreme that his brethren considered him unsuited to community life, and he thenceforth lived as a hermit.



      All through the Season of Lent he would remain in a standing position and would partake of neither food nor drink. His advice and spiritual counsel were sought by innumerable people, even by the Emperor Theodosius and the Empress Endocia. Many were the miraculous cures wrought by his prayers. But in order to escape the importunities of relic-seeking admirers, he had a column built on a hill near the boundary between Syria and Cilicia, at first only some ten feet high, but eventually raised to sixty feet; and on its unsheltered top he spent the remaining 36 years of his life. The space was only about three feet in diameter, so that he could stand, kneel or sit, but never lie down. From here he would preach to the faithful on feast days and give counsel to all who ascended to him by means of a rope ladder. Some of his letters are still extant.



      <span class="subhead">Reflection:</span> *“This is the business of our life: by effort and toil, by prayer and supplication, to advance in the grace of God, till we come to that height of perfection in which with clean hearts we may behold God”*(Saint Augustine of Hippo).
