    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Matthew 5:1–12"
    audio: null
    body: |
      The“Beatitudes” form the introductory section in Mathew’s account of the *Sermon on the Mount.* They are the heart of the Sermon on the Mount, as the Sermon on the Mount is the heart of the whole Gospel, or the “Compendium of Christian Doctrine*.”* This sermon contains the most essential aspects of Christian behaviour that we need to live out, if we are to reach Christian perfection. In essence, the Beatitudes both fulfil and complete the Ten Commandments.</span></span></span>



      Bombshells: In both Matthew and Luke the Beatitudes have been called a “series of bombshells” or blinding “flashes of lightning followed by deafening thunder of surprise and shock,” because Jesus reverses our “natural” assumption that happiness lies in riches, power, influence, and pleasure. We believe in personal pride: Jesus blesses poverty of spirit. We seek pleasure: Jesus blesses those who mourn. We see the prosperity of aggressive people: Jesus blesses the meek. We love good food and drink: Jesus blesses those who hunger and thirst for righteousness. Thus, Jesus instructs his disciples in the paradoxical blessedness of poverty, hunger, sorrow, and persecution. In poverty, we recognize God’s reign; in hunger, His Providence; in sorrow, true happiness; and in persecution, true joy. In other words, the*blessed, the “happy,”* on Jesus’ list are the poor in spirit, the compassionate, the meek, the merciful, the clean of heart, the peacemakers, and those who are willing even to be insulted and persecuted for their following of Jesus in action.</span></span></span>



      <span class="subhead">Life messages: </span>1) We need to respond to the challenge of the Beatitudes in daily life. The Beatitudes propose to us a way of life, inviting us to identify with the poor, those who mourn, who are meek, and who hunger and thirst after justice. 2) They challenge us to become compassionate people, to become men and women who are pure in heart, and to become peacemakers in our dealings with one another, in our families and in the society at large, even when this approach to things exposes us to ridicule and persecution. 3) Let us remember that each time we reach out to help the needy, the sick and the oppressed, we share with them a foretaste of the promises of the Beatitudes here and now. </span></span></span>

    body: |
      The Beatitudes mark the beginning of the Sermon on the Mount in the Gospel according to Matthew. They express Jesus’ radical values which were and are in contrast to worldly values. By aligning ourselves with the Beatitudes, we strive to become more Christ-like, as they embody the values of the Kingdom. Living a Christ-like life poses a challenge for Christians, and the Beatitudes present this challenge. According to Pope Francis, the Beatitudes serve as the “identity card” of Christians, outlining the likeness of Jesus and His way of living. We assess our inner life based on the Beatitudes and our outer life of charity based on the parable of the Final Judgement (Mt 25). How successful am I in living the Beatitudes?</span></span></span>

    audio: null
    body: |
      Mariam Thresia hailed from a wealthy family in Puthenchira, Kerala. But impoverishment set in with her grandfather having to sell his property for marrying off his seven daughters. Yet, hers was a life that was lived in a manner that challenges modern man to live heroically the values of the Gospel. </span></span></span></span>



      Thresia had a special devotion to and an unfailing trust in the Holy Family whom she saw often in visions and from whom she routinely obtained guidance in her apostolate, praying much and fasting for the conversion of sinners. Thresia also had the mystical gift of the “stigmata”—which she carefully kept hidden from public view. Her frequent ecstasies and levitations had her lifted cross-like, high up on the wall in her room, every Friday. As though to ascertain the depth of her zeal and dedication, the Lord led her through periods of torment by way of diabolical attacks, while she assiduously fought temptations against faith and chastity.</span></span></span></span>



      It was in 1913 that with her dream of having a prayer house finally taking shape, Thresia was able to lead the life she had always aspired to—of prayer, penance and austerity. 14 May 1914 marked the canonical erection of the Congregation of the Holy Family with herself the first Superior of the new Congregation.</span></span></span></span>



      Mother Mariam Thresia died in 1926 and was beatified during the Jubilee Year 2000.</span></span></span></span>
