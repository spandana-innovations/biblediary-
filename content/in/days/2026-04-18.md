    body: |
      Reflection: 1



      After he withdrew from the crowd which wanted to make him king, Jesus manifests himself to his disciples in the lake. A whirlwind blows and agitates the lake when the disciples are alone in the boat. In the dangerous hour Jesus comes to rescue them, walking on the water and says to them, “It is I; do not be afraid.” The Greek word used by John means “I am” and has strong overtones of divinity. By saying this word Jesus shows them that he is the saving God. The whole incident teaches us that even when dangerous problems occur in our lives, we should not be afraid because Jesus will surely come to our rescue. The disciples are glad to take Jesus into their boat, and immediately they reach where they want to go. When we welcome Jesus into our lives, he changes our troubles into blessings.



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      The event presented by today’s Gospel is the scene immediately following Jesus’ miraculous feeding of the five thousand with five small loaves of bread and two fish. Sensing the danger of having the people seize him and make him leader of a revolt, Jesus promptly instructed the apostles to leave the place by boat and, after dispersing the crowd, went by himself to the mountain to pray.



      A double miracle in the sea: When the apostles in the boat were three to four miles away from the shore, they faced an unexpected storm, caused by the hot wind of the desert rushing into the Sea of Galilee through the gaps in the Golan Heights. Recognizing the danger, Jesus went to the boat, walking on the stormy sea. Jesus calmed the frightened disciples as he approached the boat, and as soon as he got into the boat it “reached land they were heading for.”



      <span class="subhead">Life messages:</span> 1) We need to approach Jesus with strong Faith in his ability and availability to calm the storms in our lives and in the life of the Church. Church history shows us how Jesus saved his Church from the storms of persecution in the first three centuries, from the storms of heresies in the 5th and 6th centuries, from the storms of moral degradation and the Protestant reformation movement in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, and the storms of ongoing Clerical sex abuse scandals, particularly those beginning in the twentieth century. 2) We need to ask Jesus to protect us when we face storms of strong temptations, storms of doubts about our religious beliefs, and storms of fear, anxiety, and worries in our personal lives. 3) Experiencing Jesus’ presence in our lives, we need to confess our Faith in him and call out for his help and protection.

    body: |
      The readings of today show us the consequence of the absence of Jesus in our lives. In John, the disciples are alone in a boat, struggling against a storm in the dark. Fear and anxiety overwhelm them until Jesus comes to them, bringing peace and calm. In Acts, the early Christian community faces a problem: some members feel neglected, and the proclamation of the Good News suffers as the apostles become overwhelmed by too many other tasks. This lack of focus on the Good News creates tension and frustration. To resolve this, the apostles appoint helpers to look after the community’s needs, ensuring no one is left out. The readings today remind us that when Jesus is missing from our lives, confusion and selfishness can take over. But when we invite Him in, He brings peace and order, helping us serve others with love. Keeping Jesus at the centre of our hearts allows us to find true peace and unity in our lives and communities.

      Saint Galdinus is honoured in Milan (Italy) as one of its principal patrons; his name appears linked with that of Saints Ambrose and Charles Borromeo at the close of every litany of the Milanese rite. As a member of the famous Valvarsi della Scala family, he was chancellor and archdeacon under two archbishops of Milan, winning the confidence of the clergy and people.



      When Pope Alexander III was elected in 1150, a few dissentient cardinals elected a rival pope more favourable to Frederick I Barbarossa. Milan had already offended the emperor by claiming the right to select its own magistrates but when the citizens acknowledged Alexander III he became further infuriated. Archbishop Hubert and his archdeacon Galdinus were forced to withdraw into exile, and the following year, Frederick, with a great army, laid siege to the city.



      In 1166, on the death of Archbishop Hubert of Milan, Galdinus, much against his own will, was consecrated his successor by Pope Alexander III, with additional charge of Lombardy. Deeply concerned about the poor, the new Prelate made the care and comfort of his distressed flock his chief priority. Thus, when the Lombard states entered into a league to rebuild Milan, Galdinus threw himself heart and soul into the new undertaking.



      He preached constantly and enforced discipline among the clergy who, incidentally, had become lax. In time, as his earthly sojourn drew to a close, although too weak to celebrate Mass, he succeeded in delivering a stirring sermon against all false doctrines. Towards the conclusion of the Mass he lost consciousness and died. He was canonized by the then reigning Pope, Alexander III.
