  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Mark 3:1–6"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Today’s Gospel describes a miraculous healing done by Jesus on one Sabbath as a public violation of Sabbath law to prove that God’s intention for the Sabbath was to do good and to save life rather than to do evil or to destroy life.



      The incident and the reaction: Ex 20:8 and Dt 5:12 instructed the Jews to keep the Sabbath holy. But the Scribes and the Phariseeshadamplified God’s law on the Sabbath by misinterpreting it and had made it burdensome for the common people through man-made laws. Jesus wanted to demonstrate in public the original intention of God in declaring the Sabbath holy. For Jesus, the Sabbath was a day of rest to be used in adoring God, learning and teaching His laws, and doing good to/for others. Hence, Jesus took the liberty of granting healing to a man with a withered hand in the local synagogue immediately after the worship service, thus infuriating the scribes and the Pharisees.



      Life messages:



      1) Our Christian Sabbath, that is, our Sunday, observance of participating in the Eucharistic celebration is meant to recharge our spiritual batteries for doing good to/for others and avoiding evil.



      2) Our Sunday observance is also meant to be an offering of our lives to God on the altar, to ask God’s pardon and forgiveness for our sins, to present our needs before the Lord and to participate in the Divine Life by Holy Communion.



      3) Sunday is also a day for us to spend time with the members of the family and to participate in the activities of our parish and neighborhood.

      Jesus picks up another controversy at Capernaum by healing a man with a withered hand on a Sabbath. Jesus performs a miracle as well as he gives a new teaching. Jesus transcends the parameters of Rabbinical debates and understanding of Sabbath laws and reaches out in love to the person in need. Jesus holds on to the principles of love and goodness. As a result, the Pharisees plot against Jesus to destroy him. Faith requires our response to God’s invitation. The man stretched out his hands at the command of Jesus in order to be healed. Like David, filled with the Spirit and trust in God, was able to defeat Goliath, we shall approach Jesus for healing of our withered hearts.

    ref: "Saint Anthony the Great - Abbot Confessor (251-356)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Born and raised in southern Egypt by pious Christian parents, Anthony was orphaned at eighteen. Called to the eremitical life, he sold his inheritance for the benefit of the poor, and retired to a deserted tomb for fifteen years, and then for another twenty years shutting himself off in an abandoned fort on a mountainside near Der el Memum, refusing to see anyone.

      The number of his disciples nevertheless increased continually, settling down in nearby caves and huts and, in 305, they succeeded in persuading Anthony to become their spiritual guide. The saint emerged, not emaciated as expected after his rigorous fasts and vigils but vigorous and healthy.

      For five years Anthony devoted himself completely to the organization and spiritual instruction of his disciples. In 311 Anthony went to Alexandria to encourage the faithful who were smarting under the Maximinan persecution, before retiring to a mountainside in the desert of Clysma, between the Nile and the Red Sea where there stands to this day the monastery named after him “Der Mar Antonios”. Here he spent the remaining 45 years of his life, advising and inspiring all who came to him, and making frequent visits to his first foundation at Der el Memum. About 335 he went once more to Alexandria and preached against the errors of the Arians and Manichaeans.

      Anthony shared a deep bond with Saint Athanasius who has left us an authentic life of the saintly founder of Christian monasticism, portraying him as highly dignified, full of divine peace and joy, and filled with marvellous zeal for God’s work. He died at the venerable old age of 105 and his intercession is sought especially in cases of epilepsy, pestilence, ery¬sipelas (“Saint Anthony’s Fire”), and skin diseases.

From each of his companions, he should gather some good lesson—from one, modesty, from another silence, from a third patience, from a fourth resignation and indifference”* (Saint Anthony the Great).
