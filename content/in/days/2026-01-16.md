      Reflection: 1



      A paralytic was brought by his friends to Jesus. As they could not reach Jesus because of the crowd they took the patient to the roof of the house where Jesus was and let him down on his bed through a hole they made there. Jesus healed him with the words, “Son, your sins are forgiven.” Jesus healed not only the disease but also its cause, sin. The teachers of the law objected because only God can forgive sins. They failed to see hat God’s power was working through Jesus. Jesus knew their thoughts and, to prove that he had authority to forgive sins, said to the paralytic to pick up his bed and go home. The man now got up, carried his own bed and walked freely. We notice the fraternal concern of his friends and the trouble they took for him to get him healed, besides the compassion of Jesus and his authority to forgive sins. How compassionate are we to others, and how much trouble are we willing to take for them?



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      Today’s Gospel presents the last in a series of five healing stories. This one demonstrates the power of Faith, and in this particular case we learn what others can do for us if they are persons of Faith. As soon as Jesus got back to Capernaum after a preaching tour of Galilee, the crowds gathered in and around the house, so that there was no room to get in or out. Four men, carrying their paralyzed friend, tried in vain to get to the house through the crowd. Here is the wonderful picture of a man who was saved by the Faith of his friends. His friends were men who had trusting Faith in the healing power of Jesus, and they were men with initiative, tenacity, and creativity. So they carried their friend to the roof of the house, made a hole in the roof, and lowered the man on his mat, placing him right in front of Jesus. Luke tells us that there were in the crowd Pharisees and Doctors of the Law from Galilee, Judea, and Jerusalem sent to check out Jesus, the new preacher, and to report back to the Sanhedrin.



      The sick man’s paralysis was seen by the people around as a punishment for some serious sin in his own life or the lives of his parents. It was a common belief that no major sickness could be cured until sin was forgiven. For that reason, Jesus began the young man’s healing by audibly forgiving his sins, so that he might feel no longer estranged from God. Then the young man was able to receive the physical healing he and his friends desired for him. But the Pharisees thought that, in forgiving sin, Jesus had insulted God by blasphemy, because forgiving sin is the exclusive prerogative of God. Jesus asked them whether it was more difficult to command, [and be obeyed], *“Your sins are forgiven,”* or *“Rise and walk.*” Jesus posed the question so that when Jesus commanded the man to rise, take up his bed and walk, the man, his enemies must recognize that both Jesus’ authority to forgive sin, and Jesus’ healing power were from God. Getting no response, Jesus commanded the paralytic to rise and he did so, but we do not know whether any of the objectors believed in Jesus.



      Life message: We are called to intercede for others and to bring them to Christ. 1) In the Old Testament, it is Moses who constantly begs God’s mercy and forgiveness for the Israelites’ sins. Later, we find the prophets interceding for the unfaithful Israelites. 2) In the New Testament, the dramatic role played by the friends of the paralyzed man in the healing story reminds us of the continuing need for, and power of, intercession for/by others. The text gives us encouragement to intercede for those who are ill or in special need. When we pray and invite God into the situation, healing takes place.

      Today’s Gospel story describes the healing of a paralytic man. That the paralytic is “carried by four men” not only points to the crippling effects of his incapacity and powerlessness to walk alone and his dependence on others, but also to the communal and interpersonal dimension of human solidarity, especially in times of urgent need. The four friends’ innovative effort involves three actions: “opening of the roof”, “making a hole” and “lowering the mat”. Opening of the roof is not just about letting down the individual but also about the care and concern of those around him, guided by deep faith in the healing power of Jesus, who not only heals the paralytic physically but also spiritually (forgiveness of sins). May the opening of the roof *let us in* to be faithful and *let us out* to be creative disciples of Jesus.

    audio: null
    body: |
      Ordained priest at twenty-five in Old Goa in 1676 by Monsignor Antonio Brandao, Archbishop of Goa, Joseph, born the third son to Christopher and Maria Vaz, went on to become one of the greatest missionaries produced by Asia.



      Setting off for Kanara in 1681, Joseph’s ministry took him to Ceylon (now Sri Lanka) where, at Kandy, he proved his missionary acumen, facing bravely the vagaries of time and weather, and hardship of every kind including suspicion and imprisonment just so that the Lord’s Word is proclaimed and His love reigns supreme!



      Long before inculturation became popular, Joseph had already shown how authentic Christian asceticism could be blended with Indian *sanyasism*. He learnt the local language, Sinhala, and compiled a English-Sinhala dictionary for the benefit of his companions! Such was his practice of the Faith that his whole life has come to be regarded as “an object lesson in missionary methods” (Father Cyril Papali) that drew, rather than repulsed, non-Christians to the Faith. His dealings with people of every rank and status earned him the distinction of being referred to as “the perfect model of an apostle” (Father Pierre Charles).



      Joseph’s pioneering efforts in organizing the first ever indigenous missionary institute in the Third World, known as the Oratory of the Cross of Miracles of Old Goa, ensured the continuance of his work, saving the Church in Sri Lanka from virtual extinction.



      His love for Our Lady was such that in a letter written shortly after his ordination, he states: “Let it be known to all who see this ‘Letter of Bondage’, angels, human beings and all creatures, that I, Father Joseph Vaz, sell and offer myself as a perpetual slave of the Virgin Mother of God.”



      Dying as he had lived, serene and peaceful, a light to all around him, with a lighted candle in his hands and the sweet name of Jesus on his lips, Joseph was beatified in 1995 and canonized in 2015.
