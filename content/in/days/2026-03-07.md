      Reflection: 1



      Jesus told the parable of the prodigal son to the Pharisees and the scribes who criticized him for welcoming sinners and eating with them. Both the sons in the parable go wrong in their view of themselves and their father. They consider themselves like paid servants, and fail to understand their father’s love. The elder son, in spite of the father’s pleas, remains adamant and unchanged. But the younger son, though he went away from the father, recognizes his fault and returns home, and the father welcomes him wholeheartedly regardless of his past. Through this parable Jesus confronts the Pharisees of his time. It applies equally to the Pharisees of today who pretend to be holier than the others and do not fraternize with them. The younger son can be compared to sinners and tax-collectors; and the elder son, with the scribes and the Pharisees.



      No matter how far we stray from Him, the loving Father welcomes us when we repent and return to Him. In today’s parable Jesus teaches that God is love. Let us use this Lenten season to correct the mistakes in our lives.



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      Chapter 15 of Luke’s Gospel has been called “the Gospel within the Gospel,” because it is the distilled essence of the Good News about our Heavenly Father. The whole chapter is essentially one complete parable, the “Parable of the Lost and Found,” with three illustrations: the story of the lost sheep, the story of the lost coin and the story of the lost son. These parables remind us that we have a God Who welcomes sinners and forgives their sins when they return to Him with genuine contrition, resolved to reform. In addition, He is always in search of His lost and straying children.



      The lost son: This parable speaks about the deep effects of sin, the self-destruction of hatred, and the infinite mercy of God. This is a story of love, of conflict, of deep heartbreak, and of ecstatic joy. The scene opens on a well-to-do Jewish family. With the immaturity of a spoiled brat, the younger son impudently extracts his share of the coming inheritance from his gracious father. He sells out his share and then squanders the money in a faraway city. Then, bankrupt and starving, the prodigal son ends up feeding pigs, a task that was forbidden to a Jew (Lv 11:7; 14:8). Finally, comng “to his senses” (v. 17), he decides to return to his father, asking for forgiveness and begging to be given the status of a hired servant. When he sees his son returning, however, the father runs to him, embraces him, kisses him and gives him a new robe, a ring and new shoes. The father also throws a great feast for him, to celebrate his return, killing the “*fatted calf’”* reserved for the Passover feast, so that all may rejoice with him at the wanderer’s return.



      <span class="subhead">Life messages:</span> 1) We need to meet the challenge for self-evaluation: If we have been in sin, God’s mercy is seeking us, searching for our souls with a love that is wild beyond all imagining. God is no less ready to receive and welcome us back than Jesus was to welcome sinners in his time. 2) We should also ask God for the courage to extend this forgiveness to others who have offended us. 3) Let us confess our sins and regain peace and God’s friendship. The first condition for experiencing the joy and relief of having our sins forgiven is to see them as they are and give them up. We have to be humble enough to recognize that we need God’s forgiveness to be whole.

      The Parable of the Prodigal Son(s) beautifully unveils God’s boundless love through three central figures. The younger son – who was lost and found – represents our tendency to stray, seeking fulfilment outside the Father’s embrace, only to find emptiness. The elder son – who was also lost, but given the encouragement to come back – mirrors our reluctance to accept the mercy shown to others, a reminder of the Pharisees’ hardened hearts. Yet, towering above these distorted characters is the Loving Father, whose unconditional love and forgiveness welcome us back, no matter the depth of our failings. Amid life’s messes – whether spiritual, mental, social, academic, familial, or personal – God waits with open arms. Like the prophet Micah declares, He casts our sins into the sea. Yes, God’s embrace is open; His mercy has no bounds! No matter how far we stray, His love awaits our return. Let us return, wholeheartedly, to His embrace and find restoration in His mercy.

    body: |
      The record of the passion of St Perpetua, her slave St Felicity and their companions is among the most ancient, reliable hagiological histories that have come down to us.



      During the persecutions of Septimius Severus, five catechumens were arrested. They were Vibia Perpetua, a noblewoman of 22 and her infant, Felicity, a pregnant slave, Revocatus, Satwininus, Secundulus and later Saturus their Catechist. While still under arrest they were baptized. Perpetua’s father besought her to apostatize, because she was his favourite child. She, however, had the vision of a golden ladder guarded by the dragon and strewn with hooks and weapons that made difficult the ascent. But, following the example of Saturus, she trod over the dragon and, climbing the ladder, reached a beautiful place where a tall man in the garment of a shepherd welcomed her and offered her curds and milk. This was the premonition that she was to suffer and die.



      Perpetua, Felicity and their companions were all condemned and ordered to be thrown before the wild beasts, at the garrison games festival in honour of Ceasar Geta. On the day of their triumph, they were flogged and were beheaded by the gladiators.
