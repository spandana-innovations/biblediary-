  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Matthew 7:15–20"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Jesus draws attention to the gap between appearance and reality. Just as there can be more to some people than meets the eye, so there can be less to others than meets the eye. It is that second situation that Jesus warns about: those who look like sheep but underneath are ravenous wolves. They project an attractive image but it is false and deceptive. What’s really in our hearts does not always correspond to how we appear to others.



      The real test of our hearts is the kind of attitude that bears fruit in our behaviour. “You can tell them by their fruits.” St Paul used that same imagery of “fruit” when, in his letter to the Galatians, he speaks about the “fruit of the Spirit”–“love, joy, peace, kindness, generosity, faithfulness, gentleness and self-control.” Even though Paul lists a variety of qualities, he doesn’t speak of “fruits” (plural) but of “fruit” (singular).There is one fruit of the Spirit which can be described in all these different ways; the term which best describes this comes first in Paul’s list, “love.” If our lives bear that kind of fruit, our heart belongs to God. We are like the “sound tree” that Jesus calls us to be.

  - key: reflection
    title: Reflection
    ref: null
    audio: null
    body: |
      A prophet is someone who speaks on behalf of God. Jesus warns his listeners to be beware of the false prophets, and how they are to be recognized. It is by their fruits that we identify them. Fruit is a metaphor for their works or what they produce. One’s identity is determined not by what one says but by what one does, because what one does inevitably reveals one’s heart. Today false prophets come to us in different forms including the media materials we are exposed to. The test is not for the existence of fruit, but for the quality of fruit. We need to make wise assessments about people and media we see and listen to. It is better to keep ourselves away from them whose fruits are inconsistent with the values of Christ.

      St Jose maria Escrívá de Balaguer (Priest) (1902-1975)

      Josemaria was a man who became a “master of Christian living” by reaching “the heights of contemplation with continuous prayer, constant mortification” and “exemplary docility to the movements of the Holy Spirit.”

      It was at the early age of 16 that Josemaria felt called to some as yet unknown but important task. With characteristic faith he responded with the words of scripture: “Here I am Lord, because you have called me” (1 Sam 3:5). Ordained priest in 1925, he took up the study of civil law in Madrid in 1927, his apostolic zeal soon having him interacting with a cross-section of society—artists and academicians, labourers and intellectuals, army-men and clergymen alike. To strive for sanctity by way of living Christ’s own life among his people as enjoined by Baptism was the call that became evident in 1928 when making a retreat. Thus was born Opus Dei whose mission would be “to open up in the Church a new vocational path aimed at spreading the quest for holiness and the practice of the apostolate through sanctification of ordinary work in the world.”

      With the rapid spread of Opus Dei came trials and hardships of every kind, financial difficulties assailing the worldwide expansion of apostolic works. Josemaria’s sense of humour, however, stood him in good stead. In 1954, he was miraculously cured of a serious form of diabetes that had troubled him for over a decade.

      The heart of Josemaria’s message is: Sanctify your daily life, and sanctify yourself in and through that daily life. Beatified in 1992, he was finally canonized in 2002.

      Reflection: “To be holy is not easy, but it is not difficult either. To be holy is to be a good Christian, to resemble Christ.” (St Josemaria Escrivá).
