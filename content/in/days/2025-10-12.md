      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2Our response to healing



      The details of today’s Gospel episode are well-known. Jesus cures ten lepers, sending them to the priests, who can authorize the return of the healed lepers to their families. The account could have ended here…. but the evangelist is interested in highlighting the reaction of one of them.



      Once cured, the other nine lepers disappear from the scene and we know nothing more about them, as if they took the cure for granted and just got on with their lives. But one of them, *realizing he has been healed*, understands just what a great gift he has received: and that God is the source of his healing. Excited, he returns *glorifying God in a loud voice* and *thanking Jesus*.



      Most commentaries interpret the Samaritan’s reaction as pointing to the theme of thanksgiving: the other nine are ungrateful; only the one who has returned knows how to be thankful. Certainly, that is what the story appears to suggest. But Jesus does not speak of *gratitude.* The one Samaritan has returned *glorifying God*… and giving praise to God is much more than saying thanks.



      For a person of faith who is tested with illness, pain or any affliction, healing is a privileged experience that leads to giving glory to God. There is a famous quote from St. Irenaeus of Lyon that *the glory of God is man fully alive*. The healed body of this leper is a body that sings glory to God. We think we know all about how our organism functions, yet the healing of a serious illness never fails to surprise us still. It is always a “mystery” to experience in ourselves how life recovers, our strength is renewed and how, consequently, our trust and freedom grow.



      Few experiences are so radical and basic as being healed, so that we get a taste of victory in the face of evil, of the triumph of life over the threat of death. That is why, with healing, we are offered the possibility of welcoming in a wholly new way the God who comes to us as the foundation of our being and the font of new life.



      Modern medicine lets people today to live the process of healing more frequently than in times past. We are of course grateful to those who cure us, but our healing can also be the stimulus to begin a new relationship with God. We can pass from indifference to faith, from rejection to welcoming, from doubt to trust, from fear to love. This gift of God can cure us of fears, emptiness and wounds that hurt us. It can make us have deep roots in life in a more healthy and free manner. It can wholly heal us.

      The Gospel of today has many interrelated dimensions: of healing, of gratitude (or lack of it) and of the Samaritan, who is a foreigner. We are told of the ten lepers who asked Jesus for ‘mercy’; they were asking him to cure them of the disease which had made them outcasts in their society. Realising their suffering, Jesus heals them instructing them to “go and show yourselves to the priests.” They do so with diligence; delighted with their healing they completely forget who has cured them. All of them but one who is a Samaritan, a ‘foreigner’, go away, the Samaritan coming back to thank Jesus, falling at his feet and praising God. Jesus is prompted to ask ‘Were not ten cleansed? Where are the nine?” We need to introspect on the way we often treat ‘others’: those who are not like us, the strangers in our midst.

    audio: null
    body: |
      St Wilfrid, a native of Northumbria (England), who was to become one of the great men of his age, is best remembered for his part in championing the supreme ecclesiastical authority of Rome. Born of noble stock and educated at the court of King Osway, at Lindisfarne and at Canterbury, he accompanied St Benedict Biscop to Rome as a young man. On his return he founded the monastery of Ripon and, though aged only 24, became its first Abbot. Here he introduced the Benedictine rule, and five years later was ordained priest. At the famous Synod of Whitby, in 664, he was able to persuade the majority to accept for all of England the Roman usage concerning Easter and the tonsure, in preference to the customs which had been introduced by the Scottish monks from Iona.



      Shortly after, Wilfrid was made Archbishop of York and received his consecration in France. His energetic administration witnessed the founding of a large number of Benedictine monasteries and the further extension of Roman usage and church chant. But when King Egfrid and St Theodore proceeded to divide his archdiocese into three parts, St Wilfrid went in appeal to Rome — the first known instance of an English Prelate’s appeal. He won his case, but upon his return was imprisoned and then exiled by the King. He thus preached the faith for five years in Sussex and Wessex, and there converted King St Ceadwalla. Not until 686, was a reconciliation effected so that he could return to York. He died on 24 April c.709.
