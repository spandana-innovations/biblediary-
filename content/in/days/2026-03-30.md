      Reflection: 1



      Jesus visits the home of Lazarus whom he had raised from death to life. While Jesus is reclining at table with Lazarus, Mary anoints the feet of Jesus with a costly perfume. She is full of love and gratitude to Jesus who accepted her as a disciple. Jesus points out the symbolic manning of this action as an indication of his death and burial. But Judas who was to betray Jesus criticizes her for not selling the perfume and giving the money to the poor because of his own materialistic and financial interest. Because many Jews believed in Jesus on account of Lazarus, the chief priests decided to kill Lazarus as well so that their own interests would be safe. Reflecting on the treacherous kiss which Judas was soon going to give Jesus, let us pray for the grace to deepen our love for Jesus and to make our devotion more genuine. </span></span></span></span>



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      The scene depicted in today’s Gospel reading took place exactly one week before the crucifixion of Jesus. Jesus was anointed on two different occasions — first, at the start of his public ministry, in Galilee, as recounted by St. Luke (7:36-50: Jesus was anointed by a repentant sinful woman in the house of a Pharisee), and, second, towards the end of his life, in Bethany, as reported here by St. John. Instead of brooding over the sufferings and death waiting for him, Jesus, along with his Apostles, enjoyed a party given for his friend, Lazarus by his family and friends. Jesus was on his way to Jerusalem to be crucified. Probably he halted in Bethany, both because Jerusalem was overcrowded, and because Jesus wanted to spend time in with his closest friends.</span></span></span></span></span>



      The Gospel summarizes two attitudes: Mary’s and Judas.’ Mary expressed her love for Jesus and her gratitude to God by an extravagant action. Before all the Apostles, she anointed Jesus’ feet with nard (a very costly perfume worth (said Judas critically), the wages of a labourer for 300 days), then wiped them with her hair. Her lovely deed shows the extravagance of love. Judas on the other hand criticized Mary for spending so much on perfume, suggesting that the money could have been used to help the poor. Mary’s action was extravagant, but her motive justified it. However, Judas’ motive in suggesting the good action of helping the poor was selfish and impure, spoiling the action.</span></span></span></span></span>



      <span class="subhead">Life messages: </span></span></span>1) We need to express our love and gratitude to Jesus during this Holy Week for what he did for us centuries ago and for what he continues to do for us now: a) He poured out his Blood for us and for our salvation. b) He anointed us with his Holy Spirit and made us the Temple of his Spirit. c) He continues to nourish us with his Body and Blood through the Holy Eucharist. 2. We can express our love and gratitude to our Saviour Jesus during Holy Week a) by spending more time in prayer and adoration, especially by participating in the liturgical celebrations b) by doing acts of penance for our past sins and c) by actively engaging in acts of loving and humble service. </span></span></span></span></span>

      Reflecting on the profound love of Mary, we see a model of devotion that disregards shame and societal judgement. Her singular focus was Jesus, embodying ‘a love that knows no bounds’. Can we aspire to nurture such an undivided love for Him in our lives? Conversely, we confront the danger of materialism that can corrupt our piety, as illustrated by Judas. Let us examine our hearts: Is our devotion diminishing in the face of distractions? This week, let’s recommit to living authentically, prioritising our relationship with Christ above all else. ‘True love is not just felt; it is lived.’</span></span></span>
  - key: saint
    title: Saint of the Day
    saintName: "Saint John Climacus, Abbot Confessor (c. 525–605)"
    ref: "Saint John Climacus, Abbot Confessor (c. 525–605)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Syrian by birth, John lived in a cave-hermitage at the foot of Mt Sinai in the desert of Thola, studying the lives of the Saints and gaining a reputation for profound learning. At 75, he was elected Abbot, and such was his holiness and wisdom, that even Pope St Gregory the Great was informed of it and sent him a contribution for his hospital and pilgrim’s hostel and asked for his prayers. But St John resigned his office after some 4 years in order to return to his hermitage and prepare himself for death.</span></span></span></span>



      The most important of his writings is entitled*Ladder to Paradise,* “Scala Parodisi”in Latin, meaning climax,from which has been derived his surname Climacus. This ascetical treatise, which attained great popularity throughout the Church and was translated into many languages, was addressed to anchorites and cenobites; its 30 “rungs” (chapters) — in memory of our Lord’s 30 years of hidden life — treat of the vices to be subdued and the virtues to be cultivated in order to attain the highest spiritual perfection. The practical application of the precepts is richly illustrated with parables and historical facts.</span></span></span></span>



      St John Climacus died in his hermitage on 30 March 605 aged 80.</span></span></span></span>
