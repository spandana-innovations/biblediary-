    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Matthew 6:1–6, 16–18"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Reflection: 1



      Jesus insists that good actions must be done with good intentions. If one does good actions for publicity and self-promotion, that is his reward; he earns no merit because his deeds are hypocritical. This is true of every good deed: doing acts of charity, praying, fasting etc. If we do these things for the sake of being noticed by others that they may praise us and consider us righteous people, we cannot expect a reward from the Father in heaven. Even if no one knows the good things we do, he Father sees them in secret and will certainly reward us. We should have right motivation in everything we do and should be happy to make some sacrifices for the genuine good of others. That will undoubtedly please God.



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      In today’s passage from the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus takes three cardinal works of religious life in Judaism, namely, almsgiving, fasting, and prayer, and instructs the apostles, the crowd of disciples, and us on the principles underlying these acts of personal piety.



      <span class="subhead">Life Messages: </span>



      1) Almsgiving becomes a noble and meritorious religious act when we give alms to others in order to bring glory to God. a) We are to help the poor as an expression of our sharing love, in thanksgiving for the blessings we have received from God. b) But Almsgiving becomes solely an act of self-glorification when we do it as the Pharisees did, to demonstrate our generosity in public and to get popular acclaim.



      2) Fasting becomes a noble act pleasing to God when we do it: a) to experience what the real hunger of the poor is, b) to help the poor better by giving the price of what we do not eat to feed them, c) to discipline ourselves in eating and drinking and d) to appreciate better God’s blessings of good health, good appetite, and generous provisions. e) Fasting solely for show, as the Pharisees did, is wrong and sinful.



      3) Prayer: Prayer is opening our connection to God by talking to Him and listening to Him, convinced of His all-pervading holy presence within us and all around us. a) By prayer we acknowledge our total dependence on God, draw from Him our daily spiritual strength, and recharge our spiritual batteries from God’s infinite power. b) Long, noisy, repetitious prayer performed in public solely for show as the Pharisees did is no prayer at all. It is hypocrisy.

  - key: reflection
    title: Reflection
    ref: null
    audio: null
    body: |
      By giving alms to the needy, we imitate God Himself, who is generous towards us. However, this practice can also be corrupted when we give alms with the motive of showing off our ‘generosity’ to be praised by others. Again, prayer puts us in direct contact with God. However, this noble practice has its corrupt side, as people use it as a show of piety to be praised by those who watch them. And, fasting helps us serve God with total love. It brings us face to face with the reality of deprivation and misery being experienced by millions of people in the world. However, when we practise fasting for the motive of getting the approval and sympathy of others, it loses its merit. Saint Paul says, “God loves a cheerful giver.” Are we cheerful and rightly motivated in our charity, in our prayers and our penance?
  - key: saint
    title: Saint of the Day
    saintName: "Sts Mark and Marcellian (Martyrs) (- 287)"
    ref: "Sts Mark and Marcellian (Martyrs) (- 287)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Sts Mark and Marcellian were twins hailing from an illustrious family in Rome. They had been converted to the faith in their youth and were honourably married. When Diocletian ascended the imperial throne in 284, the persecution of Christians increased. Both Mark and Marcellian were cast into prison and tormented and condemned by Chromatius, Lieutenant of the Prefect of Rome, to be beheaded.



      Their friends, however, obtained a 30 days’ respite of the execution, that they might prevail on them to worship the false gods. Tranquillinus and Martia, their afflicted heathen parents, in company with their sons’ wives and their little babes, in vain endeavoured to move them with the most tender entreaties and tears.



      On the other hand, St Sebastian, an officer of the imperial household, coming to Rome soon after their commitment, visited them daily and encouraged them to persevere in their faith. The result of the confabulations was the happy conversion of the father, mother and wives, followed by Nicostratus the public registrar and then Chromatius, the judge, who retired after freeing the saints. Mark and Marcellian were hid by a Christian officer of the household in his apartments, in the palace. But they were betrayed by an apostate and recaptured. They were condemned by Fabian—who had succeeded Chromatius—to be bound to two pillars, with their feet nailed to the same. Kept in this posture a day and a night, they were lanced to death the following day.
