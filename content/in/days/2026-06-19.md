  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Mathew 6: 19-23"
    audio: null
    body: |
      In today’s passage from the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus instructs the crowd that they should not pray like the Gentiles, repeating empty phrases. He means that true prayer is not so much a matter of the number of words as of the frequency and the love with which one turns towards God, raising one’s mind and heart to God. So, Jesus teaches them a model prayer. Jesus’ prayer, “Our Father,” consists of two parts. In the first part, we praise and worship God, addressing Him as our loving, caring, and providing Heavenly Father and asking Him to help us to do His Holy Will in our lives as obediently and lovingly as His Will is done in Heaven and, thus, to remain remaining in His kingdom. In the second part, we present our petitions before the Triune God. First, we ask God for our present needs, food clothing, and shelter, (“*give us this day our daily bread”),*then for our past needs, especially for forgiveness of our sins (“*forgive us our trespassesas we forgive those who trespass againstus”),* and finally, for our future needs, protection against the tempter and his temptations (“*and lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil”)*. In this part, we also bring the Trinitarian God into our lives. We bring in: 1) God the Father, the Provider, by asking for daily bread; 2) God the Son, our Saviour, by asking forgiveness for our sins; and 3) God the Holy Spirit, the Paraclete, Who is our Guide, Advocate, Comforter, and Illuminator, by asking for protection and deliverance from evil. Special stress on the spirit of forgiveness: We are told to ask for forgiveness from others for our offenses against them, and to offer unconditional forgiveness to others for their offenses against us as a condition for receiving God’s forgiveness. Jesus clarifies, “*If you forgive others their wrongs, your Father in Heaven will also forgive yours. If you do not forgive others, then your Father will not forgive you either*” (*Mt* 6:14-15).



      *“For Thine is the Kingdom, the Power, and the Glory, now and forever. Amen*.” The manuscripts of the Gospel of Matthew do not contain this phrase, nor do any of the Catholic translations. Martin Luther added this doxology to the Our Father in his translation of Matthew’s Gospel, and the *King James* editions of the Bible keep it. The doxology is actually taken from the Divine Liturgy or Catholic Mass. Known as the final doxology, it takes up the first three petitions to our Father. By the final “Amen,” which means, “So be it”, we ratify what is contained in the prayer that God has taught us.

    body: |
      Today’s Gospel urges us to seek what is everlasting rather than temporary. It reminds us that wealth, health, relationships, power, and reputation are fleeting, while our place in the heavenly kingdom is everlasting. To attain this place, we need to undergo daily conversion and cultivate a loving relationship with God and others. As St Ignatius of Loyola expressed, “How insignificant the earth seems to me when I consider heaven.” Throughout history, many martyrs have embraced this truth, sacrificing their lives for the Kingdom of Heaven. St Don Bosco once said, “A piece of heaven fixes everything.” Ultimately, we should evaluate what we consider as important in life and whether it will be significant when we stand before God.

    ref: "St Romuald (Abbot, Confessor) (952-1027)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Born and raised in an illustrious family in Ravenna (Italy), Romuald led a frivolous life until shocked into realization at the age of 20, when he witnessed the murder of a near relative by his father. In order to atone for this crime he entered the Benedictine monastery of St Apollinare at Classe, near Ravenna; but the persistent laxity of its monks made him seek out the hermit Marinus, under whose guidance he went on to live a life of extraordinary severity.



      In 996 Otho III had him designated Abbot of St Apollinare, but Romuald resigned the charge after three years and spent the next 30 years wandering all over northern Italy, southern France and northeastern Spain, reforming monasteries and establishing hermitages for serious and determined souls. His most famous foundation, which endures to this day, is at Camaldoli, charmingly situated in a high wooded glen of the Appennines. Here, in 1012, he at first erected five hermit cells on the “Campus Maldoli”, the land which had been given him by a certain Maldolus. Although Romuald had no intention of founding a new religious order, this was to become the motherhouse of the Camaldolese Order, a joint Congregation of Benedictine hermits and cenobites, which marked the revival of the eremitical life in the Western Church.



      Numerous miracles have occurred at the tomb of this saint whose ideal was to turn the entire world into a hermitage and all the people into monastic associates. In 1466 his body was found to be still incorrupt and, since 1481, lies enshrined at Fabriano.
