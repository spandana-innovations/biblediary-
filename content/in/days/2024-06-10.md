    body: |
      The“Beatitudes” form the introductory section in Mathew’s account of the Sermon on the Mount. They are the heart of the Sermon on the Mount, as the Sermon on the Mount is the heart of the whole Gospel, or the “Compendium of Christian Doctrine." This sermon contains the most essential aspects of Christian behavior that we need to live out, if we are to reach Christian perfection. In essence, the Beatitudes both fulfill and complete the Ten Commandments.



      Bombshells: In both Matthew and Luke the Beatitudes have been called a “series of bombshells” or blinding “flashes of lightning followed by deafening thunder of surprise and shock," because Jesus reverses our “natural” assumption that happiness lies in riches, power, influence, and pleasure. We believe in personal pride: Jesus blesses poverty of spirit. We seek pleasure: Jesus blesses those who mourn. We see the prosperity of aggressive people: Jesus blesses the meek. We love good food and drink: Jesus blesses those who hunger and thirst for righteousness. Thus, Jesus instructs his disciples in the paradoxical blessedness of poverty, hunger, sorrow, and persecution. In poverty, we recognize God’s reign; in hunger, His Providence; in sorrow, true happiness; and in persecution, true joy. In other words, the blessed on Jesus’ list are the poor in spirit, the compassionate, the meek, the merciful, the clean of heart, the peacemakers, and those who are willing even to be insulted and persecuted for their following of Jesus in action.



      <span class="subhead">*Life messages:*</span>



      1) We need to respond to the challenge of the Beatitudes in daily life. The Beatitudes propose to us a way of life, inviting us to identify with the poor, those who mourn, who are meek, and who hunger and thirst after justice.



      2) They challenge us to become compassionate people, to become men and women who are pure in heart, and to become peacemakers in our dealings with one another, in our families and in the society at large, even when this approach to things exposes us to ridicule and persecution.



      3) Let us remember that each time we reach out to help the needy, the sick and the oppressed, we share with them a foretaste of the promises of the Beatitudes here and now.



      (Fr. Tony: https://frtonyshomilies.com/)

    body: |
      In the gospel, Jesus surprises his followers by giving a series of statements that contribute to the ‘blessed life’ which is commonly known as the Beatitudes. It is countercultural and contrary to a common-sense list of things – poverty, hunger, meekness, grief, persecutions, etc., which Jesus says is at the root of being blessed by God. By giving these radical teachings, Jesus invites us to a meritorious attempt at earning salvation by living out these qualities in order to enter the kingdom of God. Thus, the beatitudes provide us the guidelines for the kind of life that God intends to produce in us. Living the beatitudes should begin with a gradual transformation of our inner life which will be reflected in our external behaviour.

      St Getulius and Companions (Martyrs) (c.120)

      Getulius, the husband of St Symphorosa, had been an officer in the Roman army under Trajan and Hadrian. Upon his conversion to the Christian faith, he resigned his commission and withdrew to his estate in the Sabine Hills, near Tivoli (Italy).

      Here he lived in seclusion, surrounded only by a little band of Christians whom he taught and supported. He was one day engaged in instructing some of his people when he was surprised by a visit from Cerealis, the imperial vicar, who had been sent to arrest him. Incidentally, Cerealis himself was won over to the Faith as a result of conversations with Getulius and with his brother Amantius who, though an ardent believer, still remained a tribune in the Roman army.

      As soon as the Emperor learned of what had taken place, he ordered the Consul Licinius to apprehend the three men and to condemn them to death unless they consented to abjure their faith. They were brought before the Consul who ordered them to renounce their faith. But they all made a bold confession, whereupon they were imprisoned for 27 days at Tivoli and subjected to diverse forms of torture—to no avail. They were finally beheaded or burnt at the stake on the Via Salaria. With them perished another Christian, named Primitivus. The relics of these Saints were buried by St Symphorosa in an arenarium upon her estate.
