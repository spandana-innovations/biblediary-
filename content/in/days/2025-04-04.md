      Reflection: 2



      Today’s Gospel passage describes Jesus’ secret journey to Jerusalem to participate in the Feast of Tabernacles (Booths), amid rumours of his possible arrest. But Jesus courageously made his public appearance in the crowd in Jerusalem and started teaching in the Temple. Naturally, people started wondering why the authorities did not arrest him.



      Jesus’ Messianic claim and the Jewish reaction: Jesus made two unique and seemingly blasphemous claims. 1) He claimed that he was the Messiah, God’s Anointed One. 2) Jesus made the additional claim that only he knew God as He is because Jesus had come from God. By this claim, Jesus contradicted the belief of the Jews that they had the perfect and final revelation of God given through Moses and the prophets. In addition, Jesus’ claim to be the Messiah, and the “Son of Man” prophesied by Daniel, with exclusive and intimate knowledge of God was, they thought, nothing but blasphemy. The Jews argued that a mere carpenter-turned- wandering-preacher from Nazareth could not be the Messiah because nobody was supposed to know where the Messiah would come from. According to Jewish belief, the Messiah would emerge quite unexpectedly from Mount Olivet, cross the Kedron Valley, enter the city of Jerusalem, be anointed by Elijah the prophet, take possession of the City and the Temple and establish His Messianic kingdom.



      Life messages: 1) Like the Jews, we, too, can be prejudiced and occasionally refuse to accept and follow the teachings of the Church. We need to have the humility to honor the teaching authority of the Church and its guidance by the Holy Spirit.



      2) We need to accept Jesus as our Lord and personal Savior, experience him through prayer and the Sacramental life and surrender our lives to him.



      3) Like the Jews who expected the surprise appearance of a super-human Messiah we, too, show the tendency to seek God only in miraculous and extraordinary events, ignoring His presence within us and in everyone around us.

      In today’s gospel passage, we observe a complex and dynamic scene of Jesus’ ministry. It shows his willingness to engage with various opinions and challenges, his proclamation of himself as sent by the Father whom he knows, and the ongoing opposition he faced as a result of this revelation of his relationship with the Father. It calls us to examine our beliefs, seek the truth in Jesus, and respond to his invitation to faith and eternal life. Jesus, the Living Water, is the source of spiritual life, satisfaction, and eternal fulfilment. He invites all who are spiritually thirsty to come to Him and experience a deep, lasting quenching of that thirst (v. 37-38). This metaphor underscores the central theme of Jesus as the way to salvation and the fulfilment of our deepest spiritual needs.

      Hailing from a noble Roman family in Cartagena, Spain, Saint Isidore was the younger brother of three other saints, viz., Leander, Archbishop of Seville, Fulgentius, Bishop of Eeija, and Florentina, the nun who is said to have governed 1,000 religious in 40 convents.



      In 599 Isidore succeeded his own brother at Seville. He so governed the great See for 37 years as to strengthen Church discipline and encourage monasticism and, in time, even converting to the Faith from Arianism the Visigoths, ruling Spain for almost 200 years.



      Among his synods, the fourth national council at Toledo in 633 was particularly important in that it obliged each Bishop to establish a seminary in his respective diocese and to encourage the study of Greek, Hebrew, law and medicine. An amazingly learned man, considered the last of the ancient Christian philosophers and great Latin Fathers, Isidore compiled a Spanish missal and breviary and so is credited with being the author of the beautiful Mozarabic Liturgy.



      This “School-master of the Middle Ages” who had mastered every branch of science, the best known among his versatile writings being his *Etymologiae*, a systematic anthology of knowledge which became the most popular text book for instruction in theology, asceticism, history, geography, astronomy, grammar, biography etc in the Middle Ages, died in 636, was canonized in 1598 and declared Doctor of the Church in 1722.

The more we are afflicted in this world, the greater is our assurance for the next. The more we sorrow in the present, the greater will be our joy in the future”*(Saint Isidore of Seville).
