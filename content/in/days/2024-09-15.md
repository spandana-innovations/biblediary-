  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Mark 8:27-35"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Today’s readings, stern and forthright, make it very clear. To drive home to the disciples who is the essential Christ and what does it mean to be a Christian, Jesus asks them: “Who do you say that I am?” Their answers carefully avoid any hint of suffering. Hence, the declaration of Jesus that “The Son of Man will suffer many things and be rejected by the elders and be killed” shocks them to the core. Peter wants a soft Christ and an easy religion. But that would be clearly contrary to what Christ wants. So Jesus treats him harshly to the point of rudeness and tells him: “Get behind me, Satan”. And Jesus explains, “You are not on the side of God, but of men” – men having a penchant for the soft and the sweet and the smooth. Then to make clear to all the harsh demands of Christianity Jesus calls to Him the crowd and speaks out: “If any man would come after Me, let him deny himself, take up his cross and follow Me.” Authentic Christianity is not an easy religion. The God of Christians, though comforting, is not a comfortable God. It is a God who sacrifices His own only Son for the redemption of the world. The Son of Man did suffer many things, was rejected and killed. Christianity is a religion that demands that we lose our life in order to save our life. </span></span></span></span>



      *(New Horizon Homilies, Fr Joe ssp and Fr Philip ssp)</span></span>*</span></span>

    body: |
      “For you are not setting your mind on the things of God, but on the things of man” (Mk 8:33b). These words of Jesus in the Gospel Reading of today are a stark reminder to all of us about the necessity of following the will of God and obeying His word. We may, like Peter, prefer to choose the easier path, a way that is pleasant and more convenient. But Jesus demands from us, if we really want to be his disciples, that we carry our cross – just as he carried his cross. Jesus has not only demanded this of us in words but has shown us through his own deeds. Just as we see in the Second Reading, faith to be genuine has to go along with works. As Isaiah prophesied and prayed, may we also be intent on following God’s will, no matter how demanding and difficult it is. ‘Thy will be done.’ </span></span></span></span>

      Our Lady of Sorrows



       A</span></span></span></span>s on the Friday in Passion Week, so again in mid-September, Holy Church especially commemorates Our Lady’s great sorrows. The seven principal ones were: the prophecy of the aged Simeon in the Temple, the 300 mile flight into Egypt with the Infant, the loss of Jesus in Jerusalem when he was a boy of 12, Mary’s encounter with her Divine Son on his way to Calvary, his crucifixion, the taking down of his body from the Cross and his burial. Today’s feast was first granted to the Servites in 1668, and when Pope Pius VII came out of exile in 1814, he extended it to the Universal Church in gratitude.</span></span></span></span>

      St Catherine of Genoa -</span></span></span></span>*Widow (1447 -1510)</span></span>*</span></span>








       B</span></span></span></span>orn the youngest of five children in the powerful Fieschi

       family of Genoa, Catherine lost her father when she was barely 14. At 16, Catherine, who was highly gifted, was married off by her mother to a profligate and violent tempered Genoese nobleman, Giulianno Adorno as part of a political realignment. During an ecsatatic vision in 1463, after having led a virtually wasteful life for 10 years, she was given an insight into God’s infinite love and Goodness, and as a result, was, for the remainder of her life, literally consumed by the fire of divine love; she received Holy Communion daily, and spent herself in heroically caring for the sick in the city hospital she governed, particularly during the plague which claimed four-fifths of Genoa’s population between 1497 and 1501. In this mission she was joined by her husband after his conversion.</span></span></span></span>









      Catherine herself suffered from ill health for some years. In delirium since 13 September 1510, she died at dawn on 15 September and was canonized by Pope Clement XII in 1737.</span></span></span></span>



      St Catherine’s famous revelations in connection with Purgatory are set forth in her two books, “Spiritual Dialogue” and “Treatise on Purgatory”. Considered outstanding works on Christian mysticism, these writings have also earned her the title, “Apostle of Purgatory”.</span></span></span></span>
