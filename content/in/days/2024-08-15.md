    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Luke 1:39-56"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Every individual of the human race, male and female, is made in the image and likeness of God. (Genesis 1:26). We can celebrate Our Lady’s Assumption in light of this basic principle from the creation story. Mostly we are tempted to reverse this process and try to shape God in our own image and likeness. Naturally we have a limited concept of our Creator. How we cling to the notion of a docile God, who will grant our wishes if only we ask him in the right way; or an indulgent God who will turn a blind eye on the double standards in our lives; or a vengeful God, whom we obey only out of fear. To test the quality of our faith we might wonder, Do my ideas resemble Mary’s vision of God, especially as in today’s gospel when she says, “My soul glorifies the Lord, and my spirit rejoices in God my Saviour.”

      Mary combines sincere worship of God with deep joy in his presence, in the spirit of the advice given to the early Christians, “Rejoice always. Pray without ceasing” (1 Thess 5:17). In her Magnificat, Mary is enraptured by God, she does not ask anything for herself, she is not worried about the future; she thinks only of the goodness of God up to the present moment, and of the gratitude she owes to God. In her we have the supreme example of someone totally captivated by the love of God. In this sense, she is the fairest of all God’s creatures.



      First of all, she is the handmaid, the willing and joyful servant of the Lord. She lived solely for God and therefore God, who cannot be outdone in generosity, gave her the fullness of grace. Now just as Jesus did not abandon us when he ascended to heaven, so Mary has not been separated from the Christian community by her Assumption, but remains for each of us a sign of hope. We are called, like her, to share in the fullness of Christ’s glory. She is the model and guarantee of all that the believer hopes to become in heaven. We pray today that we too may be found worthy to come to that place in heaven which God has prepared for those who love him.

      The Assumption of the Blessed Virgin Mary



      Lack of explicit mention of the privilege of the Assumption as such in the NT notwithstanding, tradition and sound theological reasoning indicate its implicit revelation in the scriptures. St Germanus of Constantinople (d. 733) wrote that Mary’s virginal body is an altogether holy and chaste dwelling for God and can never know dissolution into dust.

      “Mary conceived without detriment to her virginal modesty, brought forth her Son without pain, passed hence without decay, according to the word of the angel, or rather God speaking by the angel, that she might be shown to be full, not half-full, of grace,” wrote Pope Alexander III (1159-1181). In 1568, Pope Pius V made the feast of the Assumption a holy day for the entire Church.

      Since Christ, the Way, the Truth and the Life himself has said, “Where I am, there shall my servant be also” (Jn 12:26), how can his own Mother not share his dwelling place? Consequently, it was only after he had sought and obtained from the world’s bishops a formal assessment as regards the sentiments of the people in each diocese that Pope Pius XII defined the dogma of the Assumption as a doctrine of faith in his Encyclical, Munificentissimus Deus, on 1 November 1950. Rightly then, on this feast, the greatest of Mary’s feasts, the Church Universal with one voice joins Mary in exulting: “Holy His Name!”



      <span class="subhead">Reflection</span>*: “The present life is for eternity and our happiness is proportionate to our goodness on earth. And Mary our mother will help us to lead a good and holy life” (Blessed James Alberione).*St Tarcisius(c. 3rd/4th cent)



      In the late 3rd or early 4th century, the Christians met daily to celebrate the Eucharist and to send, though discreetly, sufficient portions of the Sacred Bread to the Christians on the death row. This particular day, when the celebrant, Dionysius, turned around from the altar to see who could be considered the safest bearer of the Bread of Life, just consecrated, to the condemned Christians, a 12-year-old boy named Tarcisius stepped forward and knelt at his feet.

      “You are too young, my child,” said the kindly priest, filled as he was with both admiration for the lad and fear for the safe success of the mission involved, given the hostile atmosphere prevalent. “Your Reverence, my youth will itself be my protection. Oh please! Deny me not this rare privilege!” he entreated the Celebrant, his plea full of irresistible fervour and courage.

      Carefully wrapping the sacred species in linen and placing them on Tarcisius’ palms, Dionysius beseeched him to be ever conscious of the great treasure entrusted to his care. “I will die rather than betray God’s Sacred Gifts!” Tarcisius promised

      Tarcisius encountered a group of boys playing in the open who unsuccessfully tried to drag him into their game. He, however, resisted with all his might, until heavy blows felled him to the ground, bleeding, his arms crossed tightly over the Sacred Substance. Just as the mob made a grab fo his holy Treasure, they felt themselves pushed off violently by some giant force. As Quadratus knelt and raised the bruised and dying boy in his arms Tarcisius said, “Never mind me. Just take care of the Divine Mysteries I am carrying,” before breathing his last.
