    ref: "John 1:35-42"

    body: |
      John 1:35-42 gives us a picture of how Jesus conducted his first apprentice interview for the job of fishing for people. Andrew is his first candidate. And the interview goes like this: “What are you looking for?” Andrew answers, “Teacher, where are you staying?” Jesus answers: “Come and see.” Andrew went and saw where Jesus was staying, and remained with him that day and for the rest of his life. Then it was his job also to start ‘fishing for people’. The first one he tried was his own brother Peter and he set before us an example of evangelization. Without Andrew, there wouldn’t have been a Peter.



      Often people today think that telling others about Jesus is the last thing we can do as Christians. With Andrew it was the most natural thing he could do. If you’ve encountered Jesus and been touched by Him, then tell somebody. If someone brought you to Jesus, you go and bring someone else to him yourself.



      Not everyone can be a Peter. We don’t all have Peter’s personality, his gifts, his flare, but everyone can be an Andrew. Everyone can encounter Jesus. Everyone can accept His invitation to follow. Every one of us can bring people to Jesus.



      (Fr John Pichappilly, Kindle Your Spirit, ST PAULS, Mumbai)
  - key: reflection
    title: Reflection
    ref: null
    audio: null
    body: |
      The two call narratives in the readings of this Sunday invite us to reflect on our response to God who invites us to be his followers. Young Samuel recognizes the voice of God at the advice of Eli, the priest. John the Baptist told his two disciples about Jesus and they were interested in following him. Jesus asks them a profound and challenging question inviting them to look deeply into themselves to evaluate the direction and meaning of life. The two disciples did not perceive the full implication of the question because they replied with another question. Instead of giving them answer, Jesus replies with an invitation to “come and see”. It is an invitation to a relationship and to become part of his life and mission. The experience of being with Jesus transforms them that they become evangelizers to proclaim that they have found the Messiah. This Sunday the Lord wants to know why we are following him. When we answer this question honestly, it will purify our motivations and intensify our commitment to follow him. As Thomas Merton said, “Our idea of God tells us more about ourselves than about Him”.

      (1) Devasahayam Pillai

      Popularly known as Devasahayam Pillai (God’s help), he was born Nilakandan Pillai (1712-1752) into a Hindu family in the then Kingdom of Travancore (in the present district of Kanyakumari, in Tamil Nadu) southern India.

      Pillai, an upper-caste Hindu nair convert to Christianity, was a member of the royal service and was close to King Marthanda Varma, then-ruler of Travancore. He was very much loved by the king and was a good man who was faithful to his duty. However, after several bad harvests, he lost his goods. This devastated him and who wondered: “Who will respect me now that I’m poor?”

      Nilakandan shared his worries with a devout Catholic, a Dutch official by the name of Benedictus Eustachio De Lannoy who explained to him the meaning of suffering in the light of the Book of Job. For Nilakandan, Job’s example and his trust in God was decisive. Convinced of the truth of the Christian mysteries, Nilakandan asked for Baptism which he received in 1745 from the hands of Father Giovanni Battista Buttari, a Jesuit missionary, after nine months of preparation. He took the name Devasahayam, the Tamil translation of the biblical name Lazarus, which means “God has helped.”

      On the day of his Baptism, Devasahayam dedicated himself solemnly to Christ: “No one [forced] me to come; I came by my own free will. I know my heart: He is my God. I have decided to follow Him and will do so my whole life.” His life was no longer the same; Devasahayam dedicated himself to the proclamation of the Gospel for four years.

      However, the heads of his native religion did not look kindly on his conversion to Christianity. He was threatened, beaten, mistreated, imprisoned and tortured uninterruptedly for three years. Despite his sufferings, Devasahayam remained firm in his faith. His wife, Bhargavi Ammal too became Catholic and she took the name Gnanapoo Ammal (Theresa). Their conversion antagonized upper-caste Hindus and King commanded Pillai to reconvert to Hinduism but he refused.

      Noticing that his example caused many to do the same, the king ordered his arrest in 1749, charging him with treason and espionage. He was imprisoned, tortured and finally banished to the Aralvaimozhy forest, a remote border area of Travancore. According to Church documents, en route to the forest, he was beaten daily, pepper was rubbed in his wounds and nostrils and he was exposed to the sun and given only stagnant water to drink.

      Finally, he was shot dead in the Aralvaimozhy forest on January 14, 1752.

      Very soon Devasahayam was venerated in the region. His beatification was already desired in 1756, but it was only in 1993 that his cause was opened canonically in the diocese.

      He was beatified on 2 December 2012 in the Roman Catholic diocese of Kottar, southern India.

      Pillai’s tomb at St. Francis Xavier Cathedral in Nagercoil attracts large numbers of devotees. His life teaches how to witness our faith. While undergoing severe persecution, he never complained or was deterred. The spirit of evangelism was strong in him.

      Pope Francis canonized Blessed Devasahayam Pillai on 15 May 2022 at St Peter's Basilica together with six other blesseds.



      (2) Saint Felix of Nola - Priest (-260)

      A native of Nola (Italy), Felix, born of a Syrian father named Hermias, an ex-army man who had settled down in a Roman Colony in Campania, having resolved to serve Jesus Christ, the King of kings, distributed most of his possessions among the poor. In time he was ordained a priest by Maximus, Bishop of Nola, who, charmed with his virtue and prudence, made Felix his right hand man and counted him as his destined successor.

      In 250, Bishop Maximus was forced to retire into the desert, following the persecution begun by Emperor Decius. Felix, who ran errands in the absence of Maximus, was seized and scourged before being cast into a dungeon where, one night, an angel appeared, bidding him go to the aid of Maximus, who was in great distress. His chains having miraculously fallen off and the door of the dungeon standing open, he was led by the angel to where Maximus lay in hunger, cold, speechless and unconscious. Such was his state that Felix could do nothing for him and so he had recourse to prayer, his only handy aid. While at prayer, he found before him a bunch of grapes, some of which he squeezed into the Bishop’s mouth. Maximus regained consciousness and as desired by him, was carried by Felix on his shoulders to the church in the city.

      Felix remained under cover until the death of Decius, but when he reappeared people had difficulty recognizing him despite his zeal, even as his persecutors began to hunt after him, thus compelling him to remain for six months between the ruins of two houses where not even sufficient water was available. Peace having been restored, he re-emerged in public and started to till his own land which was left to him after the confiscation of his property by his persecutors.

      When Maximus died people persuaded Felix to the chair, but he declined and Quintinus was elected successor. Felix died at a very old age and Saint Paulinus of Nola erected a basilica in his honour. Invoked in finding lost articles apart from being the avenger of perjury, many miracles are attributed to his intercession.

      Reflection: *“Not only does the shedding of blood constitute martyrdom, but the perfect service of a soul devoted to the Lord is a daily martyrdom”* (Saint Jerome).
