    body: |
      Reflection: 1



      If we are honest in what we say, it is enough to say Yes for Yes and No for No, and there is no need of taking any oath. Integrity of the person is even more valuable than a solemn oath. Taking an oath to convince others against the truth is a great evil. An honest person’s word is more precious than a crooked person’s oath. Jesus asks all of us to be honest and trustworthy. Taking an oath is an effort to justify oneself before others when one is not certain about being just oneself. Let us learn to be simple, plain and truthful in our speech on all occasions. In his letter, St James speaks very strongly about the necessity of taming the tongue (Jams 3:1-12). </span></span></span></span>



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP</span></span>*Reflection: 2



      Jesus outlines a new moral code for his followers in his Sermon on the Mount, different from, and superior to, the Mosaic moral code. According to the teachings of Jewish rabbis, the world stands fast on truth, justice and peace; hence, liars, slanderers, scoffers and hypocrites will not enter Heaven. The rabbis classified two types of oaths as offensive to God:



      1) frivolous oaths using God’s name to support a false statement, because this violates the second commandment.



      2) evasive oaths using words like *Heaven,Jerusalem*, *my head*, because God is everywhere, and He owns everything.



      Jesus’ teaching: Be righteous; be men and women of integrity and character. If one is honest in one’s words and deeds, there is no need for one to support one’s statements and transactions with oaths or swearing. “*How forceful are honest words”!(Job 6:25).* An oath is a solemn invocation of God (“*So help me, God!”*) to bear witness to the truth of what one asserts to be the case or to the sincerity of one’s undertakings in regard to future actions. It is necessary and admissible to ask God’s help in the discharge of an important social duty (e.g., President’s oath of office) or while bearing witness in a court of law (“I will tell the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth, so help me, God”). In other cases, Jesus teaches, “Say ‘Yes,’ when you mean ‘Yes.’ and say ‘No,’ when you mean ‘No,’ (*Mt* 5:37). That is, He invites us to live in truth in every instance and to conform our thinking, our words and our deeds to the truth.



      <span class="subhead">Life messages: </span>



      1) *Let us be true *to God, to ourselves and to others.



      2) Let us allow God’s word of truth to penetrate our minds and heart and to form our conscience.

    body: |
      This section of the Sermon on the Mount calls for Christians to be honest and trustworthy. They must be people of Yes or No, and not Yes and No, that is, not unconvincing and wavering. To say Yes or No means to be persons of integrity and honesty, steeped in strong values and convictions, and above any kind of reproach. In other words, a Christian is to be a person of his word. When, on the other hand, a person swears by someone or something, he shows that he is uncertain of the truthfulness of what he says. People often resort to oaths to conceal a lie. Truthfulness seems to be such a rarity that we think we must end our statements with some sort of vow or oath to emphasize its credibility. “A man should never be ashamed to own he has been in the wrong, which is but saying in other words, that he is wiser today than he was yesterday,” says Alexander Pope.

    ref: "St Methodius I (Patriarch of Constantinople) (A,D.-847)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      A Sicilian by birth, Methodius received his preliminary education in his native town, Syracuse (Italy). He went to Constantinople (now Istanbul, Turkey), with the ambition of acquiring a post at court. Instead, he met a monk, influenced by whom, he renounced the world and joined the Monastery of Chenolakhos.



      During the second iconoclastic persecution under Leo the Armenian, in 815, he boldly stood his ground in favour of the veneration of the sacred images. However, after the deposition and exile of St Nicephorus, he went to Rome, apparently charged to inform Pope St Pascal I, of the state of affairs. There he remained till the death of Emperor Leo V.



      In 821 he returned to Constantinople with a letter from the Pope to the Emperor, Michael the Stammerer, requesting him to reinstate Nicephorus. But Michael, after reading the letter denounced Methodius as a stirrup of sedition and ordered him scourged and deposed. Thus he was persecuted by the iconoclasts who kept him confined in a tomb on the island of St Andrew, for seven years. Summoned to depose before Emperor Theophilus, Methodius boldly asserted: “If an image is so worthless in your eyes, how is it that when you condemn the image of Jesus Christ you do not also condemn the veneration paid to the representations of yourself? Far from doing so, you are continually causing them to be multiplied!” Methodius died of dropsy in 847.
