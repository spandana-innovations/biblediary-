    ref: "Matthew 5:20–26"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Reflection: 1



      The righteousness of the teachers of the Law and the Pharisees was based on literal fulfillment of the Law without caring for its spirit or purpose. Unless we practise righteousness greater than theirs, we will have no admission to the kingdom of heaven. Murder is prohibited by Law, but insulting a brother or calling him a fool are also serious crimes that deserve serious punishment. In fact Jesus gives priority to living in peace and reconciliation with others, over offering gifts at the altar. First we must make peace with the brother who has something against us so that we can offer gifts to God with a peaceful conscience and in fraternal love. Then our offering becomes pleasing and acceptable to God. </span></span></span></span>



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP</span></span>*Reflection: 2



      For the Scribes and the Pharisees, the external fulfilment of the precepts of the Mosaic Law was the guarantee of a person’s salvation. In other words, a man saved himself through the external works of the Law. Jesus rejects this view in today’s Gospel passage, taken from the Sermon on the Mount. For Jesus, justification or sanctification is a grace, a free, strengthening gift from God. Man’s role is one of cooperating with that grace by being faithful to it, using it as God means it to be used. Jesus then outlines new moral standards for his disciples.



      Control of anger: Anger is the rawest, strongest and most destructive of human emotions. Describing three stages of anger and the punishment each deserves, Jesus advises his disciples not to get angry in such a way that they sin.



      1) Anger in the heart (“brief stage of insanity” Cicero): It has two forms: a) a sudden, blazing flame of anger which dies suddenly. b) a surge of anger which boils inside and lingers, so that the heart seeks revenge and refuses to forgive or forget. Jesus prescribes trial and punishment by the Village Court of Elders as its punishment.



      2) Anger in speech: The use of words which are insulting (“*raka*“=“fool”), or damaging to the reputation (“*moros*” = a person of loose morals). Jesus says that such an angry (verbally abusive) person should be sent to the Sanhedrin, the Jewish religion’s Supreme Court, for trial and punishment.



      3) Anger in action: Sudden outbursts of uncontrollable anger, which often result in physical assault or abuse. Jesus says that such anger deserves hellfire as its punishment. In short, Jesus teaches that long-lasting anger is bad, contemptuous speech or destroying someone’s reputation is worse and harming another physically is the worst.



      <span class="subhead">Life messages: </span>



      1)Let us try to forgive,forget, and move toward reconciliation as soon as possible. St. Paul advises us “*Be angry* (righteous anger), *but do not sin”* (Eph 4: 26).



      2) When we keep anger in our mind, we are inviting physical illnesses like hypertension and mental illnesses like depression. Let us relax, keep silence when we are angry, and pray for God’s strength for self-control.

    title: Reflection
    ref: null

    body: |
      The Gospel passage of today challenges us to go beyond the spirituality and social justice practised by the religious leaders of Jesus’ time. A follower of Christ always has God as his point of reference. In a given situation what would God do or how would God react? As a loving Father full of compassion, slow to anger and abounding in mercy, God would never harbour anger, hatred or revenge against anyone. As His children, when we come to worship and offer gifts to Him, we should have the same mind and heart, free from all anger, hatred, feelings of revenge and jealousy against God’s children. God always forgives our sins, no matter how grave they are. We imitate him in reaching out to our brothers and sisters with a forgiving heart and thus, as Saint Paul teaches in the First Reading, be “transformed into His image from one degree of glory to another.”

    audio: null
    body: |
      St John has been named after his birth-place Sahagun (or St Fagondez) in northern Spain. A bright, attractive and high spirited lad, Juan Gonzalez Catrillo, as he was baptized, soon came to the attention of the Bishop of Burgos, who had him educated in his own household. Ordained priest at 26, he was made a Canon at the cathedral, and then completed four years of study in theology at the great University of Salamanca. However, in 1463, on recovering from surgery, he decided to join the Hermits of St Augustine in that city, and soon rose to be Master of Novices and eventually Prior.



      John had a particularly deep devotion to the Blessed Sacrament and was often privileged during Mass to behold the Sacred Host resplendent with divine glory. Daily, after rising for Matins, he would remain in the choir for adoration and prayer until it was time to celebrate Mass. A powerful and utterly fearless preacher, he inveighed relentlessly against impurity and vice in high places and the extravagances of feminine apparel. Assassins who had been hired by his enemies, seemed to become disarmed by his angelic sweetness and serenity, and hardened sinners found their innermost consciences revealed to his gaze. Many an enmity and family feud was settled through his intervention.



      St John Sahagun passed away on 11 June 1479 as a result of poisoning. He was beatified in 1609 and canonized in 1690.
