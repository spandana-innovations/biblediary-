      Reflection: 1



      The entire chapter 6 of John’s Gospel is centred on Jesus the Bread of life. In today’s text, by multiplying five loaves and two fish Jesus feeds a large crowd. He does not allow his disciples to lose any part of the bread that is left over, indicating God’s universal salvific will to save all, not permitting anyone to be lost. Twelve baskets of bread which the disciples gather up represent the new covenant community, the new Israel founded on the twelve Apostles. But the people who are crowded around Jesus do not understand the significance of this miracle. They take him for a political Messiah who will take care of their material needs. So they come to take him by force and make him their king. Knowing their mind, Jesus withdraws to the hills by himself. He has come for the salvation of all, and not to be their material provider; and he has no intention be possess any earthly power. </span></span>



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      Today’s Gospel describes one occasion when Jesus tried in vain to withdraw from the crowds at Capernaum. He travelled by boat to the other side of the Sea of Galilee to a remote village called Bethsaida Julius, where there was a small grassy plain. But when Jesus stepped ashore, He was faced with a large crowd of people. This was the scene of the miraculous feeding of the five thousand as described in today’s Gospel. This is the only miracle, other than the Resurrection, that is told in all four Gospels, a fact that speaks of its importance to the early Church. Today’s Gospel passage invites us to become humble instruments in God’s hands by sharing our blessings with our brothers and sisters. We may regard the incident in which Jesus multiplied loaves and fish in order to feed his hungry listeners, both as a miracle of Divine Providence and as a Messianic sign. The lesson for every Christian is that, no matter how impossible one’s assignment may seem, with Divine help it can be done: *“For with God nothing shall be impossible” (Lk 1:37).* Jesus used as his starting point for the miraculous meal a young boy’s generous gift of all the food he had, perhaps to remind us that love is the prime requirement for salvation, and selfishness blocks the life-giving action of the grace of God in us. The early Christian community especially cherished this story because they saw this event as anticipating the Eucharist.</span></span></span></span></span>



      <span class="subhead">Life message:</span></span></span> 1) As Christians we need to commit ourselves to share all we have and are, and to work with God in communicating His compassion to all. God is a caring Father, but He wants our co-operation. That’s what the early Christians did, generously sharing what they had with the needy. 2) We, and others in our time, need to ask for the courage to share, even when we think we have nothing to offer. Whatever we offer through Jesus will have a life-giving effect in those who receive it. 3) We are shown two attitudes in the Gospel story: that of Philip and that of Andrew (Jn 6:7-9). Philip said, in effect: “The situation is hopeless; nothing can be done.” But Andrew’s attitude was: “I’ll see what I can do; and I will trust Jesus to do the rest.” We need to have Andrew’s attitude*.*</span></span>

      The 11-year reign of Anicetus, 11th successor to the Petrine throne, coincided with the disturbance caused to the Roman Church by two major heresies, viz., “Gnosticism” (Valentine’s teaching that humankind is divided into three classes, viz., material men, i.e., those predestined to destruction, psychic men, who may attain salvation through redemption, and spiritual or perfect men who, like his followers, were destined for eternal life) and “Marcianism” (Marcian’s teaching that Jesus Christ was not the Son of Yahweh, the God mentioned in the Old Testament and that the God of the Jews was different from the God of the Christians).</span></span></span></span>



      Saint Polycarp, the aged Bishop of Smyrna, conferred with Anicetus on the long disputed question of a date for the celebration of Easter, for the Pope was anxious to bring uniformity in the entire Church. The Asiatic Church still followed its ancient tradition of the 14th day of the month of Nisan (regardless of whether this date fell on a weekday or a Sunday), because this was believed to have been the actual date on which Jesus arose from the dead. Of course, as Saint Irenaeus states, “neither could Anicetus persuade Polycarp, nor Polycarp, Anicetus”, the issue, known as the “paschal controversy”, thus remaining unsolved, the bonds of charity however staying intact. He was probably martyred under the persecution of Marcus Aurelius.</span></span></span></span>
