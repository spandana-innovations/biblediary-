      Reflection: 1



      The coming of the Greeks to meet Jesus symbolizes the coming of the hour of his suffering, death and resurrection. Jesus compares himself with a grain of wheat which can produce fruit only if it dies. Jesus was going to suffer and to die in order to save humanity. A new community will be born from his death. Anyone who wants to follow Jesus should also take the same path he took. The loving obedience of Jesus to the Father’s will is going to overthrow Satan, “the ruler of the world.” By his sacrificial death he is drawing all people to himself and offering them salvation. In this obedience Jesus glorifies the Father, and the Father’s voice confirms it. Like Philip and Andrew, every disciple of Jesus is called to bring others to meet Jesus. </span></span></span></span>



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      Today’s Gospel passage describes the painful indifference Jesus met in his audience and the jealous, hurtful comments Jesus heard when, as a carpenter-turned-Rabbi with a band of his own disciples, he started preaching in the synagogue of his hometown, Nazareth.A prophet without honour: The people of Nazareth literally jammed the synagogue, eager to see their familiar carpenter-turned-miracle-working preacher, Jesus, working miracles as he had done in neighbouring towns and villages. But they were jealous, incredulous, and critical, rather than believing, which prevented Jesus from doing miraculous healings. They were jealous of the extraordinary ability of a former carpenter without formal education in Mosaic Law to give a powerful and authoritative interpretation of their Holy Scriptures. A carpenter’s profession was considered low in social ranking. Besides, they could not accept a prophet coming from so low a family background as Jesus’ was, nor could they accept his “blasphemous” claim to be the promised Messiah. Jesus’ relatives, known to them, were equally unimportant people. But the most offensive thing he did, in their judgment, was to point out to them their own unbelief, citing examples of the famous prophets Elijah and Elisha favouring Faith-filled Gentiles over unbelieving Jews. Brothers and sisters of Jesus: “Ancient Hebrew, Aramaic and other languages had no special words for different degrees of relationship, such as are found in more modern languages. In general, all those belonging to the same family, clan, and even tribe, were *brethren*. Jesus had different kinds of relatives, in two groups–some on his mother’s side, others on St. Joseph’s. Matthew 13:55-56 mentions, as living in Nazareth, “*His brethren”* James, Joses, Simon and Judas*,* and elsewhere there is reference to Jesus’ *sisters* (cf. Matthew 6:3). But in Matthew 27:56 we are told that James and Joses were sons of a Mary distinct from the Blessed Virgin, and that Simon and Judas were not brothers of James, or St. Joseph’s children from a previous marriage. Jesus, on the other hand, was known to everyone as *the son of Mary* (Mark 6:3) or *the carpenter’s son* (Mt 13:55). The Church has always maintained as absolutely certain that Jesus had no brothers or sisters in the full meaning of the term: it is a dogma that Mary was Ever-Virgin” *(Navarre Bible Commentary).*</span></span></span></span></span>



      <span class="subhead">Life messages:</span></span></span> 1) Perhaps we have experienced the pain of rejection, betrayal, abandonment, violated trust, neglect, or abuse from our own friends and relatives. On such occasions, let us face rejection with prophetic courage and optimism. 2) Let us not, like the people in Jesus’ hometown, reject God in our personal lives. 3) Our country needs to hear God’s Truth from Spirit-filled Christians with the prophetic courage of their convictions. 4) Trusting Faith in the Divinity and goodness of Christ is essential, if Jesus is to work miracles in our personal lives. In addition, we need to be docile to the Holy Spirit living within us, so that He may work miracles in our lives. When we are challenged by the Gospel and by the Church, we should be thankful and should not allow the prophetic voice of the Church die in our hearts. </span></span></span></span></span>

      St John de Britto’s life reflects the missionary zeal and sacrificial love emphasized in Scripture. In 1 Corinthians 9:19-27, St Paul speaks of becoming “all things to all people” to win them for Christ. St John de Britto embodied this spirit, adopting the customs and lifestyle of the people he served in India, bridging cultural divides to proclaim the Gospel effectively. John 12:20-32 highlights Jesus’ teaching that a grain of wheat must die to bear much fruit. St John de Britto embraced this call to self-sacrifice, ultimately offering his life as a martyr for the faith. His legacy challenges us to adapt, persevere, and give generously of ourselves in service to others, trusting that our sacrifices, united with Christ, will bear lasting spiritual fruit.</span></span></span>
  - key: saint
    title: Saint of the Day
    saintName: "St John De Britto, Martyr (1647-1693)"
    ref: "St John De Britto, Martyr (1647-1693)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      When Don Pedro II of Portugal was young, he had among his little pages a modest lad named John de Britto, the son of rich and princely parents. It is stated that when John as a child fell grievously ill, his mother, whose family was connected with the court of Lisbon, invoked the aid of the great Jesuit Saint, Francis Xavier, and dedicated her son to him. In answer to her prayers, he recovered, and his mother vested him for a year in the garb worn in those days by the Jesuit Fathers. From then on, John’s heart burned to follow the example of the Apostle of the Indies, aspiring only to wear the habit of the great missionary and to devote his life to the conversion of the infidel. </span></span></span></span>



      He was admitted into the Society of Jesus at 15. His success in studies was so remarkable that great efforts were made after his ordination to keep him in Portugal, but grace triumphed, and in 1673 he set sail for Goa with sixteen of his fellow-Jesuits. The rest of his life, except for a brief interval, was spent amid incredible hardships and hindrances of all kinds in evangelizing Southern India. </span></span></span></span>



      Those who worked with him, in their letters to Europe, speak in glowing terms of his courage and devotion, of the extraordinary austerity of his life, and of the rich harvest of conversions which were the fruit of his labours.</span></span></span></span>



      Forced to return to Portugal in 1683, John once more broke through every obstacle, and returned to his labour of love in 1691. Like St John the Baptist, he was beheaded after a torturous imprisonment. “I await death,” he had written to his Superior, “and I await it with patience. It has always been the object of my prayers. It forms today the most precious reward of my labours and my sufferings.”</span></span></span></span>



      St John was canonized by Pope Pius XII in 1947.</span></span></span></span>
