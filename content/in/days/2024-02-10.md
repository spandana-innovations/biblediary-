  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Mark 8:1–10"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Today Saint Mark presents to us the second miracle of the loaves, which is similar in its form and content. The first miracle of the loaves (6:30-44) had five loaves and two fish which fed five thousand with a balance of twelve basketfuls while in this account we have seven loaves and a few fish shared by four thousand, with seven basketfuls to spare.



      Both these miracles prove that God is faithful in all situations. God’s nature is mercy and compassion and He continues to be compassionate to all people. Jesus felt sorry for the people for they had been with him for three days. Having felt compassion, he moves a step further to satisfy their hunger. He provided for them from what they had. Likewise, he expects us to be participants in his works of mercy and compassion, believing in the words of Saint Paul: “I can do everything with the help of God” (Phil 4:13).

      The miracle is a reminder that God cares for us and that we can trust Him. Following the example of the Lord who is merciful and compassionate, let us be compassionate and walk the extra mile to help the needy.



      (Fr Shinto Joseph SSP)

      We have probably read this passage on the great miracle of the multiplication many times over. However, what we ought to note is the love and compassion Jesus had for the people and His sensitivity to their needs, both material and spiritual. He provided them with food for the body and food for the soul! The unbelief and helplessness in the voices of the disciples must sound familiar to us because we do not often trust God enough with our lives as well as with the resources we have at our disposal. Does the sight of a hungry child or a lame man move me? Does news of famine, drought and natural disasters stir me into action? Lord Jesus, teach me to trust in your love for me.

    ref: "St Scholastica Virgin (c. 480-547)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      St Scholastica was the twin sister of St Benedict of Nursia (Italy). Dedicated to God from infancy, she chose, like her brother, to renounce her family’s wealth and status and to follow him to Subiaco. When he later on moved south to Monte Cassino, she founded a convent nearby for women who desired to live according to the Benedictine rule.

      Once a year only, just before Lent, brother and sister would meet to converse on spiritual matters in a small dependency of the abbey. St Gregory the Great, writing toward the end of the same century, has left us a touching account of their last annual reunion, when Scholastica, who felt her death to be near pleaded with her saintly brother to continue their conversation until daybreak. Benedict, however, saw no need for dispensing himself from his monastic rule, according to which he had to return to his own monastery by nightfall and thus would not consent. At this his sister implored God with urgency to grant her wish and she was heard — from the hitherto cloudless, serene sky there now broke forth a tremendous thunder and windstorm, with rain coming down in such torrents that Benedict was compelled to acknowledge God’s sign and stay. The law of love, as St Gregory puts it, had triumphed over the law of justice and discipline. So it was that the 2 Saints passed the night in spiritual edification, and in the morning returned to their respective abodes.

      St Scholastica is appealed to especially during storms.
