      Reflection: 1






      Whoever exalts himself will be humbled, and whoever humbles himself will be exalted. It is hard for the world to understand this lesson. How many people, in all walks of life, do all kinds of things for self-promotion, to go higher in rank, power and position, to become more popular, to be honoured in society! They expect others to speak highly of them and to greet them reverentially. But they themselves exploit these others, make them work hard and take unfair advantage of them. They teach good things but do not practise them.



      That is not the way the disciples of Christ should live. They are not to make any show of their importance, influence, power and pelf. Their deeds should not be a mere show, but done out of genuine goodness, humility, simplicity and sincerity. Hypocrisy has no place in the life of a follower of Jesus.



      *— Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      Three sins of the Scribes and Pharisees: Jesus raises three objections to the Pharisees: (1) “They do not practice what they teach” (v. 3). They lack integrity of life and fail to practice what they preach, namely, justice, mercy and charity. (2) They overburden the ordinary people (v. 4). The scribes and the Pharisees, in their excessive zeal for God’s laws, split the 613 laws of the Torah into thousands of rules and regulations affecting every movement of the people, thus making God’s laws a heavy burden. (3) “They do all their deeds to be seen by others” (v. 5). Jesus accuses the scribes and Pharisees of seeking the glory that rightly belongs to God. They express their love of honour in several ways, thereby converting Judaism into a religion of ostentation: (a) “They make their phylacteries broad and their fringes long” (v. 5). b) They “love to have the place of honour at banquets and the best seats in the synagogues” (v. 6). (c) They “love to be greeted with respect in the marketplaces and to have people call them rabbi” (v. 7).



      <span class="subhead">Life messages: </span>



      1) We need servant-leaders in a serving community: The Church is a servant-community in which those who hunger, and thirst are to be satisfied; the ignorant are to be taught; the homeless are to receive shelter; the sick is to be cared for; the distressed are to be consoled; and the oppressed are to be set free. Hence, leaders should have a spirit of humble service in thought, word and deed.



      2) We need to live the Faith we profess. Our Faith tells us that we are all brothers and sisters, children of the same Heavenly Father. Hence, we should always pray for each other. Instead of judging the poor, we should be serving them both directly and through our efforts on behalf of economic justice. Instead of criticizing those of other races, we should be serving them both directly and through our efforts on behalf of racial justice. Instead of ignoring the homeless, we should be serving them through efforts to supply them with adequate housing.



      3) We need to accept the responsibilities which go with our titles. Titles and polite forms exist to remind each of us of our specific responsibilities in society. Hence, let us use everything we are and have in a way that brings glory to God, by serving His children.

    title: Reflection

    body: |
      We see Jesus using very harsh language when speaking about the Scribes and Pharisees. He pointed out the gap between their teaching and practice. He also found that they were performing good deeds to gain popularity and admiration from the people around them. They were proud of their abilities and achievements. Jesus therefore warned his disciples not to imitate the Scribes and Pharisees although they could listen to what they told them. Jesus did not want his disciples to use the titles of ‘Father’, ‘Rabbi’ and ‘Instructor’. The title ‘Father’ is most fittingly used for the Heavenly Father. The title ‘Rabbi’ is most fittingly used for Jesus. The title ‘Instructor’ can be used for both the Holy Spirit and Jesus.

      St Cyril succeeded Maximus as Bishop of Jerusalem at 35, but nearly 17 out of the ensuing 36 years of his stormy episcopate he spent in exile for maintaining firm faith in the divinity of Christ — he was banned twice by Arian synods and once by the Emperor Valens.



      In 381, Cyril attended the Council of Constantinople and, for the first time, Jerusalem was recognized as a patriarchal See along with Rome, Alexandria, Antioch, and Constantinople.



      Cyril was a man of gentle nature and a peacemaker. It was he who fostered the development of the “holy city” of Jerusalem as a pilgrimage centre for all Christendom. A pillar of orthodoxy and a great teacher of the early Christian era, his primary surviving work is the “Catecheses”, a collection of 23 catechetical lectures to candidates for Baptism — the first 18, based on the Jerusalem baptismal creed, given during Lent, the concluding 5 instructing the newly baptized during the week after Easter. His Eucharistic theology, an advance on that of earlier writers, interprets the Lord’s presence in the terms of the later dogma of transubstantiation, the rite pronounced in sacrificial language.



      Thought to have died in 387, St Cyril was declared a Doctor of the Church in 1882.
