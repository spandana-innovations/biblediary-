    body: |
      Reflection: 1



      Jesus calls Levi the tax collector who is at work, to be his disciple, and he promptly leaves everything and follows him. He is so happy that in honour of his new Master he throws a party in his house. Jesus sits with Levi and other tax collectors and sinners. The self-righteous scribes and Pharisees who were also there object to this. Jesus associating with such people was against their custom. Jesus silences them by saying that he has come not call the righteous but sinners because it is not the healthy people but the sick that need the physician. He has come as the Messiah for forgive, to heal and to establish a society where there would be no discrimination. For him Levi is not someone to be excluded but to be welcomed as a valuable disciple. If we acknowledge our sins and sicknesses, Jesus will heal us and we can follow him faithfully.



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      Today’s Gospel episode, telling of Matthew’s call as Jesus’ Apostle, reminds us of God’s love and mercy for sinners and challenges us to practice this same love and mercy in our relations with others.



      The call and the response: Jesus went to the tax collector’s station to invite Matthew to become his disciple. Since tax collectors worked for a foreign power and extorted more tax money from the people than the area owed, they were hated and despised as traitors by the Jewish people and considered public sinners by the Pharisees. Jesus could see in Matthew a person who needed Divine love and grace. While everyone hated Matthew, Jesus was ready to offer him undeserved love, mercy, and forgiveness. Hence, Matthew abandoned his lucrative job, because for Matthew, Christ’s call to follow Him was a promise of salvation, fellowship, guidance, and protection.



      Scandalous partying with sinners. It was altogether natural for Matthew to celebrate his new calling by holding a feast for his friends. But Jesus’ dining with outcasts in the house of a “traitor” scandalized the Pharisees for whom ritual purity and table fellowship were important religious practices. But instead of asking Jesus directly, they asked the disciples, “*Why does your master eat with tax collectors and sinners*?” In answer to their question, Jesus stressed his ministry as healer: *“Those who are well do not need a physician; the sick do.”* Then, in Matthew’s own account of his conversion, Jesus challenged the Pharisees, quoting Hosea, “*Go and learn the meaning of the words, ‘I desire mercy, not sacrifice.’ (Hosea 6:6).”* Finally, Jesus clarified his position, *“I did not come to call the righteous, but sinners.”*



      <span class="subhead">Life messages:</span> 1) Jesus calls you and me for a purpose: Jesus has called us through our Baptism, forgiven our sins, and welcomed us as members of the Kingdom. In fact, He calls us daily through the Word and through His Church, to be His disciples, and to turn away from all the things that distract us and draw us away from God. Just as Jesus did for us and for Matthew, we are to reach out to the unwanted and the marginalized in society with God’s own love, mercy and compassion.

  - key: reflection
    title: Reflection
    ref: null

    body: |
      In today’s Gospel text, Jesus calls Levi, the tax collector, to be His disciple. In Jewish society, tax collectors were generally despised by their fellow Jews, for they used to collect taxes on behalf of the Roman Empire that occupied Israel. In their turn, they oppressed the taxpayers in order to secure as much profit as possible for themselves and were thus corrupt. This exploitation led to widespread contempt. Again, because of their association with Gentiles, they were considered ritually unclean and were often excluded from religious practices and looked down upon by the religious authorities. The call of Levi is a radical act of Jesus, who does not see him as a sinner but as a potential disciple of the kingdom of God, thus communicating God’s forgiveness, outreach and inclusion. Do I practise the logic of exclusion or inclusion in my relationships with others?

    audio: null
    body: |
      Born and raised in southern Egypt by pious Christian parents, Anthony was orphaned at eighteen. Called to the eremitical life, he sold his inheritance for the benefit of the poor, and retired to a deserted tomb for fifteen years, and then for another twenty years shutting himself off in an abandoned fort on a mountainside near Der el Memum, refusing to see anyone.



      The number of his disciples nevertheless increased continually, settling down in nearby caves and huts and, in 305, they succeeded in persuading Anthony to become their spiritual guide. The saint emerged, not emaciated as expected after his rigorous fasts and vigils but vigorous and healthy.



      For five years Anthony devoted himself completely to the organization and spiritual instruction of his disciples. In 311 Anthony went to Alexandria to encourage the faithful who were smarting under the Maximinan persecution, before retiring to a mountainside in the desert of Clysma, between the Nile and the Red Sea where there stands to this day the monastery named after him “Der Mar Antonios”. Here he spent the remaining 45 years of his life, advising and inspiring all who came to him, and making frequent visits to his first foundation at Der el Memum. About 335 he went once more to Alexandria and preached against the errors of the Arians and Manichaeans.



      Anthony shared a deep bond with Saint Athanasius who has left us an authentic life of the saintly founder of Christian monasticism, portraying him as highly dignified, full of divine peace and joy, and filled with marvellous zeal for God’s work. He died at the venerable old age of 105 and his intercession is sought especially in cases of epilepsy, pestilence, erysipelas (“Saint Anthony’s Fire”), and skin diseases.



      <span class="subhead">Reflection:</span>*“A religious should imitate the bee gathering honey among the blossoms of the meadows. From each of his companions, he should gather some good lesson—from one, modesty, from another silence, from a third patience, from a fourth resignation and indifference”* (Saint Anthony the Great).
