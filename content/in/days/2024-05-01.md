  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Matthew 13: 54-58"
    audio: null
    body: |
      During the earthly life of Jesus, people in general considered him the son of a workman, a carpenter. We hear people asking, “Is not this the carpenter’s son” (Mt 13:55)? As a woodworker, Joseph earned the daily bread for Blessed Mary, for Child Jesus and for himself. On this account he is the model of workers and the patron of all workers. He stands out as the ideal of workers, of earnest work and respectable livelihood. He also taught Jesus the art of carpentry, and the Lord, as a young man, used to help him in his workshop.



      Today’s memorial, instituted by Pope Pius XII in 1955, substitutes the previously existing feast of St Joseph, Patron of the Universal Church, which used to be celebrated by the Carmelites in the eighteenth century and was extended to the Roman Rite by Pope Pius IX.



      As the inauguration of the month of May, specially dedicated to our Blessed Mother, this feast of St Joseph the worker invites the faithful to Christianise the feast of work, and gives to the workers a patron in St Joseph, the “father” of Jesus who was known as the carpenter’s son (Mt 13:55). In St Joseph we have a marvellous example of work in the faithful exercise of artisanship. Like him, we too must fulfil faithfully the responsibilities God has entrusted

      to us.



      After creating the human being, God said to him, “Be fruitful and multiply; fill the earth and subdue it” (Gen 1:28). This involves, in fact, an enormous, unlimited task. In order to fulfil this command of God we have to use the created things and the creative power God has given us. Through our own efforts we have to develop and multiply our abilities and talents and put them to the best use, for our benefit and that of others. We know very well the parable of the talents. The one who did not multiply his talents was condemned. If we do not use God’s gifts, we shall lose them. God has created the Garden of Eden and given it to humans; it is up to humans to till it and cultivate it.



      Every form of work is a creative approach, and an opportunity to express and develop oneself. With God and under Him every good worker is creating something new. He or she is cooperating with God to renew the face of the earth. Whether we dig the field, cultivate grains, teach children, build roads, weave cloth, drive trains, run industry, carry our business or write books, we are creating; we are multiplying what is good, and thus being fruitful. When trials and difficulties come our way, if we remember these facts, our courage and hope will not fail.



      To work is a vocation given to all people. Every human activity must be according to the will and plan of God; it must be in harmony with the good of humankind. Work gives the worker

      an opportunity to fulfil his or her personal and communitarian vocation (cf. GS 35). All have to work, be they employees or employers. They must fulfil their duties with mutual respect and a sense of responsibility.



      We all are small creators appointed by God. All that we do spiritually, mentally and physically should be creative. Through our co-operation with God’s gifts, we must increase and multiply our intelligence, our health, our goodness and the things needed to fulfil our needs and those of others. That is the way to be God’s helpers in the great task of ongoing creation. By our work we shall be perfecting and sanctifying ourselves and contributing to the

      welfare of society, and thus honouring and imitating St Joseph in the best possible way.



      (Source: Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil SSP, Feast Day Homilies, ST PAULS Mumbai, India, Pages 133-135)

      When Pope Pius XII instituted the feast of Saint Joseph the Worker on 1 May 1955, he declared: “Our intention in doing so is to bring all men to recognize the dignity of labour.” The fact that both Saint Joseph and Jesus were carpenters is a testimony to the dignity of human labour. The Second Vatican Council stresses the greatness of human labour and teaches that men and women can look at their daily work as a prolongation of the work of the Creator God, a service to the community and fellowmen, a personal contribution to the fulfilment of God’s plan in history and a homage offered to God through which they are associated with the redemptive work of Jesus Christ. Pope Francis says, "We do not get dignity from power or money or culture. We get dignity from work."

      Saint Joseph the Worker (492)



      On 1 May 1955, during a public audience granted to the Catholic Association of Italian Workers, whose members had gathered that day in Saint Peter’s Square to celebrate the 10th anniversary of their society and to pledge anew their loyalty to the Social Programme of the Church, Pope Pius XII instituted the liturgical feast of Saint Joseph the Worker and assigned it to the 1st day of May, giving concrete expression to the special relationship between Saint Joseph and the working class. The Pope assured his audience and the “working people of the entire world”: “You have at your side a Shepherd, a defender and a Father.” As Saint Bernard of Clairvaux says, “Imagine his worth from his very name, which means, ‘increase’!”

      Pope Pius XII further said, “Our intention in doing so is to bring all men to recognize the dignity of labour. It is our hope, that this dignity may supply the motive for the formation of a social order and a body of law founded on the equitable distribution of rights and duties..... We are certain that you are indeed pleased, for the humble working man of Nazareth not only personifies before God and the Church the dignity of those who work with their hands, but he is also the constant guardian of yourselves and your families.”

      Holy Mother Church directs Christian workers to look up to St Joseph their Patron as model and example of Christian workmanship. Work per se was never meant to be a punishment; rather, it formed the plan of God. And, so, whether we make a chair or build a Cathedral, we are called upon to bear fruit with our hands, our hearts and minds for the ultimate purpose, viz., the building up of the Body of Christ.
