    audio: null
    body: |
      Reflection: 1



      St John concludes his Gospel stating that what he has written is an unfinished story. Nobody can write exhaustively the infinite mystery of the Word made flesh. We have to carry on this story by bearing witness to the inexhaustible riches of the mystery of Christ. John is witness to many things Jesus said and did, and he has written truthfully about some of them which he considers essential, and many other things he has not written, for there is so much that could be written. Not every word or deed of Jesus is recorded in the Gospel; the ministry of Jesus was much richer and wider than what is contained in the Gospel. It is an ever deepening story which will continue to unfold till the end of the world. </span></span></span></span>



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP</span></span>*Reflection: 2



      Today’s Gospel passage describes the role of Peter as the chief shepherd of Christ’s people and of John as a long-lived witness to Christ in the early Church. The last part of the passage was intended to correct the false notion in the early Church that John would not die until the much-expected, imminent “*second coming*” of Jesus.



      Jesus’ reply: Jesus’ response implies that what is important is not to be curious about what the future will bring but to serve the Lord faithfully each day, keeping to the way He has marked out for one.



      John’s testimony about his Gospel: The passage concludes with John’s testimony about the truth of the content of his Gospel. It also explains the purpose of John’s Gospel: to strengthen our Faith in what Jesus did and taught. In addition, it tells us that the written Gospels contain only a fraction of what Jesus taught and did, implying that we have to depend upon the Sacred Tradition of the early Church handed down to us by the early Fathers of the Church to complete the truth of the written testimony.



      Life message: 1) Just as Peter and John each had his unique role in Christ’s Church, so each believer, as a member of this Mystical Body of Christ, each of us with our different talents, strengths, weaknesses, and limits, has one’s own particular work to do in bringing the task of the Body – to bring the world to Christ and Christ to the world – to its completion. Hence it is our duty to bear witness to Christ by surrendering our lives to Christ on the altar of service for the people of God and by offering ourselves as humble instruments in the hands of Christ.

    body: |
      John the Evangelist concludes his Gospel with a rhetorical exaggeration technique to get an important point across to his audience. He wants to say that not all that he saw, witnessed and heard could be recorded in the gospel. They are far more, perhaps not in length, but in greatness and meaning, than what he could write in these few pages with his limited resources and finite understanding. The significance of each word and each deed that Jesus said or did could be described and explained infinitely. But the things that he recorded are for the benefit of his audience. In John 20:31, he makes a similar claim, “But these things have been written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God; and that by believing, you may have life in his name.” From what John says we can conclude that the story of Jesus is incomplete and it will never be completed. It will continue to be written down, spoken of, listened to and fascinate, inspire, enlighten, enliven and transform people until this world comes to an end.

    body: |
      Thomas à Kempis is so surnamed after his native town of Kempen near Cologne (Germany) where his father was metalsmith. His elder brother John had become one of the “Brethren of the Common Life”, a movement founded by Gerard Groote for the fervent revival of early Christian devotion, humility and simplicity in the love of God and neighbour.



      At 13, Thomas placed himself under Radewyn, the able successor of Groote. In 1413 he was ordained Priest, was twice elected sub-prior, and for 58 years devoted himself with enthusiasm to transcribing manuscripts, preaching, instructing the younger brethren, and to reading and meditating in strict poverty and retirement. His most celebrated work, “The Imitation of Christ”, was first published anonymously in 1418; he is also known as the author of numerous devout prayers and Latin hymns. “Commit yourself with full confidence to God! Leave to him the ‘I will’ and the ‘I won’t.’ Let there be but one heart and one mind for you and God.”
