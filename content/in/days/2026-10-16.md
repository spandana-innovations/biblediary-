  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Luke 12: 1-7"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Jesus continues his condemnation of the hypocrisy of the Scribes and the Pharisees, comparing it to leaven or yeast. The Jews considered yeast as something evil, corrupting the dough during the process of fermentation. That is why the Law given through Moses prescribes unleavened bread for offering to God. Jesus reminded the common people that the Pharisees were hypocrites who pretended to be holy, and that they corrupted people as the yeast corrupts the dough. The teaching and example of the scribes and the Pharisees influenced the crowd in a disastrous way, especially when the teachers failed to practice what they preached. Jesus also warned these religious leaders that their sins would be brought to light at the Last Judgment (CCC #678).</span></span></span></span></span>



      Hearing in secret and announcing in public: According to the *Navarre Bible Commentary*, most Palestinian houses had a roof in the form of a terrace. There people would meet to chat and while away their time in the hottest part of the day. Jesus pointed out to his disciples that in these get-togethers, things said in private became matters of public discussion. In the same way, despite the Pharisees’ and scribes’ efforts to hide their vices and defects under the veil of piety, all they had hidden would become a matter of common knowledge.</span></span></span></span></span>



      A reverential fear of God: Since nothing — not even the most insignificant thing — escapes God, no one should fear that any suffering or persecution he experiences in following Christ will remain unrewarded in eternity. But our fear of God should not be servile (based on fear of punishment). It should be a filial fear (the fear of a child who loves, and so does not want to displease, his or her father), a reverent awe nourished by our trust in Divine Providence.</span></span></span></span></span>



      <span class="subhead">Life messages:</span></span></span> 1) In contrast to the hypocrisy of the Pharisees, the followers of Jesus must display transparency in their Christian lives by practicing what they profess. 2) They should also maintain a reverential fear of God, adjusting their actions in such a way that they may not displease a loving heavenly Father.</span></span></span></span></span>

  - key: reflection
    title: Reflection
    ref: null
    audio: null
    body: |
      In today’s Gospel, Jesus warns His followers about two dangers: hypocrisy and fear. Hypocrisy, described as the “leaven of the Pharisees”, is shown in their pretence, ritual obsession, and self-righteous attitudes. Jesus reminds us that hypocrisy cannot stay hidden—it will eventually come to light. We are called to be genuine, sincere, and true in both faith and action. The second danger is fear. Jesus teaches us to fear only the one who can corrupt our soul, not those who can harm the body. God cares for us deeply, even knowing the number of hairs on our head, and treats us as His beloved children. Blessed Kunjachen, who trusted God in adversity, said, “When faced with trials, I trust in God completely, for He is my strength.” His life reminds us to rely on God’s strength, avoiding hypocrisy and fear through trust in Him.</span></span></span>

      St Margaret Mary Alacoque *Virgin (1647-1690)</span>*</span></span></span>



      With the rapid spread of Protestantism and Jansenism, England lost to the Faith and France ruled by the worldly Louis XIV, Catholicism had grown disastrously cold in the 17th century. To rekindle it, Christ chose an obscure French nun named Margaret Mary Alacoque, whom he expressly commanded to join the Visitation convent in 1671.</span></span></span>



      “My divine Heart is so inflamed with love for humankind, that it can no longer contain the flames of its burning charity.….. I ask that the Friday after the Octave of Corpus Christi be set aside as a special feast in honour of my Heart, that people communicate and make reparation to it by a solemn act, to make amends for the irreverence to which it is subjected while exposed on the altar. I promise you that my Heart will shed the gifts of its divine love in great abundance on all who will thus honour it and induce others to so honour it!” he said.</span></span></span>



      Our Lord also exhorted the faithful to receive him in Holy Communion on the first Friday of each month and to keep the Holy Hour on its eve before the Blessed Sacrament exposed. </span>It was only with the assistance of Blessed Claude de la Colombiere, Margaret’s new confessor, a Jesuit, that devotion to the Sacred Heart as the visible sign of Christ’s infinite and condescending love, received due recognition within her order and came to be established in the Church.</span></span></span>



      She was canonized in 1920.</span></span></span>

      St Hedwig *Widow (1124-1243)</span>*</span></span></span>



      Born in Bavaria, Germany, Hedwig was at 12 given in marriage to Henry I, who became Duke of Silesia (now Poland) in 1202. As Duchess, all her thoughts were directed to pleasing God and to sanctifying her own soul as well as her household and all she came in contact with. A woman of deep piety and gentleness, she wielded tremendous influence in the government, ensuring peace amongst the nobles, displaying much prudence and fortitude. She founded new monasteries, supported old ones, even introducing the mendicant orders such as the Franciscans and the Dominicans. Encouraged by Hedwig, Henry founded the first convent for women religious in Silesia, viz., the Cistercians at Trebnitz, in 1202. After the birth of their 7th child in 1208, the saintly couple avowed themselves to a life of continence in the presence of the Bishop of Wroclaw.</span></span></span>



      The death of her husband, 30 years later, saw Hedwig retire to Trebnitz in the Cistercian habit. She refrained, however, from becoming a nun as such precisely so that she might retain her right over her wealth for the purpose of aiding the poor and the needy. Indeed, she allowed no one who came to her for help to go away uncomforted, be it a widow or an orphan, a student or a religious, a leper or a weary traveller, or a needy woman nursing her offspring. Even those in chains or in prison benefited from her largesse. </span></span></span>



      Noted for her miraculous powers, Hedwig was canonized in 1267.</span></span></span>
