  - key: homily

    ref: "Luke 11:42–46"

    body: |
      In the Gospel, we see the divine wisdom of Jesus. He can see through the protective covering of the gimmicks we use to convince ourselves that we’re great stuff and everything is fine. The Pharisees had their fine little religious rituals, but theirs was a self religion. It was not a matter of listening to God. It was listening to oneself, glorifying a smug, narrow, formalistic fussing. God’s great demands for a deep charity, a deep consciousness of our neighbour’s needs – that was over their heads. They “neglected justice and the love of God .” But lest we misunderstand, Jesus clarified: “These greater things you should have done, and not leave the smaller ones undone.” Again the Lord shows His balance of mind, His honesty and His integrity. He is not the kind of radical who emphasizes one need at the expense of others. Jesus is indeed radical in the sense that He demands all; that He is never satisfied with halftruths or with a half-hearted religion. He demands a response which is full, open, unhindered by narrowness. </span></span>



      *(Pray Today’s Gospel, Bernard C. Mischke O.S.C. and Fritz Mischke O.S.C.)*</span></span>

      Jesus’ woes to the Pharisees and Scribes are ways of condemning four types of religious behaviours that are not in line with the spirit of Jesus’ teaching: *a) Misplaced emphasis:* There are essentials and accidentals in our life. Religious authorities were emphasizing the accidentals more than the essentials of spiritual life. *b) Attachment to power and respect* than desire to serve: the pharisees sought places of honour and respect from people. c) *Hypocritical behaviour at the cost of integrity of life*: Jesus compares them with white-washed tombs which appear so beautiful but are filled with all sorts of filth. *d) Lip service rather than service mindedness:* Religious authorities burdened people with lots of rules and regulations. They hardly did any service to lighten their burdens. *For which of our behaviour will the Lord condemn us?* </span></span></span></span>

      St Margaret Mary Alacoque </span>*Virgin (1647-1690)</span>*</span></span>








       W</span></span></span>ith the rapid spread of Protestantism and Jansenism, England lost to the Faith and France ruled by the worldly Louis XIV, Catholicism had grown disastrously cold in the 17th century. To rekindle it, Christ chose an obscure French nun named Margaret Mary Alacoque, whom he expressly commanded to join the Visitation convent in 1671.</span></span></span>









      “My divine Heart is so inflamed with love for humankind, that it can no longer contain the flames of its burning charity.….. I ask that the Friday after the Octave of Corpus Christi be set aside as a special feast in honour of my Heart, that people communicate and make reparation to it by a solemn act, to make amends for the irreverence to which it is subjected while exposed on the altar. I promise you that my Heart will shed the gifts of its divine love in great abundance on all who will thus honour it and induce others to so honour it!” he said.</span></span></span>



      Our Lord also exhorted the faithful to receive him in Holy Communion on the first Friday of each month and to keep the Holy Hour on its eve before the Blessed Sacrament exposed. </span>It was only with the assistance of Blessed Claude de la Colombiere, Margaret’s new confessor, a Jesuit, that devotion to the Sacred Heart as the visible sign of Christ’s infinite and condescending love, received due recognition within her order and came to be established in the Church.</span></span></span>



      She was canonized in 1920.</span></span></span>

      St Hedwig </span>*Widow (1124-1243)</span>*</span></span>








       B</span></span></span>orn in Bavaria, Germany, Hedwig was at 12 given in marriage to Henry I, who became Duke of Silesia (now Poland) in 1202. As Duchess, all her thoughts were directed to pleasing God and to sanctifying her own soul as well as her household and all she came in contact with. A woman of deep piety and gentleness, she wielded tremendous influence in the government, ensuring peace amongst the nobles, displaying much prudence and fortitude. She founded new monasteries, supported old ones, even introducing the mendicant orders such as the Franciscans and the Dominicans. Encouraged by Hedwig, Henry founded the first convent for women religious in Silesia, viz., the Cistercians at Trebnitz, in 1202. After the birth of their 7th child in 1208, the saintly couple avowed themselves to a life of continence in the presence of the Bishop of Wroclaw.</span></span></span>









      The death of her husband, 30 years later, saw Hedwig retire to Trebnitz in the Cistercian habit. She refrained, however, from becoming a nun as such precisely so that she might retain her right over her wealth for the purpose of aiding the poor and the needy. Indeed, she allowed no one who came to her for help to go away uncomforted, be it a widow or an orphan, a student or a religious, a leper or a weary traveller, or a needy woman nursing her offspring. Even those in chains or in prison benefited from her largesse. </span></span></span>



      Noted for her miraculous powers, Hedwig was canonized in 1267.</span></span></span>
