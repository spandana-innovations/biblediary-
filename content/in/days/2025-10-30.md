      Reflection: 1



      In his reply to the Pharisees who warned him about Herod’s plan to kill him, Jesus shows that he is not afraid of anyone who holds earthly power. He moves on to Jerusalem in obedience to the will of the Father. It is in Jerusalem that Jesus is going to offer his ultimate sacrifice on the Cross. Jerusalem has a history of killing the prophets and God’s messengers. The people of Jerusalem did not respond to God’s loving concern and care for them. They would not be able to escape the consequences of this. In saying this, Jesus is also exhorting all his listeners to respond to him and repent. </span></span></span></span>



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      Some Pharisees warned Jesus that he was in imminent danger of arrest by Herod. They might have been either his friends among the Pharisees or Jesus’ enemies who wanted him to leave their territory. Jesus’ reaction: Jesus called Herod a fox – a cunning, fearful, and dangerous animal. But with prophetic courage, Jesus was determined to do the Messianic work entrusted to him by God his Father. Hence, Jesus sent the message to Herod that he would continue with his preaching and healing ministry. Prophesying his death in Jerusalem, Jesus expressed his love and longing for the Holy City of Jerusalem, using the image of a mother hen gathering all her chicks under her protective wings. Across the valley from the city of Jerusalem there is a church called *Dominus flevit*, which means, “The Lord wept.” On the base of the altar of that Church, there is a small mosaic showing a mother hen with her chicks. They are under her wings for protection, some of them peering out in the way that chicks do. “The image of being protected by wings, which occurs often in the Old Testament, refers to God’s love and protection of his people. It is to be found in the prophets, in the canticles of Moses (cf. Dt 32:11), and in many Psalms” (cf. 17:8; 36:8; 57:2; 61:5; 63:8). That mosaic is the representation of today’s Gospel text expressing God’s warm and protective maternal love. Jesus loved Jerusalem and its inhabitants. Hence, he felt deep sorrow at its lack of response to his message, a lack which would continue when he preached there in the last week of his earthly life. Finally, he said to those who had warned him, *“Behold, your House is forsaken. And I tell you, you will not see me until you say*, *‘Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord’” (Luke 13:35)*– either at Jesus’ triumphal entry into Jerusalem on Palm Sunday or at his final coming as Judge and Lord of all.



      Life messages:



      1) As Christians we should have the courage of our religious convictions, and the good will to practice them.



      2) We need to be aware of the dire consequences of rejecting God’s graces and the chances He gives us every day to reform our lives.

      The Pharisees warn Jesus to go away from Jerusalem because Herod wanted to kill him! Since Jesus stood for truth, justice and all that was right, he was surely a threat – not only to the political regime of his time (like Herod) but also to the religious authorities like the Pharisees and the Scribes. Jesus always took sides: but he was never with the oppressors the exploiters and those who put heavy burdens on others. So, in response to the warning that the Pharisees give him, he bluntly tells them to tell Herod the fox that he will continue doing good. He laments that Jerusalem has killed the prophets and stoned the messengers. He is not afraid of meeting the same fate!
  - key: saint

    saintName: "St Gerard Majella Confessor (1726 -1755)"
    ref: "St Gerard Majella Confessor (1726 -1755)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Extremely devout, Gerard, the son of poor parents in southern Italy, was blessed with a gift of profound contemplation and an ardent love of the Blessed Sacrament. “Suffer only for God, and your very sufferings will bring you heaven on earth!” Admitted by the Redemptorists on probation as a lay brother at 23, Gerard, deeply rooted in humility and purity, proved a model of perfect obedience. To the customary three vows he added one to do always that which appeared to be the most perfect — “Everything that is done for God becomes a prayer!”



      God granted him remarkable spiritual gifts: ecstasies, levitations, bi-locations, invisibility, prophecy, infused knowledge, discernment of spirits, and even power over the forces of nature and Satan himself. His obedience to his superiors was such that he knew and carried out their wishes even if unspoken or given at a great distance. He subjected his body to severe penances and rigorous fasts. Every spare moment was spent in adoration of the Blessed Sacrament, and at times, when passing the tabernacle, he had to do himself violence: “O Lord, let me go, I beseech thee! There is work that I must do!” When he accompanied the Fathers on their missions, he often converted more hardened sinners by his fervent prayers and winning persuasiveness than the older priests by their sermons.



      Born on 6 April 1726 at Muzo Lucanzo, Gerard died on 16 October 1755 at Caposele, aged barely 29. Characteristic are the words he wrote on the door of his cell: “Here is done what God wills, as God wills, and for as long as God wills!”
