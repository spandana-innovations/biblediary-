      Reflection: 1



      Through the miraculous catch of fish, Jesus prepares those whom he had chosen for the vocation to be Apostles. On discovering then the power of Jesus, Simon Peter felt that he was unworthy to stand before the Lord, and so he begged Jesus to move away. But instead, Jesus appointed Peter and his two companions to catch men, that is, to bring in people to the Church. They left whatever they had and followed him. Obedience to Jesus is a necessary quality of a disciple. It demands detachment from all other ties. Jesus is not interested in our past life, he wants us to trust in his call and go forward the way he shows us. </span></span></span></span>



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      The scene is the Sea of Galilee (*Gennesaret *in Greek and *Tiberius *in Latin). The story of the miraculous catch of fish described in today’s Gospel is similar to the post-Resurrection appearance of Jesus recounted in John 21:4-14. It is one of the “epiphany-call stories” which direct our attention to the fact that Jesus had distinct criteria for selecting people to be apostles. The reading challenges us to examine our own personal calls to conversion and discipleship.



      The miraculous catch followed by the call: After teaching the crowd from a seat in the boat of Simon, Jesus said to him “*Put out into the deep and let down your nets for a catch.” And Simon answered, “Master, we toiled all night and took nothing! But at your word I will let down the nets.”* Simon and his companions were stunned by the biggest catch of their lives. This event led Simon to acknowledge his unworthiness, as a sinner, even to stand before the Divine Presence of Jesus. Impressed by Simon’s obedience and confession of unworthiness, Jesus immediately invited Simon, Andrew, James and John to become close disciples and so to “*catch men*” instead of fish.



      <span class="subhead">Life Messages: </span>



      1) Our encounter with the holiness of God needs to lead us to recognize our sinfulness. The Good News of today’s Gospel is that our sinfulness — our pride and self-centeredness – does not repel God. That is why we offer this Mass asking God’s pardon and forgiveness, and why we receive Jesus in Holy Communion only after acknowledging our unworthiness.



      2) With Jesus, the seemingly impossible becomes possible. Today’s Gospel passage tells us an important truth about how God works in and through us for His glory. God chooses ordinary people – people like you and me – as His ambassadors. He uses the ordinary circumstances of our daily lives and our responses.

      In the Gospel, Simon Peter begged Jesus to “depart from me, for I am a sinful man, O Lord.” However, Jesus, with love, mercy, and forgiveness, asserted that from then on, he would be catching men. Simon and his fellow fishermen left everything to follow the Lord. Regardless of our sins and dark past, Jesus calls us to begin a new life with him. He wants us to walk away from our sins and leave behind whatever makes us sinful. Jesus is not interested in our sinful past but in our present and future with him. We must respond to his call to become his followers. As Jesus said, “Do not be afraid,” we must trust in his companionship and follow his footsteps.

    saintName: "St Rose of Viterbo, Virgin (1235 -1252)"
    ref: "St Rose of Viterbo, Virgin (1235 -1252)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      The daughter of poor and pious parents, Rose showed remarkable holiness in her early childhood. At 7 she was already practising penitential austerities and living as a recluse. In response to Our Lady’s command, she joined the Third Order of St Francis when about 10, and started to preach public penance in the streets of Viterbo (in Italy). For two years she exhorted the citizens to remain faithful to the Pope and to resist Emperor Frederick II of Germany, who at that time held the city. The spirited youngster spoke with such power and apparent authority, accompanying her pleas with such remarkable miracles that the imperial prefect came to consider her influence a serious threat to his own authority, and so banished her from Viterbo with her family.



      When the Pope’s power was restored in Viterbo in 1251, Rose returned to her native city and asked for admission to the convent of the Poor Clares, but was refused. Thereupon she went to live in a small cell on the outskirts of Viterbo, foretelling that she would be admitted to the convent after her death. And so it happened indeed, for not long after she had died the following spring at the age of 17, i.e. on 6 March 1252, Pope Alexander IV ordered her remains to be interred at the convent of the Poor Clares.



      On the eve of her feast annually, 70 men carry her body through the streets in a triumphal procession, surmounted by a 60-foot-high wooden tower of Gothic design.
