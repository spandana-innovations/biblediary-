  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Mark 9:30-37"
    audio: null
    body: |
      *“If anyone would be first, he must be last of all and servant of all”* (Mk 9:35).



      This episode is a beautiful illustration of Jesus’ words in today’s Gospel. Jesus’ own life bore this out very clearly. After washing the feet of His disciples, Jesus said: “You call Me Teacher and Lord; and it is right that you do so, because that is what I am. I, your Lord and Teacher, have just washed your feet.…” (Jn 13:13, 14). Today’s Gospel gives evidence of our desire for greatness. Almost all of us are ambitious to be great somehow, somewhere. The disciples of Jesus, drawn from the common people, were no exception. And they wanted to know who would be the greatest among them. In itself it was something silly, but the context in which their discussion took place makes it all the more ridiculous. On the one hand, Jesus has just predicted the impending persecution, rejection and crucifixion awaiting Him. On the other hand, His disciples, illiterate fisherfolk, are discussing who is the greatest among them. What callous insensitivity! But Jesus teaches them patiently. He calls them to Him and asks: “What were you discussing on the way?” Realizing their foolishness, they remain tongue-tied and silent. So Jesus arranges a superb demonstration, a compelling, convincing study in contrast. Jesus brings a child in their midst, takes it in His arms and says: “Whoever receives one such child in My name receives Me; and whoever receives Me, receives not Me but Him who sent Me.” No more arguments, no more discussion about greatness. A child resting in the arms of a parent knows the source of its security and safety. A child by definition is one who depends on another for everything. Whatever greatness he has is derived from someone greater. And that greater one is God who is the Father of all. So we are all His children. That is our greatness.



      *(New Horizon Homilies, Fr Joe ssp and Fr Philip ssp)*
  - key: reflection
    title: Reflection
    ref: null
    audio: null
    body: |
      The Readings of this Sunday present a very grim and violent picture of what a good man will have to undergo. The First Reading narrates the plot of those who oppose a good and righteous person. This description is perfectly applicable to Jesus, the righteous person, who was unjustly condemned. But the death of Jesus was not merely the result of a plot by evil people, rather, it was a divine plan. Jesus was intent on fulfilling the plan of God and hence he was more than willing to embrace the cross and was ready for the most painful death on the cross. However, in the Gospel we do see the contrasting characters: while Jesus is talking about his impending death, the disciples are discussing about who is the greatest! How often we get embroiled in our own ego and status, ignoring the will of God for us?

    title: Saint of the Day
    saintName: "St Thomas of Villanova -Bishop Confessor (1486 -1555)"
    ref: "St Thomas of Villanova -Bishop Confessor (1486 -1555)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Born at Fuentellana in Castille, Spain, Thomas got the suffix “Villanova” by virtue of the close proximity of his own birthplace to that of his parents, i.e., Villanueva de los Infantes. This “Almsgiver” and “Father of the Poor”, was raised in the virtue of practical charity by his father, a miller, who gave away in alms all he earned.



       At 16, he joined the Hermits of St Augustine at Salamanca. After his Ordination he gained fame as a learned lecturer of Theology and was much in demand all over Spain for his preaching. Emperor Charles V had great reverence for his personal sanctity and made him his court preacher and councillor of state.









      As Provincial of his congregation, he sent out the first band of Augustinian missionaries to Mexico. As Archbishop of Valencia, he laboured at reforming the morals of both clergy and laity and did away with underground prisons. A money gift which he received from the cathedral chapter for use in his palace, he used instead for rebuilding the fire-gutted general hospital; he founded a college for young clergy, another for poor students and an orphanage for foundlings and children of indigent parents; and for the benefit of working people, Masses were offered at very early hours of the morning.



      The epitome of utter simplicity and poverty, Thomas mended his own clothes and gave away two thirds of his Episcopal revenues in alms. He also healed the sick and converted hardened sinners. His writings on mystical theology have seen over 20 editions.



      He died in 1555, was beatified in 1618 and canonized in 1658.
