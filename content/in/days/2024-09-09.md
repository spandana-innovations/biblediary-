  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Luke 6:6–11"
    audio: null
    body: |
      In today’s Gospel, curing a man of withered hand on Sabbath day, is provoked by Jesus himself and reveals the seriousness of the conflict between Him and the religious authority. It is a conflict between life and death. It is a question of the Pharisees and the Herodians, that is, of both, the religious and civil authority. The whole issue could be: what is lawful or what is not lawful on the Sabbath day: The Pharisees, who had followed Jesus through the fields on their way to the synagogue, are still observing Jesus and his disciples with a critical eye on that same Sabbath day: they watched him, to see whether he would heal him on the sabbath, so that they might accuse him. Being fully aware of their wicked intention, that his action will invite wrath from scribes and Pharisees, casting all fears aside, he heals the man who had the withered hand. What we observe here is not just a miracle, it is the confrontation of the unjust structures that oppress the people. Are we prepared to take this task seriously to clean up our own system? Or let people go on suffering all through their life? Doing good for the mankind on any day is very good.

      *(Fr Michael Raj ssp) *
  - key: reflection
    title: Reflection
    ref: null
    audio: null
    body: |
      “Lawful on the Sabbath” was the same question that the Jewish leaders had raised in the Gospel Reading on Saturday (see Lk 6:2). Life-saving activity and physical wellbeing of a person is ‘lawful’ even on the Sabbath is what Jesus taught and emphasized. Though Jesus was aware that the Jewish leaders were plotting to find fault with him in order to have him put to death, Jesus daringly went ahead with the healing of the man with a withered hand. Do I have the courage to stand up for what is right and true? Do I value human welfare more than the observance of the Law, no matter how important that law might be? Am I willing to lay down my life, if need be, for the good of others? Like the Lord, may we have due courage!

    audio: null
    body: |
      The son of a Spanish farmer living near Barcelona, St Peter became a Jesuit at the age of 20, and while finishing his novitiate on the island of Majorca, was inspired by St Alphonsus Rodriguez, the holy doorkeeper of the Jesuit college there, to devote his life to missionary work in America. In 1610 he arrived at Cartagena in Central America, was ordained priest, and for the next 44 years made that port city his headquarters for missionary work among the Negro slaves.

      Peter Claver was probably the first white man to take interest in these unfortunates. Accompanied by a small band of interpreters, he would lovingly minister to the sick and the dying in the horribly fetid hold of ships, and hand out fruit and cooling drinks to the half crazed, fear-struck Negroes who had only been permitted on deck as the ship cast anchor. Undeterred by the antagonism of the callous merchants and the misgivings of his own superiors who were apt to look askance at his “indiscreet zeal”, Peter calmly went about, instructing his charges in the elements of the Christian Faith. At a solemn baptismal ceremony in the large town square, he would present each Negro with a small but greatly treasured medal of Jesus and Mary. In all, it has been estimated, he administered the Sacrament to over 300,000 people.

      Peter Claver was canonized at the same time as his friend St Alphonsus Rodriguez in 1888 and declared special Patron of all Catholic missions in 1896.
