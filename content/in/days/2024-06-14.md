  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Matthew 5:27–32"
    audio: null
    body: |
      The words of Jesus about perfect chastity may cause some unrest in light of today’s tolerance for free sexual expression, at least in many parts of the world. Apart from the challenge to couples to stay faithful to their marriage covenant, he warns against unchaste thoughts and desires. Some time back, in a cover story on “The Sex Addiction Epidemic”, Newsweek magazine highlighted how internet pornography is rampant and proves addictive to many. People have written about the cybersex compulsion and priests have noted anecdotally how one of the sins most frequently brought to the Sacrament of Penance is seeking out pornography, particularly on the internet.



      Various factors might impel one to seek release through internet pornography, such as overwork, loneliness and an immature spirituality. For a workaholic, pornography can seem a risk-free stress-reliever; for a lonely or isolated person it offers the illusion of connecting with others; and for a wavering character it can be the shadow side of a double life, marked by a perfect observance of external rules and rigid, black-and-white thinking. People with other addictive behaviours are at risk of adopting internet pornography as a form of self-medication.



      We need to beware of internet pornography. Even priests and religious need the same self-control as others to resist this miasma in our society. Pornography is a symptom of the modern de-emphasis on chastity and responsibility. The Catechism of the Catholic Church (par. 2339) calls chastity a training in human freedom . We guard our dignity when, resisting slavery to our passions, we press on with freely choosing what is good. Later it says: “The baptized must continue to struggle against concupiscence of the flesh and disordered desires.”



      (Association of Catholic Priests: Homily Resources).
  - key: reflection
    title: Reflection
    ref: null

    body: |
      In the Gospel, Jesus intensifies the sixth commandment by raising the bar of sexual values. He moves beyond the physical act to the attitude of the heart. Just as we can murder with our words, we can also commit adultery with our heart. When we look at another person with the covetous desire to possess and manipulate, we commit adultery with that person in our hearts. Here, from the point of view of the mystery of personhood, we reduce the other to a consumer item and covet him/her as a thing, rather than accept him/her as a person. No person per se is meant to be an object of lust ever. Every person is God’s gift to be valued, and any action from our side that devalues a person’s dignity amounts to sin.

      St Methodius I (Patriarch of Constantinople) (A,D.-847)

      A Sicilian by birth, Methodius received his preliminary education in his native town, Syracuse (Italy). He went to Constantinople (now Istanbul, Turkey), with the ambition of acquiring a post at court. Instead, he met a monk, influenced by whom, he renounced the world and joined the Monastery of Chenolakhos. During the second iconoclastic persecution under Leo the Armenian, in 815, he boldly stood his ground in favour of the veneration of the sacred images. However, after the deposition and exile of St Nicephorus, he went to Rome, apparently charged to inform Pope St Pascal I, of the state of affairs. There he remained till the death of Emperor Leo V.

      In 821 he returned to Constantinople with a letter from the Pope to the Emperor, Michael the Stammerer, requesting him to reinstate Nicephorus. But Michael, after reading the letter denounced Methodius as a stirrup of sedition and ordered him scourged and deposed. Thus he was persecuted by the iconoclasts who kept him confined in a tomb on the island of St Andrew, for seven years. Summoned to depose before Emperor Theophilus, Methodius boldly asserted: “If an image is so worthless in your eyes, how is it that when you condemn the image of Jesus Christ you do not also condemn the veneration paid to the representations of yourself? Far from doing so, you are continually causing them to be multiplied!” Methodius died of dropsy in 847.
