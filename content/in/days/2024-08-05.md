    ref: "Matthew 14:13–21"

    body: |
      Today’s Gospel describes Jesus’ miraculous feeding of a great multitude. The story is told in all four Gospels and serves as Jesus’ way of introducing to those listening a merciful and providing God. This miraculous feeding was meant to remind people of God’s provision of manna in the wilderness and to foreshadow the true Heavenly Bread which Jesus would offer those who listened, believed, and chose to receive. Moses, Elijah, and Elisha had all fed people without the benefit of resources. The present miracle resembles particularly the one performed by Elisha in 2 Kgs 4:42-44.



      Jesus took pity on the growing physical hunger of his listeners as they listened and challenged the apostles to feed them. They brought him what they had — five loaves of bread and two dried fish. Jesus took these, said a prayer of blessing, broke them and asked the apostles to distribute them. Since it was mid-April, springtime in Israel, the people could sit comfortably on green grass in groups of hundreds and fifties as Jesus asked. After serving a sumptuous meal, which satisfied everyone’s hunger, the apostles collected twelve wicker baskets filled with leftover bread and fish pieces, a vivid demonstration of God’s generosity in giving.



      Life message: We may not be able to feed the hungry millions in the world, but today’s Gospel challenges us to do our humble share in alleviating hunger and poverty in our neighborhood. God will amplify our little contributions and reward our good will and generosity. Let us be thankful to Jesus for feeding us spiritually with the word of God and with the Holy Eucharist.

      In the miracle of the multiplication of the loaves three things draw our attention: a) The disciples see the place as desolate, but Jesus orders the people to sit on the grass; b) There are only five loaves at the beginning, but in the end twelve baskets of leftovers are collected; and c) The reluctant disciples become the active disciples. Jesus saw the negative situation of hunger as an opportunity while the disciples saw it as a problem. Jesus became proactive whereas the disciples were reactive. And Jesus asked the disciples to bring the five loaves and two fish to him, thus making them aware of the resources. How do we handle a negative situation in our life?

      Dedication of the Basilica of St Mary Major



      Theological debate over Christ’s dual nature as God and man turned into dissent in Constantinople in the early 5th century with Bishop Nestorius and his chaplain, Athanasius preaching against the title Theotokos, i.e., “Mother of God”, insisting that Mary was merely the mother of the human Jesus. The Council of Ephesus in 431, however, reaffirmed Mary’s title as Mother of God. In commemoration, Pope Sixtus III (432-440) rebuilt the Liberian Basilica in Rome and dedicated it to Saint Mary Major.

      Built atop the Esquiline, one of the seven hills of Rome, it is one of the four Patriarchal Cathedrals commemorating the first centres of the Church, Saint John Lateran representing Rome, the See of Peter, Saint Paul Outside the Walls, the See of Alexandria, and Saint Peter’s, the See of Constantinople being the other three. Saint Mary Major, representing the See of Antioch is the largest Church in Christendom honouring God through Mary. Legend has it that this magnificent Basilica, originally built by Pope Liberias (352-366), was the result of a dream of a wealthy though childless Roman couple, desirous of pledging their entire fortune to the Mother of God. Her approval of their vow was indicated by a sudden midsummer snowfall one night in August 355 and in an apparition instructing them to build a church at the spot, an identical dream of the Pope corroborating the same.

      It was Pope Pius V, who lies buried in the Basilica, that extended this feast to the Universal Church.



      <span class="subhead">Reflection:</span>*“I encourage you, be men and women of deep and abiding faith…. Let the Good News of Christ radiate from your hearts, and the peace he alone gives remain forever in your souls” (Pope Saint John Paul II).*Saint Mary MacKillop Foundress (1842-1909)





      Baptized Maria Ellen at Fitzroy near Melbourne, Australia, this daughter of Highland Scottish immigrants was a governess by profession. Inspired by Father Julian Tenison-Woods, a missionary from England and one of the chief architects of Australia’s Catholic education system, Mary and two younger sisters began teaching in an abandoned stable at Penola, South Australia.

      Moving to Adelaide, Maria Ellen and Father Tenison-Woods founded the Institute of the Sisters of Saint Joseph of the Sacred Heart. Together with her companions, Maria pronounced the vows of religion and took the name ‘Mary of the Cross’. Her efforts to adapt the new community to a colonial environment encountered a decade of lay and clerical misunderstanding and opposition, climaxing in the disbanding of the sisterhood and her excommunication in 1871 by the Bishop of Adelaide. A compassionate Jew gave the homeless nuns a house rent free, until their restoration in 1872.

      In 1874 Mary travelled to Rome and submitted her rule to Pope Pius IX. Rome’s eventual decision was a compromise but the Foundress won her principal point of central government for the sisters throughout the Australian colonies. By 1964, her congregation, which numbered about 1,000 at the time of her death, had 2,106 sisters ministering in 22 dioceses in Australia, 4 in New Zealand, and 1 in Ireland.

      Mary Helen Mackillop was beatified in 1995 and canonized in 2010.
