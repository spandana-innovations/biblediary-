    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Matthew 13:47–53"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Reflection: 1



      Just as there are good and bad fish in the sea, so there are also good and evil realities in the Church. Not all who become members of the Church are holy, nor are they all children of the kingdom, because all do not have the right spirit. The parable of the fishing net teaches us that we will see scandals in the Church, but a time of judgement will come when he wicked will be separated from the just and thrown away; and they will bear the terrible consequences of their evil deeds. We must never forget that what we do today will produce its effects also in the future, and will decide the kind of eternal life we shall have. Everything is to be dome with his in mind.



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      Today’s Gospel presents the third in a set of three parables Jesus preached on the Kingdom of God/Heaven and the conditions for entering it.



      The parable of the fishing net: In Palestine, there were two main ways of fishing. The first was with the casting-net, which required a keen eye and great skill in throwing the net at the correct moment. The second was with a dragnet or seine. Galilean seine nets were tied to two boats and drawn through the water. The catch was sorted only afterwards, with edible or kosher fish going to market and unacceptable fish being thrown away. Just as a dragnet collects good and bad fish indiscriminately, so the Church is bound to be a mixture of all kinds of people, good and bad, useless and useful.



      This parable encourages the Church to adopt an open approach to Evangelization. The parable also teaches that the time of separation will come in the Final Judgment, when the good and the bad will be sent to their respective destinies. This parable is, thus, a counterpart to the parable of the weeds and the wheat. The concluding simile or mini parable: Jesus concludes his parables by advising the listeners to imitate wise scribes (Jewish religious teachers who specialized in Sacred Scripture and its application to life). A scribe/scholar need not give up his scholarship when he became a Christian; rather he should use his learning for Christ. Christians are also expected to be like scholars who study both the old wisdom of their ancestors and the new vistas of knowledge. They have a duty to pass on to others the Christian teaching they have received in language their hearers can understand.



      Life message: We need to learn tolerance and compassionate understanding.) The lesson of this parable is that the Church is a mixed body of saints and sinners (good and bad fish). There will be always a temptation on the part of some who feel they are more “faithful” to separate themselves from the “unfaithful.” But Jesus reminds us that the final judgment resulting in reward or punishment is the work of God. Thus, we must learn to be tolerant, patient, compassionate, and understanding of those who seem to us to fall far below the requirements of the Gospel and the Kingdom. Let us humbly admit the fact that only Jesus and Mary were not a mixture of good and evil. Let us acknowledge as St. Paul did, “I am what I am with the grace of God.

      We commemorate today Saint Ignatius of Loyola, the founder of the Society of Jesus. His motto was the *Magis*, ‘For the greater Glory of God.’ Life is meant to aim at the higher realities that take us to new horizons. Jesus in today’s gospel forms a platform from which we can look at life and live for the greater glory of God. He compares the kingdom of God with a dragnet which attracts all kinds of sea species. Through this parable, he points out the fact that there will be a final judgment for all of us. Therefore, we must look at our lives seriously. We need to take responsibility for our actions. Our present life will lay the foundation for the life to come.

    audio: null
    body: |
      Baptized Inigo, Ignatius was the last of the 11 children of parents of noble descent in northern Spain. During a long convalescence following a serious injury sustained in the siege of Pamplona, he read the life of Christ and a book of Saints’ lives which made him realize the hollowness and futility of his own. “If the Saints could do this, why not I?” he asked himself.



      After his recovery he spent a year in solitude at Manresa, searching for God’s will, and then made a pilgrimage to the Holy Land. He was already 33 when he took up the study of Latin among the schoolboys of Barcelona, and later devoted 11 years to philosophy and theology, at various Universities. At Paris, when doing his Master’s [1535], he was able to attract six young men to his life of mortification, poverty and spiritual exercises, among them Saints Peter Faber and Francis Xavier.



      The “Jesuits”, as they came to be popularly called, grew rapidly in number and fervor and were soon conspicuous in all the towns of Italy, caring for the poor and the sick, preaching in the streets, explaining Catholic truths in simple, direct language, and encouraging the frequent reception of the sacraments. At Ignatius’ death the 16 year-old Society already counted about 1,000 members and over 100 houses.



      Ignatius, an implacable foe of Protestantism, died in 1556, was beatified in 1609, canonized in 1622 and declared Patron of Spiritual Exercises and Retreats in 1922.
