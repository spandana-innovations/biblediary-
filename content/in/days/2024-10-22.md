  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Luke 12:35–38"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Today’s passage from Luke’s Gospel is one of three eschatological discourses in the Gospel. It gives us one of the two “Master – Servant” parables. It emphasizes the necessity of Faith and vigilant preparedness in the lives of Christ’s followers. Since a Jewish wedding feast could last a week, the servants had ample time to take their rest before the master’s return. Garments tied up about the waist are an image of readiness in the Scriptures because the Jewish soldiers wore full-length garments while Roman soldiers wore kilts, which enabled them to run at full speed when they had to. Jesus wants his disciples to be ready to do God’s will at every moment, by loving others through humble and sacrificial service.






      The interpretation: In the parable, the chief characters are a master (representing the risen Jesus), and his servants (Jesus’ followers). According to the Fathers of the Church, Jesus’ words in this passage have two senses. In the narrower sense, the words refer to the Second Coming of Jesus, but in the broader sense they refer to the time of our own death, when God will call us to meet Him and to give Him an account of our life on earth. Since the precise time of each is unknown to us, the proper attitude for us is constant watchfulness. Since we cannot be sure about the day of our death, we should do our present work perfectly every day, and not leave it undone, half-done or postponed.






      Life messages






      1) We need to stay vigilant and ready to face the Lord through prayer. One of the traditional means for remaining alert is prayer. The most important elements in prayer are listening to God (*1 Kgs 19:11-12*) and talking to Him. This means we have to set aside a quiet time every day during which we can tune our ears to God’s message of love, harmony, and peace, and respond to Him.






      2) We need to wait for the Lord who appears to us in different disguises everyday. We must wait for the Lord in our daily lives by learning to see Jesus in the least of our brothers and sisters. In other words, we must be prepared to serve Jesus whenever and in whatever form Jesus appears. What we discover in serving, loving, and helping other people is that God invariably comes to us through them.

    body: |
      One of the main differences between a non-believer and a believer is this: a non-believer sees life as an episode between birth and death. For a believer, life is a journey that originates from God, moves towards God and eventually reaches its fulfilment in God. In today’s gospel passage, Jesus invites his followers to be vigilant and prepared: “Stay dressed for action and keep your lamps burning” for they must be ready to continue their journey with their master, who is presently away but may return at any time! How blessed are those servants who are vigilantly waiting for the master’s arrival! Their faithful waiting will not go unrewarded. The gospel promises that their master will have them sit at table and he himself will serve them!

    body: |
      When Cardinal Karol Józef Wojtyła, Archbishop of Krakow, was elected Pope in 1978, he made history by becoming the first non-Italian in over four centuries and also the first Polish ever to occupy the See of Peter! Naming himself after the two conciliar popes, he signalled his determination to have the fruits of Vatican II benefit every believer.









      The youngest of three children, he received his First Holy Communion at the age of 9 and his Confirmation at 18. He enrolled at the Jagellonian University of Krakow in 1938, but when the occupying Nazis closed the university in 1939, Karol worked (1940-1944) in a quarry and then in the Solvay chemical factory to earn a living and to avoid deportation to Germany. Ordained priest in 1946 on completing his seminary and university studies post-war, he obtained a doctorate in Theology for his dissertation on faith as understood in the works of Saint John of the Cross in 1948 and defended his thesis at the Jagellonian University on the possibility of grounding a Christian ethic on the ethical system developed by Max Scheler.



      Consecrated auxiliary bishop of Krakow in 1958, he contributed significantly to the drafting of the Apostolic Constitution *Gaudium et Spes* of Vatican II (1962-1965). Appointed Archbishop of Krakow in 1964, he was created Cardinal in 1967.



      He survived an assassination attempt in 1981 in St Peter’s Square. Saved by the maternal hand of Mary, he forgave and visited the would-be assassin, Mehmet Ali Agca, in prison. Aware of having received a great gift, he further intensified his pastoral commitments with heroic generosity. He was canonized on Divine Mercy Sunday 2014.
