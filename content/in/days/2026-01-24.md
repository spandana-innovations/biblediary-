      Reflection: 1



      Jesus repeatedly meets opposition from Jewish leaders, and now even from his own family. They come to take him home because he is too busy with the crowd gathered around him to have sufficient rest or food. They come out of a misplaced love for him. They have heard people saying, he is out of his mind! The teachers of the law even say he is under the power of the prince of demons (Mk 3:22). Yet these are the very powers Jesus is destroying. Jesus points out to them that they have slandered the Holy Spirit and thus committed a sin that will never be forgiven. Jesus was never discouraged by people’s negative attitudes and criticisms. We should never give up our good works because people criticize or misunderstand us. </span></span></span></span>



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      Today’s Gospel tells us how Jesus’ relatives and fellow villagers wrongly judged him as out of his mind and consequently tried to take him by force back to Nazareth to his safe, secure job as a good carpenter. That might be one reason why Jesus once remarked, “*a man’s enemies will be the members of his own household.”* (Mt 10:36). However, Jesus met opposition with grace and with determination to fulfil his Father’s will.</span></span></span></span></span>



      There were five reasons why Jesus’ family thought he was mad and attempted to dissuade him from his preaching and healing mission. First, Jesus had abandoned his safe, secure job as a much-needed village carpenter with a steady income to become a wandering preacher with no residence or steady income. Second, Jesus had chosen a band of fishermen with no political or social influence, a hated tax-collector and a fanatic zealot among his disciples. Third, Jesus had begun to criticize the power lobby – the chief priests, elders, scribes and Pharisees – in the Jewish religious headquarters, Jerusalem, labelling them hypocrites. Jesus’ relatives might really have been afraid that Jesus would be arrested, and they would be persecuted with him for criticizing those in power. Fourth, Jesus had indirectly claimed to be the long-awaited Messiah and had worked miracles to support his claim. Fifth, they might have been jealous of Jesus’ huge popularity throughout Palestine.</span></span></span></span></span>



      <span class="subhead">Life messages:</span></span></span> 1) Since Jesus experienced rejection by his own relatives, he can sympathize with the hurt and rejection we receive from our family members and console us in our pain. 2) Let us learn to forgive the modern “liberal-minded” people who find our Christian beliefs and practice “crazy,” and face them with the courage of our convictions based on Christ’s Divine authority and the reliability of his doctrines and promises. 3) Let us remember that many saints, following Christ’s example, have been taken for madmen–but they were mad with love, mad with love for Jesus Christ, their God. </span></span></span></span></span>

      In the Gospel today, we see mixed reactions to Jesus’ public ministry. On the one hand, the crowds flock to Him and surround Him all day, listening to His teaching, and on the other hand, His natural family come out to restrain Him because they have heard that He has gone mad. Their motive is to discourage Him from continuing His kingdom ministry and to get Him back home where He belongs. The ministry of Jesus is a powerful example of both admiration and criticism coexisting. Congratulations and condemnation go hand in hand. Nevertheless, Jesus goes about working quietly, selflessly and tirelessly. Discouragement from His own natural family does not stop Him from doing good. Are you easily discouraged when people criticise you for your work? Do not fear criticism! You are made for greater things!</span></span>

    audio: null
    body: |
      The eldest of thirteen children, Francis de Sales was born to aristocrats Francis de Boisy and Frances de Sionnaz at the Sales castle France. At 25 he obtained his doctorate in law at Padua, but, declining a senatorship and giving up law, proceeded to become a priest.</span></span></span></span>



      The province of Le Chablais on the south shore of Lake Geneva, under the Protestant city of Berne for fifty years, had just been re-conquered by the Duke of Savoy, a Catholic. Francis and his cousin, the Canon Louis de Sales, volunteered to win its Calvinist inhabitants back to the Faith. He set forth the Church’s principles in the famous “Controversies”, hand-copied leaflets of which he would slip under house-doors or paste up on walls. No wonder he was declared patron of writers/journalists by Pius XI in 1923!</span></span></span></span>



      People who heard him preach were converted as much by his gracious attitude as by his lucid arguments. Francis succeeded in converting over 70,000 Calvinists and cherishing the joy of seeing all churches re-opened to Catholic worship. His ministrations though were not without risks. At 32, Francis had signally so impressed Pope Clement VIII, who personally examined him in the presence of his Cardinals, that he was appointed to succeed Bishop de Granier in 1602. </span></span></span></span>



      As bishop, Francis drastically simplified his lifestyle so as to enable him the better to succour the poor and the needy. He reformed religious communities, formulated simple language catechetical instruction for both young and old, and held annual synods for his clergy on whom he particularly impressed the value of short, straightforward and unadorned sermons.</span></span></span></span>



      Voluminous correspondence apart, he made time to write masterpieces of spiritual guidance like *Introduction to the Devout Life*for the benefit of the laity, and *Treatise on the Love of God* and *Spiritual Conferences*, both addressed to the Visitation sisters that he and Saint Jane Frances de Chantal had founded in 1610. </span></span></span></span>



      Beatified in 1662 and canonized in 1665 Francis was declared Doctor of the Church in 1877.</span></span></span></span>
