  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Matthew 6:24–34"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Worrying is part of the human condition. Parents worry about their children. Family members worry about each other. Young people worry about their future. Jesus must have worried about his disciples, about the lack of response on the part of some of his contemporaries to his message, about many things.



      In the gospel today Jesus is not saying ‘don’t ever worry about anything.’ The focus of worry in that gospel is food, drink and clothing, and the worry in question is excessive worry or preoccupation. Jesus makes reference in that reading to ‘the pagans who set their hearts on all these things.’



      What is at issue here is setting our hearts on things that is not of ultimate importance. To that extent, it is really about getting our priorities right, getting them into line with God’s priorities. That is why Jesus calls us to ‘Set your hearts on God’s kingdom first, and on his righteousness.’ Don’t be so preoccupied about food, drink and clothing, Jesus is saying, that there is no room in your heart for concern about the coming of God’s kingdom or the doing of God’s will. The first three petitions of the Lord’s Prayer relate to what might be termed God’s priorities, ‘Hallowed by thy name, thy kingdom come, thy will be done.’ These were Jesus’ priorities and he calls us to make them our own as well.

    ref: null

    body: |
      Human life is the biggest manufacturer and distributor of anxiety. Jesus wants his followers to stop worrying and put their energy into things that give more meaning to life. He gives the example of the birds of the air and the lilies of the field to show the way God takes care of His creation. God is intricately involved in every aspect of his creation. Worrying has never changed anything, ever. It only makes us feel worse about what we are worrying about, especially if that worry is about things that are monetary or provisional in nature. Jesus’ command not to worry should not create laziness or complacency in us. We need to work hard in life with our God-given skills and abilities which will enable us to give glory to God and ensure life eternal.

      St Paulinus of Nola (Bishop, Confessor) (c.353 -431)

      Pontius Meropius Anicius Paulinus was born near Bordeaux (France), the son of a wealthy Roman patrician who at that time held the post of praetorian prefect of Gaul. He received his education from the poet Ausonius and at 25, already a noted orator and poet, was made senator. He was appointed prefect of Rome and governor of the province of Campagna by the Emperor Valentinian.

      Not long after his marriage to Therasia, a Spanish Christian, he was himself baptized in 385, and when their only son died in infancy, they went to live in Spain for some years, and here the inhabitants of Barcelona insisted on his being ordained a priest. Therasia thereupon sold her Spanish possessions for the relief of the poor and became a ‘nun’, while Paulinus, after spending some time with St Ambrose in Milan, settled down to lead a hermit’s life at Nola near Naples (Italy). Here he had, years before, witnessed certain miracles performed at the tomb of St Felix, the martyred Bishop of Nola, and had a hospital erected nearby at his own expense. He also contributed lavishly to the erection of a great basilica in honour of St Felix who had by then become his special patron. In 409 he was elected Bishop of Nola, a See he governed with admirable success for over 20 years. He is gratefully remembered for the aqueduct he built to the town to give the people ample fresh water.

      One of the most cultured men of his age, Paulinus became famed for his scientific knowledge, his ecclesiastical writings and Christian poems. He had a miraculous power over the forces of evil.





      St John Fisher

      Cardinal Bishop and Martyr (A.D. 1469-1535)

      At 14, St John Fisher went to Cambridge University (England), where he distinguished himself in his studies, was elected a fellow of Michaelhouse, and was ordained priest with special permission since he was only 22. He became successively Senior Proctor, Doctor of Divinity, Master of Michaelhouse, Vice-Chancellor of Cambridge and chaplain to Lady Margaret Beaufort, mother of King Henry VII.

      In 1504, he was elected Chancellor of the University and was nominated Bishop of Rochester by King Henry VIII. Observing strict austerity, he carried out his pastoral duties with great zeal, held visitations, administered the sacrament of confirmation, disciplined the clergy, visited the sick poor in their hovels, distributed alms, and limited his sleep to four hours, keeping a skull before him at meal times to remind himself of death. Books were his only pleasure.

      Staunchly upholding the sanctity of marriage, he courageously championed for the rights of the Church and the supremacy of the Pope, even speaking against accepting the king as supreme head of the Church of England. Confined to the Tower for a year without trial, he was questioned repeatedly, Fisher standing his ground, stating unequivocally that the “king was not, nor could be, by the law of God, Supreme Head” of the Church. Summarily condemned to death, he was executed in 1535; his body was given a rough burial in the churchyard of All Hallows near the Tower while his head was displayed on London Bridge.

      As his place of burial attracted many who venerated him, his body was reburied in the Tower church of St Peter-ad-Vincula. Some 450 years later, i.e., in 1935, he was canonized by Pope Pius XI.

      St Thomas More (Martyr) (1477-1535)

      Described as “a man for all seasons” by Erasmus, Thomas was the son of a London Judge named John More. A lawyer by profession, he was deeply religious, and followed the Spiritual exercises of the Carthusian monks for four years before, at 27, marrying Jane Colt who bore him three daughters and a son. Jane having died in the 6th year of their happy union, Thomas married the widow Alice Middleton, who proved herself an excellent housewife and loving foster-mother to his four young children.

      Elected to Parliament at 26, Thomas, as under-sheriff of London, was “the best friend the poor ever had”. In 1529 he was appointed Lord Chancellor of England. But neither honour nor power lessened his interest in the Faith and the value-based education of his children.

      When Henry VIII, having had no male heir by his Queen, Catherine of Aragon, was declined an annulment by Rome in order to marry Anne Boleyn, he had himself declared head of the Church of England. Thomas promptly resigned his chancellorship in protest and prepared himself and his family for the consequences.

      Sentenced to death, More, prematurely aged and bent from 15 months of imprisonment, while ascending the scaffold, humorously cautioned the lieutenant who was to behead him thus: “See me safely up; as for my coming down, I can shift for myself!”

      Canonized in 1935, Thomas was declared patron of political leaders in 2000.



      <span class="subhead">Reflection:</span> “Thanks to the mercy and might of God, I have never consented to anything that went against my conscience!” (St Thomas More)
