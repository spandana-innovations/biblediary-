  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Luke 24: 13-35"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Reflection: 1



      The Emmaus incident recalls the theme of journey. The two disciples are going away from Jerusalem. The risen Jesus meets them and put them back on track. On the way Jesus reveals himself to them through a new reading of the Scriptures and through the breaking of bread. It is through the Scripture and the Eucharist that Christians discover Jesus as they are on their way. Do we recognize the presence of God in our lives? How? Do we recognize Jesus as he meets us and stands at our side? The two disciples were going to Emmaus broken-hearted. But, even without recognizing him, they invited him to stay with then that night. They sat with him and listened to him. When they recognized him, they returned to Jerusalem. If we listen to Jesus and participate in the Eucharist, we too shall understand that God’s plan is so wonderful, and we will have the courage to trust him and go on the right way in our life.



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      The Emmaus episode, which is found only in Luke’s Gospel, describes how Jesus shared the sorrow and frustration of the disciples at the sad and shameful death of their Master. Their hopes for Jesus’ conquest of the Romans and establishment of the glorious Davidic kingdom with his Divine power had been shattered. The risen Lord, unrecognized, joined them as they walked along. He explained the Scriptures and reminded them of the prophecies about the Messiah’s death and Resurrection in order to show them how the events that had happened were the fulfilment of Messianic prophecies. But it was only at supper, when Jesus blessed and broke the bread and gave it to them, that they recognized Jesus, who promptly vanished from their sight. The phrase, *“the Breaking of the Bread”*, is used repeatedly in the Acts of the Apostles (also written by Luke), to refer to the ritual meal of the Christian community, the Eucharist (Acts 2:42, 46; 20:7; 27:35). Disciples in every century have continued to recognize Jesus in *“the Breaking of the Bread.”*



      <span class="subhead">Life messages:</span> 1) The risen Lord is with us in both the joyful and the sad moments of our lives. Too often, we have our hopes and dreams shattered by the untimely deaths of our dear ones, by a split in family relationships, by the worsening of our illnesses, or by the loss of jobs. On such occasions, we have to learn to experience the risen Lord’s consoling and supporting presence in our lives. 2) As the disciples met the risen Lord on their way to Emmaus, we too must recognize and appreciate his presence in the Holy Eucharist, in the Word of God, in the praying community, at home, and in our Churches. Jesus is still beside his followers. Often, he is only dimly recognized and only by few. “*You were with me,”* wrote St. Augustine some centuries later, *“but I was not with You” (Confessions, X, 27).* Let us learn to talk to Jesus whenever we are alone and listen to him when we read the Bible, hear it preached, or have it explained to us.

      Today’s Gospel teaches us about recognising God’s presence in our lives. The disciples on the road to Emmaus are confused and disheartened, unable to comprehend the events surrounding Jesus’ death and resurrection. Jesus gently rebukes their “foolishness” for failing to grasp the Scriptures. It is in the breaking of the bread – the Holy Eucharist – that their eyes are finally opened, and they recognise Him. This moment highlights the Eucharist’s transformative power to deepen our understanding of God and restore us to the community of believers. As Pope Benedict XVI stated in *Sacramentum Caritatis*, “The Eucharist is not a reward for the good, but a powerful medicine and nourishment for the weak.” Through the Eucharist, we are given new sight, encouraged to grow in wisdom, and sent back to our communities with renewed faith and hope.

    title: Saint of the Day
    saintName: "Saint Julie Billiart - Virgin (1751-1816)"
    ref: "Saint Julie Billiart - Virgin (1751-1816)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Early in life, Marie Rose Julie Billiart, the fifth of a devout family of seven children in Piccardy, France, showed a deep attachment to things spiritual and took a vow of chastity at 14. Two years later, the family business having failed miserably, Julie had to “hire out” and work for others. About 1770, radiating a contagious love of God and an eagerness to be spiritually helpful to the young around her, she was permitted to receive Holy Communion daily—something unheard of at the time.



      An attempt made on her father’s life so unnerved Julie, then 23, as to leave her paralysed and bedridden for the next 30 years. Her saintly spirit intact, however, her mystical life and her intimacy with God deepened further. Repeatedly in danger of death at the hands of the Republicans during the French Revolution (1789-95) she had to be precipitously moved from place to place, Mass and Communion becoming possible only at odd intervals. Through it all Julie continued to direct her charitable works from her bed, particularly anxious to offset the evil of the day by providing, through pious friends, for the education of poor orphans and, later on, of girls in general.



      In August 1803, with the cooperation of Francoise Blin de Bourdon, Visçountess of Gezaincourt, and of Father Joseph Varin d’Ainville, Superior of the Fathers of the Faith, Julie set her work on a permanent footing with the founding of the “Institute of the Sisters of Notre Dame de Namur”. The following June, the then 53 year-old crippled, urged by a certain priest named Father Enfantin, took ‘a single step for the love of the Sacred Heart’ and found herself completely cured!



      In time, she founded ten convents in France and Belgium. Considerable opposition and misunderstanding, particularly on the part of the Bishop of Amiens, caused her, in 1809, to transfer the motherhouse to Namur, Belgium.



      Marie Rose Julie Billiart who died in 1816 was canonized in 1969.
