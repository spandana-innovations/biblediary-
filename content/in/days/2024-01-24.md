  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Mark 4:1–20"
    audio: null
    body: |
      This parable of the sower was originally meant as an encouraging word to his followers. As Mark has been reporting public ministry prior to this parable, Jesus and his disciples have met many difficulties and obstacles. The religious leaders have accused them of breaking the Sabbath. They even have claimed that Jesus heals by the power of Satan. Jesus’ own relatives wanted to confine him to the house because of they feared he had lost the run of himself. That was the context for Jesus talking about the farmer sowing his seed in the field. The Galilean farmer had all kinds of obstacles to deal with, so that many of the seeds he sows never takes root, or never reaches maturity. Yet, in spite of all obstacles and setbacks, the harvest turns out to be great.



      The message is, surely, “Look beyond the obstacles, the set-backs, the disappointments; God is at work in my ministry and the harvest will be great in the end.” We can all become absorbed by what is not going well, by the failures, the losses all around us. The parable encourages us to keep hopeful in the midst of loss and failure, because the Lord is always a work in a life-giving way even when failure and loss seem to dominate the landscape.

      When King David had consolidated his Kingdom and was at the high point of his reign, he thinks of building a temple for God to dwell. However, God speaks through prophet Nathan that it will not be David who will build the temple but his son, Solomon. God wants to continue to be with His people in humble ways until He has fulfilled the promises made to their ancestors. In the Gospel Jesus teaches the large crowd through parables. As a master story-teller he narrates the parable of the Sower and later explains it to his disciples. As followers of Jesus, we too experience the different situations in which the seed, the word of God, grows in our life. The seed that has fallen on the pathways of our life, the seed on the shallow ground and the ones that fell among the thorns of our sinfulness do not allow the seed to produce fruit. We shall identify the soil of our heart and create conditions conducive for the seed to yield a harvest a hundredfold!

    saintName: "Saint Francis de Sales - Bishop Confessor Doctor of the Church (1567-1622)"
    ref: "Saint Francis de Sales - Bishop Confessor Doctor of the Church (1567-1622)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      The eldest of thirteen children, Francis de Sales was born to aristocrats Francis de Boisy and Frances de Sionnaz at the Sales castle France. At 25 he obtained his doctorate in law at Padua, but, declining a senatorship and giving up law, proceeded to become a priest.

      The province of Le Chablais on the south shore of Lake Geneva, under the Protestant city of Berne for fifty years, had just been re-conquered by the Duke of Savoy, a Catholic. Francis and his cousin, the Canon Louis de Sales, volunteered to win its Calvinist inhabitants back to the Faith. He set forth the Church’s principles in the famous “Controversies”, hand-copied leaflets of which he would slip under house-doors or paste up on walls. No wonder he was declared patron of writers/journalists by Pius XI in 1923!

      People who heard him preach were converted as much by his gracious attitude as by his lucid arguments. Francis succeeded in converting over 70,000 Calvinists and cherishing the joy of seeing all churches re-opened to Catholic worship. His ministrations though were not without risks. At 32, Francis had signally so impressed Pope Clement VIII, who personally examined him in the presence of his Cardinals, that he was appointed to succeed Bishop de Granier in 1602.

      As bishop, Francis drastically simplified his lifestyle so as to enable him the better to succour the poor and the needy. He reformed religious communities, formulated simple language catechetical instruction for both young and old, and held annual synods for his clergy on whom he particularly impressed the value of short, straightforward and unadorned sermons.

      Voluminous correspondence apart, he made time to write masterpieces of spiritual guidance like Introduction to the Devout Life for the benefit of the laity, and Treatise on the Love of God and Spiritual Conferences, both addressed to the Visitation sisters that he and Saint Jane Frances de Chantal had founded in 1610.

      Beatified in 1662 and canonized in 1665 Francis was declared Doctor of the Church in 1877.
