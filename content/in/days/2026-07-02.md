  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Matthew 9:1–8"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Beyond exercising Divine authority over temptation, over the lives of men, over nature, over demons, and over sickness, Jesus, as we see in today’s Gospel demonstrates a new form of authority – the Divine authority to forgive sins. Jesus miraculously restores a paralyzed man to health as a sign of having this Divine authority. The healing episode presents Jesus as God Incarnate was sent to save us, restore us, and make us new. So, we have to look beyond the boundaries of our limited religious experience to appreciate the healing and forgiving operation of our God in newer and newer ways.



      Many kinds of sickness, like the paralysis of the man in the story, were seen by the Jews as punishment for the personal sins of the sufferer or of the sufferer’s parents. It was also a common belief that no sickness could be cured until sin was forgiven. For that reason, Jesus had first to convince the paralyzed man that his sins had been forgiven. Once Jesus had granted the paralytic the forgiveness of God, the man knew that God was no longer his enemy, and he was ready to receive the cure which followed. It was the manner of the cure which scandalized the Scribes. By forgiving sin Himself, Jesus had, they thought, blasphemed, insulted God, because forgiving sin is the exclusive prerogative of God. This healing demonstrates two facts: that we can never be right physically until we are right spiritually, and that health in body and peace with God go hand in hand.



      Life messages: 1) We need God’s forgiveness to live wholesome lives. The heart of the Christian Faith is the “forgiveness of sins.” In the Creed we say, “I confess one Baptism for the forgiveness of sins.” While we have the power to forgive others, we need to be forgiven ourselves by the One who has the authority to forgive. In Jesus, we see this authority, the same authority He gave to his Church. Today’s Gospel gives us an invitation to open ourselves to God’s forgiveness in the Sacrament of Reconciliation and to hear from the priest’s mouth the words of Jesus to the paralytic being spoken to us: “*Your sins are forgiven*.”

  - key: reflection
    title: Reflection
    ref: null
    audio: null
    body: |
      It was a widely held Jewish belief that any sickness was the result of sin and that the authority to forgive sins belonged exclusively to God. When Jesus says to the paralytic, “Your sins are forgiven,” He claims for Himself the authority that belongs only to God. He then heals the paralytic, demonstrating His divine power to forgive sins. The focus of the narrative is not on the healing itself but on the forgiveness of sins that Jesus grants. In fact, it is the very purpose of the Incarnation, as the angel of the Lord said to Joseph, “He will save his people from their sins.” Jesus is our Saviour who came to liberate us from all sins and evil. In the words of Pope Francis, “He set us free who were slaves to sin.” Through the cross, Jesus “made us children of God and gave us the freedom of the children”, teaches the Holy Father.

    ref: "Sts Processus and Martinian Martyrs (c. -100 -)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Though about their life nothing much is known, the veneration in which these two martyrs were held by the

      Christians of Rome goes beyond the 4th century. However, according to Roman Martyrology, they were warders of the Mametine prison and were converted along with 40 others by the teachings and miracles of two prisoners, Sts Peter and Paul. The warders offered the apostles their liberty. It is said that a sudden flow of water miraculously sprang up in the prison thus enabling Peter to baptize them.



      Paulinus, the officer in charge, tried to persuade both Processus and Martinian to denounce Christianity. Finding them firm in their resolve to persevere in their new faith they were subjected to cruel torture. They boldly declined to offer incense on the altar of Jupiter, and all through their suffering their only utterance was “Blessed be the name of the Lord!” until they were finally slain by the sword. They were buried by a woman called Lucina, on her own property.



      In the 4th century a Basilica was built over their tomb, where, in the course of time many miracles and healings took place drawing great crowds. At the beginning of the 9th century St Pascal I had their relics transferred to St Peter’s where an altar was built in their honour.
