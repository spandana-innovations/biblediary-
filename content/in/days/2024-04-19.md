  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "John 6:52–59"
    audio: null
    body: |
      The Jewish audience for Jesus’ teaching on the Bread of Life were scandalized at his statement that he was going to give them his Flesh to eat, for it suggested to them cannibalism, forbidden in the Jewish Scriptures. Hence, they wanted to know how Jesus could give his Flesh to eat as a means to gain Eternal Life. Jesus asserted that it was a must for them to eat his Body and drink his Blood if they were to receive Divine Life, Eternal Life, and resurrection from the dead. There is no way to interpret Jesus’ words as “simply symbolic,” which would mean that receiving Communion is only a metaphor, and not really eating and drinking the Body and Blood of Christ. Jesus stresses very forcefully that it is necessary for us to receive him in the Blessed Eucharist in order to share in Divine Life and to develop the life of grace we have received in Baptism. “We receive Jesus Christ in Holy Communion to nourish our souls and to give us an increase of grace and the gift of eternal life” (*St. Pius X Catechism*, # 289). “Really sharing in the body of the Lord in the breaking of the Eucharistic bread, we are taken up into communion with him and with one another.” (Vatican II, *Lumen Gentium,* 7). Jesus adds that eating his Body and drinking his Blood are essential for abiding in him, which is, on this earth, the beginning of the Eternal Life of Heaven. Communion with Jesus enables us to start enjoying Eternal Life with God here on earth, while resurrection gives us eternal life with God forever. St. Thomas Aquinas gives this explanation: “The Word gives life to our souls, but the Word made Flesh nourishes our souls.” (“Commentary on St. John, *in loc*.”).






      <span class="subhead">Life message:</span>



       We need to receive Holy Communion with the full awareness that we are abiding in Jesus, carrying him wherever we go. Hence, we are expected to radiate to all around us the love, the mercy, the spirit of service, and the forgiveness of Jesus.

      God has a plan for each human person and it unfolds in the life of the person gradually. We see how Saul who was persecuting the followers of Jesus was converted to an apostle of the gentiles. After feeding the five thousand, Jesus is now teaching about the Bread of Life at Capernaum. Even though Jesus is aware that the crowd came looking for him only for the bread he gave them, he is using this occasion to teach them about the heavenly Bread which is his own body. This teaching is actualized when Jesus dies on the cross giving up his body and shedding his blood for the life of the world. Like Jesus, do I break myself for the sake of others?

      Saint Leo IX - *Pope Confessor (1002-1054)*



      The “Good Bruno” became Canon of Toul at 15, its Bishop at 25 (in 1027), and governed it for over 20 troubled years. While on a pilgrimage to Rome, he reluctantly accepted the Papal dignity as Leo IX in 1049. In the short period of five years that followed, he showed himself to be the first great medieval reforming Pope. His repeated travels across the Alps to Germany and France, and back down into southern Italy—then being sorely harassed by the Normans—earned him the title of “Peregrinus Apostolicus”, i.e. the *Apostolic Pilgrim*; he called councils and inveighed against the widespread evils of simony (buying and selling what is spiritual for what is temporal), lay investiture and clerical concubinage, examining and deposing unworthy clerics in person.



      During Leo’s pontificate, King Macbeth of Scotland visited Rome, the heresy of Berengarius — denial of “Transubstantiation” — was condemned, the first native bishop of Iceland was consecrated, and Bremen was made an archbishopric with control over the Church in Scandinavia, Greenland and Iceland.



      Leo tried to stem the Norman advance by force of arms, but his troops were defeated and he himself was captured and imprisoned for 9 months. Though released unharmed, he was greatly depressed over the many lives lost in battle. He died in 1054, aged only 52, before the high altar in Saint Peter’s, Rome and was canonized in 1087.
