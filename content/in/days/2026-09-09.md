  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Luke 6: 20-26"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Luke presents the *Sermon on the Plain* as following immediately upon the choosing of the twelve Apostles. Today’s Gospel passage, taken from Luke’s *Sermon on the Plain,* teaches us that true happiness or beatitude lies in the awareness of who we are and what we are supposed to do. The eight beatitudes Jesus gives in Mathew, like the four in Luke, contradict the ideas of “real” happiness prevalent in the Jewish culture of Jesus’ day (and in our modern society as well), according to which wealth, health, power, pleasure and influence are the “true” beatitudes.



      The Beatitudes: Jesus instructs his disciples in the paradoxical blessedness of poverty, hunger, sorrow, and persecution, which contradict our natural expectations in every way. Blessed are those who are poor, hungry, weeping, hated, excluded, insulted, and denounced because in poverty, we recognize God’s reign; in hunger, His providence; in sorrow, true happiness; and in persecution, true joy. Experiencing these miseries opens the way for us to receive the true riches, the food, comfort and acceptance we find only in His love and His presence here and in His Kingdom forever. The Beatitudes are commands for how we should live, and what we should do. What makes one blessed is not simply poverty or hunger or sadness or suffering for one’s Faith, but commitment to Jesus and His spirit of sharing.



      <span class="subhead">Life messages:</span> 1) We need to respond to the challenges of the Beatitudes in our daily life. Millions are starving, persecuted, homeless, and leading hopeless lives. When we reach out to help them, we are living out the Beatitudes. In addition, Jesus tells us that we are serving him in these suffering people. We are also loving our neighbours as Jesus loves us. That is why we are told that we will be judged on the basis of our acts of mercy and charity*(*Mt 25:31-46). 2) Let us also remember that each time we reach out to help the people who are needy, sick, and/or oppressed, we give them the experience of God’s love for them. 3) Just as the Apostles were called to minister to society’s untouchables, so all Christians are called to minister to the untouchables, the discriminated against and the marginalized in our own modern society, that they may meet God’s love in human flesh.

    body: |
      Jesus teaches the Beatitudes, blessing those who are poor, hungry, and sorrowful while warning those who are rich, satisfied, and praised. His words challenge our usual ideas of success and happiness. Jesus tells us that true blessing isn’t found in wealth, comfort, or popularity, but in relying on God and being close to those in need. When we are poor or hungry, we often turn to God for help, finding strength and hope in Him. In contrast, worldly comforts can lead us to forget our need for God. The Beatitudes of Saint Luke invite us to live with humility and compassion, seeking God’s Kingdom above all. Jesus calls us to trust that God is with us in our struggles and to find lasting joy in loving and serving others, rather than in the temporary comforts and applause of the world.

    ref: "St Peter Claver Confessor (1581-1654)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      The son of a Spanish farmer living near Barcelona, St Peter became a Jesuit at the age of 20, and while finishing his novitiate on the island of Majorca, was inspired by St Alphonsus Rodriguez, the holy doorkeeper of the Jesuit college there, to devote his life to missionary work in America. In 1610 he arrived at Cartagena in Central America, was ordained priest, and for the next 44 years made that port city his headquarters for missionary work among the Negro slaves.



      Peter Claver was probably the first white man to take interest in these unfortunates. Accompanied by a small band of interpreters, he would lovingly minister to the sick and the dying in the horribly fetid hold of ships, and hand out fruit and cooling drinks to the half crazed, fear-struck Negroes who had only been permitted on deck as the ship cast anchor. Undeterred by the antagonism of the callous merchants and the misgivings of his own superiors who were apt to look askance at his “indiscreet zeal”, Peter calmly went about, instructing his charges in the elements of the Christian Faith. At a solemn baptismal ceremony in the large town square, he would present each Negro with a small but greatly treasured medal of Jesus and Mary. In all, it has been estimated, he administered the Sacrament to over 300,000 people.



      Peter Claver was canonized at the same time as his friend St Alphonsus Rodriguez in 1888 and declared special Patron of all Catholic missions in 1896.
