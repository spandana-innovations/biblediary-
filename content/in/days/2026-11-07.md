  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Luke 16: 9-15"
    audio: null
    body: |
      After telling the parable of the rascally steward as an example of shrewdness and as a warning against using unjust means for gain, Jesus advises his listeners to make friends with the poor by almsgiving and to be faithful and honest in the little things entrusted to them by God.</span></span></span></span></span>



      The teaching: Jesus advises his followers to imitate the shrewd steward who used money generously to make friends for himself. Jesus suggests that his disciples should show their generosity and mercy by almsgiving: “sell your possessions and give alms” (Luke 12:33). The recipients immediately become friends of the kind donor. It is God’s generosity which makes one rich, and, hence, the money we have is unrighteous in the sense that it is unearned and undeserved. So, God expects us to be generous stewards of His generous blessings. Generosity curtails our natural greed, making almsgiving an act of thanksgiving to God for His generosity. Then Jesus tells us that what we get in Heaven will depend on how we have used the things of the earth and on how faithful we have been in the little things entrusted to us. A slave is the exclusive property of his master, and our Master, God, is the most exclusive of masters. So, serving Him cannot be a part-time job or spare-time hobby; it is full-time job. Finally, Jesus warns the Pharisees that material prosperity is not a sure sign of one’s goodness and God’s blessing, but a sign of God’s mercy and generosity.</span></span></span></span></span>



      Life messages:1) We need to share our blessings with others. Since all our blessings are God’s generous loans to us, we need to be equally generous with others. 2) We need to serve God full-time: Since God owns us totally, we are expected to be at His service doing His holy will all the time. Hence, there is no such thing as a part-time Christian.</span></span></span></span></span>

      Today’s Gospel teaches that if we’re faithful or dishonest in small matters, we’ll likely be the same in bigger ones. God’s kingdom values small, faithful acts more than grand accomplishments. Being faithful in small things prepares us for larger roles. Jesus reminds us that we can’t serve two masters. Money and wealth are important for a good life, but we must be careful they don’t replace God in our lives. Integrity in serving God is crucial. St Paul shares, “I have learned to be content in all situations.” Whether in need or abundance, he finds strength in God. We, too, must practise this discipline to stay true in both good and hard times. The question is: can I be trusted in the small responsibilities I have?</span></span></span>

    saintName: "Saint Willibrord, Bishop, Confessor (658-739)"

    audio: null
    body: |
      Born in Northumbria in 658, St Willibrord was a Benedictine monk who had his early education at the Abbey of Ripon near York. At 20, he went to Ireland and spent 12 years under St Egbert who commissioned him and 11 companions to the mission to Frisia.</span></span></span></span>



      When Willibrord visited Rome in 692 he received the apostolic authorization for his missionary labours. When he visited Rome a second time he was consecrated Archbishop of the Frisians by Pope Sergius I on 21 November 695 and given the name Clement, which means “merciful”. </span></span></span></span>



      He founded a monastery at Utreht (Holland), built a church in the city which he made his cathedral and, in 698 established an abbey at Villa Echternach.</span></span></span></span>



      In 716, Radbod gained possession of Frisia, forcing Willibrord into exile, destroying churches and replacing them with temples dedicated to idols. But soon after his death in 719, Willibrod returned and, with the assistance of St Boniface, undertook the repairs and rebuilding of churches and the work of conversion in Frisia.</span></span></span></span>



      St Willibrord died at Echternach, Luxembourg, on 7 November 739 and was buried at the abbey church which has ever since been a place of pilgrimage.</span></span></span></span>

Others, again, desire it that they may themselves be built up thereby; and that is prudence. Of all these types, only the last two put knowledge to the right use”* </span></span>(St Bernard).</span></span></span></span>
