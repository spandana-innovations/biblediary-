  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Matthew 5:38–42"
    audio: null
    body: |
      The context: During their captivity in Egypt, the Jews became familiar with the crude tribal law of retaliation called *Lex Talionis (*=Tit-for-Tat) given by the ancient lawmaker Hammurabi during the period 2285-2242 BC. In the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus rejects even the concession of milder retaliation allowed by Moses. In its place, Jesus gives a new law of love and grace — and no retaliation.



      *“An eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth.”* Moses instructed the Israelites to follow tit-for-tat retaliation, rather than to wreak total destruction upon their enemies. That is, instead of mutilating or murdering all the members of the offender’s family or tribe, they should discover, then punish by an equal mutilation or harm, only the offender. Later, a milder version of this law was substituted. It demanded monetary compensation, as decided by a judge, in place of physical punishment. Moses also gave the Israelites several laws commanding merciful treatment for the enemy if he also was a Jew (e.g., Lv 19:18).



      The true Christian reaction: For Jesus, retaliation, or even limited vengeance, has no place in the Christian life. Jesus illustrates the Christian approach by giving three examples:




       - “*Turn to him the other cheek”:*Striking someone on the right cheek (with the right hand), requires striking with the back of one’s hand, and, according to Jewish concepts, the blow inflicts more insult than pain. Jesus instructs his followers to forgive the insult gracefully and convert the offender. 2)*“Let him have your cloak as well.”* Jesus instructs his followers that they should show more responsibility and a greater sense of duty than to fight over possessions. 3) “*Go with him two miles.”* A Christian has the duty of responding, even to seemingly unjust demands by helping or serving gracefully not grudgingly*.(Fr Tony: https://frtonyshomilies.com/)*

      The victory of Jesus on the cross over sin and death was a triumph of love. He loved the world so much that He gave His life for the world. Jesus replaced the old “eye for an eye and tooth for a tooth” morality with His teachings of loving our enemies, unconditional forgiveness, and selfless giving. This new teaching is based on God’s relationship with humanity. God does not seek revenge for the wrongs we do but instead forgives. Adopting this divine way, we show them what God is like. This is how we testify to God’s love and reveal our identity as His followers, “By this everyone will know that you are my disciples if you love one another.” (Jn 13:35). Am I true to my identity as a Christian?

    audio: null
    body: |
      The daughter of a peasant named Laurent Cousin in Pibrac near Toulouse, France, Germaine was born with a deformed hand and suffered from *scrofula* (swelling of the glands). While still little, her mother died, and when her father remarried, her stepmother treated her with the utmost cruelty and insisted on her being ostracized, compelling her to sleep with the sheep in the stable. God rewarded the humility and patience with which she bore all manner of suffering and neglect, by granting her a marvellous sense of his presence, and a deep love for the Holy Eucharist and the Blessed Virgin.



      Germaine used to assemble the village children and teach them to love Jesus and Mary; from her scanty allowance of food which consisted of leftovers from the family table she managed to distribute portions to those who appeared to be in still greater misery than herself.



      In reparation for the sacrileges of which the heretics in neighbouring towns were guilty, Germaine subjected herself to additional austerities; people were just beginning to realize her deep-rooted sanctity when God called her to himself in her 22nd year. She was canonized by Pope Pius IX on 29 June 1867. Every year an annual pilgrimage takes place on 15 June to the church in Pibrac where her incorrupt body still rests.
