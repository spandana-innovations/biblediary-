    body: |
      Reflection: 1



      This is a difficult and much discussed text. Let us try to have a simple understanding of it. Both John the Baptist and Jesus faced opposition and violence that ended in their death. They both conquered not by physical force but by the power of a holy and single-minded pursuit. They fought the forces of sin and evil by their humble and loving and self-sacrificing lives. That is the kind of force by which the kingdom of heaven can be taken. The ones who suffer violence without reacting violently have a force that worldly powers cannot defeat.</span></span></span></span></span>



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      John the Baptizer preached the coming of a fiery Messiah. But what he heard about Jesus from prison was that Jesus was a loving, merciful, and forgiving preacher who befriended tax collectors and sinners. Hence, John sent some of his disciples to Jesus to learn whether Jesus was, or was not, the expected Messiah. After sending them back to John to report the actions by which He was fulfilling the Messianic prophecies, Jesus gave the highest compliments to John in today’s Gospel.</span>



      Jesus praised John first as a prophet and second as the expected Elijah. As a prophet, John had God-given wisdom in his mind, God’s truth on his lips, and God-given courage in his heart. He had been heralding the Messiah with the courage of his prophetic convictions. John had lived like the Prophet Elijah who was expected to come just before the Messiah. He had spoken with the same prophetic authority and had corrected the self-righteous, attracting Jewish followers by the hundreds to receive the baptism of repentance. Jesus, however, stressed the fact that His own followers were greater than John because, while John knew only God’s judgment and punishment, we know God’s love, forgiveness and the salvation, given through Jesus. But Jesus warned his followers that they would be persecuted for their trust in God’s Kingdom, and that they would have to use force on their selfish and evil tendencies to reach God’s Kingdom.</span>



      <span class="subhead">Life message:</span> We need to have the courage of our Christian convictions to profess in public what we believe and to practice what the Church teaches.</span>

    body: |
      The call of every follower of Christ is to possess the zeal that passionately seeks the Kingdom of God. This is not physical force but a determined pursuit. St Ambrose says that our ‘violence to the Lord’ is through humility and virtuous living. Christian life demands a relentless quest for holiness, shunning all sinful attachments. St Francis de Sales speaks of a similar “violence” when filled with deep zeal to conquer Geneva back from the clutches of Protestantism, he says: “We will breach the walls of Geneva with ardent prayers and storm the city with mutual charity!” This then is the zeal – the fire – that we need to carry in our hearts. It is a powerful passion that makes no compromise in the values of the Kingdom and makes it willing to suffer any hardship for the sake of the Lord.
  - key: saint
    title: Saint of the Day
    saintName: "Saint Damasus I - Pope Confessor (c.304-384)"
    ref: "Saint Damasus I - Pope Confessor (c.304-384)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Saint Damasus was elected Pope on 1 October 366 to succeed Saint Liberius under whom he had served as a deacon. Hailed by Saint Jerome as “an incomparable man”, “a Virgin Doctor of a Virgin Church”, he was equally famed for his personal holiness and learning as for his great zeal for the purity of the faith and his love of the poor. </span>



      The first to term Rome the ‘Apostolic See’, Damasus emphasized at the Roman Council of 382 that the ecclesiastical supremacy of Rome was based not on the decree of any council but on the words of Christ himself. Saint Jerome attended the synod and stayed on as confidential secretary and friend to Damasus who, in turn, encouraged him in his biblical studies, commissioning him to prepare the Vulgate version of the Bible. </span>



      Also the first Pope to send an apostolic vicar to Eastern Illyricum (Greece) to counter the jurisdictional claims of the patriarchate of Constantinople, Damasus duly enforced the imperial edict that prohibited Roman clergy from soliciting legacies and gifts from widows and orphans and another that confirmed the Pope’s right to decide on all matters ecclesiastical. </span>



      His reverence for the Martyrs led him to discover, restore and redecorate their tombs and the catacombs. We owe much of our knowledge about the early Martyrs to the numerous marble inscriptions composed by him and which are still extant. Damasus, who is also called the Father of the Roman Mass whose ancient liturgy he revised and shortened and who introduced the use of Latin, died on 11 December 384.</span>



      <span class="subhead">Reflection:</span> “The reading of the Holy Scripture is the life of the soul, according to the words of our Lord: “The words which I have said are spirit and life”” (Saint Ambrose).</span>
