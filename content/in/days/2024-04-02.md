  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "John 20:11–18"
    audio: null
    body: |
      A fascinating side of the Easter stories is how the risen Jesus was recognised only gradually by his closest friends. John’s report about Mary Magdalene challenging the gardener to hand back the body of Jesus conveys their stupor and confusion. All they hoped for at first was to be able to reverence his mortal remains. But when he calls Mary by her name (in Hebrew, Miriam,), she makes a joyful discovery: he was truly there, alive! Notice their eagerness to tell each other about him, to share their religious experience. “Go and tell” is a recurring theme in these Easter stories. Magdalene will tell the rest of the group, not just that he is alive, but that he is going back to the Father, his Father and theirs. The unique, special relationship conveyed by the phrase “my Father and your Father” is what the Magdalene recognises and passes on.</span></span></span></span></span>

      Then we have Peter, the church’s leading public witness, trying to help his Jewish people to recognise Jesus as their Messiah and saviour, even those who had called for his death by crucifixion. The kind of Messiah Peter had come to know wanted to call everyone to salvation, with their sins forgiven. All they need do to draw close to God was to welcome Jesus with faith, and receive the gift of his Spirit.</span></span></span></span></span>

      We too, in this Easter week, can come to recognise the various ways that Jesus is still alive among us, not only in the holy eucharist and in the Gospel we read, but also in our fellow-Christians, in the blessings of this world, and in whatever is best in our own selves.

      Today’s gospel begins in sadness and ends in joy. In the early morning Mary Magdalene stood outside the tomb weeping. But it ends with joy when the Lord for whom she was grieving found her, and called her by name. We will find the Lord if we search for him, for he is never far away. He is the good shepherd who calls by name. He came to seek and to save the lost, and we need to let ourselves be met by him, as Magdalene was. She has something to teach us about seeking the Lord even in our grief.</span></span></span></span></span>

      The encounter between Mary Magdalene and the risen Jesus is beautifully portrayed in today’s Gospel. This chapter begins with Mary going to the tomb early in the morning and finding the stone removed. She ran to tell the disciples. Two of them come running to the tomb. They go inside, see the place and return. But Mary refuses to return. Her perseverance is rewarded when Jesus appears to her and calls her by name, ‘Mary.’ With this encounter ‘the ‘weeping’ Mary is transformed into an apostle who proclaims, ‘I have seen the Lord.’ Mary symbolizes all those who love Jesus deeply and are in search of him in the varied circumstances of life and will, in time, surely hear him calling out their names! </span></span></span></span>

      Saint Francis of Paola - </span></span>*Confessor (1416-1507)</span></span>*</span></span>



      The belief of Robert Martolella’s parents that his birth, after years of childlessness, was the result of the intercession of Saint Francis d’Assisi was further reinforced with the healing they obtained through his intercession for their young infant’s eye. The boy was 13 when they fulfilled their vow of having him spend a year in a Franciscan monastery, donning a Franciscan habit, in accordance with medieval practice, where he proved himself a model of extraordinary humility, abnegation and love of prayer. </span></span></span></span>



       Returning from a pilgrimage to Rome, Assisi and other places, he took the name of Francis and began the austere, solitary life in a lonely cave on the sea-coast where, in 1435, he built a chapel and three cells when two companions joined him as anchorites, thus forming the nucleus of the Order of the Hermits of Saint Francis, later known as ‘Minims’, “the least in the House of God”. By 1454 there were monasteries established in southern Italy and Sicily, apart from a Second Order for nuns and a Third Order for those who would live out his charism in the world. Central to the severity of their life was charity, humility and poverty, abstinence covering all meat and animal products. </span></span></span></span>



       Outspoken in reproving even the King of Naples for his evil ways, Francis’ fame reached France where the dying Louis XI sought him out before dying a penitent death. Resultantly, he spent 25 years in France as counsellor to Charles VIII and Louis XII, successive occupants of the throne, restoring peace between France and Spain.</span></span></span></span>



      Francis died on Good Friday 1507, aged 91, leaving behind a legacy of 400 convents, the Order’s five provinces covering Italy, France, Germany and Spain, and was canonized in 1519. </span></span></span></span>
