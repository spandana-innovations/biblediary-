      Reflection: 1



      In his very first preaching in the synagogue of Capernaum, Jesus preaches with authority. There is a man with an evil spirit that identifies Jesus and shouts that Jesus is the holy one of God, thus trying to gain power over Jesus. But God’s power in Jesus cannot be defeated, and he drives out the evil spirit. He uses his power through his word to help and heal very many people. Whenever his word is heard or read, his power heals people and frees them from the forces of evil. His word is the good news for all, that God’s kingdom has come. Is there any uncleanness in us or anything evil that affects us? Let us approach Jesus that he may heal us and help us to live a better life and be better persons.



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      Jesus made the city of Capernaum on the shore of the Sea of Galilee, the centre of the fishing business, headquarters for a preaching, teaching, and healing Messianic ministry. The people were impressed by the authority of Jesus’ teaching. The Old Testament prophets had taught using God’s delegated authority, and the scribes and Pharisees taught quoting Moses, the prophets and the great rabbis, but Jesus taught using Divine authority and knowledge. Perfect knowledge of God, perfect accomplishment of God’s will, and absolute confidence in God were the sources of Jesus’ authority.



      The second part of today’s Gospel describes a healing by exorcism which Jesus performed in the synagogue. We are told how Jesus, using Divine authority, cast out the devil by just one command: “*Be silent, and come out of him!”* In first-century Palestine, most sicknesses, especially mental illness, were considered to be the result of demonic possession, and both Jewish and pagan exorcists used lengthy procedures and physical force in their exorcisms. When Jesus commanded the Evil One to depart, the evil one did so at once, and in its rush to depart, convulsed the man. Thus, Jesus demonstrated Divinity as God’s Messiah, the Saviour more powerful than the demon.



      <span class="subhead">Life messages:</span> 1) Our Faith is based on the Divinity of Christ, which is proved by miracles; these, in turn, give authority and validity to what Jesus teaches and promises. Hence, let us accept Jesus’ teachings even if some of them are mysteries beyond our reach. Let us read the authoritative word of God every day and assimilate it into our lives. 2) In our illnesses, let us confidently approach Jesus, the Healer, with trusting Faith, then go to the doctors who serve as the current instruments of Jesus’ healing ministry in our midst.

    audio: null
    body: |
      Saint Hilary hailed from a noble Gallo-Roman family living in Poitiers, then an important city in the western part of central France. His parents, both pagans, saw to it that he received a thorough education in all the profane sciences of the day, none of which could furnish him with a satisfactory answer as to the destiny of man, his quest finally leading him to study the Christian scriptures whose conception of God in turn led him to seek baptism.



      The conversion of a man of his age [30 years] and stature naturally aroused much interest; he so zealously defended and propagated his new-found Faith, both by pen and by word of mouth, that, although married and the father of a growing daughter, was elected Bishop of Poitiers in 350.



      The Church was at that time afflicted by the devastating heresy of Arius of Alexandria: a denial of the divinity of Christ, a concept that was gaining ground, threatening to make deep inroads also into Gaul. The Archbishop of Arles in Southern France, an avowed Arian, tried to get the Synod of Beziers (356) to ratify the faulty doctrine. Hilary went there to defend the truth but was prevented from doing so by the Arian majority and, shortly afterwards, was banished by the Arian Emperor Constantius to distant Phrygia in Asia Minor.



      Recognized as one of the most outstanding and intrepid defenders of the Faith, Hilary used his three-year exile to study the Greek Fathers and to write his great thesis on the Trinity. In 359 he attended the Synod of Seleucia at the invitation of the Eastern Bishops and later went to Constantinople with the Synod’s delegates. But the Emperor persisted in his Arian viewpoint and at the suggestion of his advisers who were dismayed by Hilary’s persuasive powers had the bishop sent back to Gaul, where in 361 he made his triumphal re-entry into his See.



      Hilary died in 367 or 368 and was proclaimed Doctor of the Church by Pope Pius IX in 1851.
