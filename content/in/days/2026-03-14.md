      Reflection: 1



      The Pharisee’s prayer is all about himself, proudly glorifying himself. He considers that he has done enough and more of good deeds, and boasts about them. He criticizes and condemns everybody else. But his prayer was not pleasing to God. The tax-collector, on the other hand, humbly admits his faults and asks God for mercy. His prayer was pleasing to God, and he went home justified, but the other one was not justified. The tax-collector lives in the truth and God’s grace is with him; for God has made him righteous. Jesus told this parable to those who were convinced of their uprightness and looked down on others. Such self-proclaimed saints will be rejected by God whereas those who humbly confess their sins will be justified by God. No one can earn anything from God by merit, but all receive everything from the Father in heaven. Through his deceitful prayer the Pharisee is only deceiving himself. How sincere and humble is our prayer? Let us admit our sins before God and humbly pray for His mercy. </span></span></span></span>



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      The main theme of today’s Gospel is that true humility must be the hallmark of our prayers. However, the central focus of today’s parable is not prayer, but rather pride, humility, and the role of grace in our salvation. The parable was mainly intended to convict the Pharisees who proudly claimed they obeyed all the rules and regulations of the Jewish law, while they actually ignored the Mosaic precepts of mercy and compassion. Through this parable of Jesus, Luke was reminding his Gentile listeners that God values the prayer of any humble and contrite heart.</span></span></span>



      In the parable, Jesus tells us about two men who went to pray, a Pharisee and a tax-collector. The Pharisee stood in the very front of the Temple, distancing himself from his inferiors, and explained to God his meticulous observance of the Mosaic Law, at the same time despising the publican. But the tax-collector, standing far off, would not even lift up his eyes to Heaven, but beat his breast, saying, “*God, be merciful to me a sinner!” *Jesus declared that only the humble tax-collector went home justified in the eyes of God.</span></span></span>



      <span class="subhead">Life messages: </span>1) We need to evict the Pharisee and revive the publican in each one of us. There is a big dose of the Pharisee’s pride in us and a small dose of the tax-collector’s humility. Hence, we have to make a pilgrimage from pride to humility, realizing the truth that if we are not sensitive to other people, we are not sensitive to God.</span></span></span>



      2) Let us have the correct approach in our prayer life. For most of us, prayer means asking God for something when we are in need. We conveniently forget the more important aspects of prayer: adoration, praise, contrition, and thanksgiving. If we have forgotten God through our years of prosperity, how can we expect Him to take notice of us when something goes wrong? Yet, even there His mercy welcomes us. Our day’s work and our day’s recreation, if offered for the honour and glory of God, are prayers pleasing in His sight. </span></span></span>



      *(Fr Tony: https://frtonyshomilies.com/)*
  - key: reflection
    title: Reflection
    ref: null
    audio: null
    body: |
      Pride can lead to devastating consequences, as highlighted in the parable of the Pharisee and the Tax Collector. Pride harms life, breaks hearts, and destroys reputations. The Pharisee boasts of his achievements, while the Tax Collector humbly acknowledges his need for mercy. Prayer acts as a mirror, reflecting our true selves; it reveals whether we see our accomplishments or our shortcomings. Our attitude shapes this reflection. Do we cling to pride, pushing God aside, or do we embrace humility, recognising our dependence on Him? Let us choose humility, giving way to others, and avoiding the collisions that pride brings into our lives. In doing so, we can foster deeper connections and truly reflect the love of God.</span></span></span>

    audio: null
    body: |
      Daughter of Count Dietrich of Westphalia and Reinhild of Denmark, St Matilda was brought up in the Benedictine convent at Herford (central Germany), where her grandmother was Abbess. Here she became known for her rare talents and virtues. At 14 she married Henry the Fowler of Saxony who later was chosen to succeed Conrad as King of Germany. As Queen she proved herself a model of piety and prudence, and was ever intent on succouring the oppressed and the poor. </span></span></span></span>



      Left a widow in 936, Matilda redoubled her prayers, mortifications and almsgiving, and founded numerous hospitals, churches and Benedictine abbeys. But this liberality so exasperated two of her sons, Otto and Henry, that they banished her from court and deprived her of all her property until misfortune overtook them in turn and they became reconciled with her and begged for forgiveness.</span></span></span></span>



      Of her children, the eldest son became Otto the Great, Emperor of Germany; another son was St Bruno, Archbishop of Cologne; a daughter Gerberga married Louis IV of France, and Hedwig became the mother of Hugh Capet, the founder of the Capetian dynasty.</span></span></span></span>



      She died on 14 March 968 at the monastery of Quedlinburg and was buried there with Henry.</span></span></span></span>
