  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Mark 5:21-43"
    audio: null
    body: |
      What motivated the two persons – Jairus and the woman suffering from haemorrhage – to go to Jesus in their utter desperation?



      It was their firm faith in the power of Jesus. The Gospel tells us that, as a ruler of the synagogue, Jairus was one of the most important and respected men in the country. Now he is one of the many figures in the Gospels who are made immortal. The person of Jairus reveals two things worthy of reflection, among others.



      First, he was a man with an open mind and spirit. When all possible roads had been closed, he was ready to experiment with something new. He knew that even though nobody was able to help, perhaps this leader, Jesus, reputed to be a healer, could help.



      Second, Jairus besought Jesus, saying, “My little daughter is at the point of death. Come and lay your hands on her so that she may be made well and live.” What a tremendous affirmation of faith! It was a clear indication of a father’s love for his child and of his sense of need. But those words also carry valid and arresting suggestions of the eternal truth that whatever Jesus lays His hands upon lives.



      Today, how many millions are spiritually sick and dying, who have not the faith, humility and confidence of Jairus to cast themselves at the feet of Jesus and ask Him to cure them? If only they would, like the suffering woman, push their way towards Christ through the throng of worldly pride, worldly interests and worldly pleasures and say to Him, “Come, Lord, and lay your hands upon me so that I may be made well and live.”



      (Philip John SSP & Premdas SSP, New Horizon Homilies, ST PAULS, Mumbai, India)
  - key: reflection
    title: Reflection
    ref: null
    audio: null
    body: |
      In the Gospel, Mark uses the sandwich technique which weaves two stories together. It begins with the story of Jairus, a desperate father who pleads with Jesus for the cure of his 12-year-old sick daughter. Then it is interrupted by Jesus’ encounter with a woman who was suffering for 12 years. The first story resumes where the second story ends. One common element in both the stories is the touch. In one story, Jesus was touched by the woman, and in the other Jesus touched the girl. Like the woman, we can touch Jesus in faith and receive his blessings. We must take matters into our own hands and approach him with faith. At the same time, Jesus can also touch us in mercy as he did to the little girl. He is ready to reach out and touch us when we are in need, provided we allow him to touch us. Let us remain connected to Jesus and experience his divine touch in our life!

      First Martyrs of the Church at Rome (64 - 314)

      In the early years of the Church, persecutions kept breaking out like rash across the vast Roman Empire. But given the present situation, we hardly give those terrible persecutions so much as a thought. One of the first major persecutions began under Nero in the year 64 A.D. and was followed by one under Domitian in 95 A.D. In 107 A.D., Trajan tested the faith, hope and love of the first Christians with another terrible ordeal by fire, prison and the sword. In 135 A.D., Hadrian followed his example and Marcus Aurelius followed suit in 180 A.D. In 222 A.D., Septimus Severus did his best to shake the constancy of the early Christians, followed by Decius in 250 A.D. and Valerian in 257 A.D., the last attempt being that of Diocletian in 303 A.D.

      The Church today offers us this Commemoration of the First Martyrs to help us always bear in mind that the blood of martyrs is first and foremost the seed of faith; secondly, to have us prove, as they did, that our faith is a living, vibrant reality; and thirdly, consider the possibility of someday being called upon to bear witness as they did—to our faith and our hope, to our love for God and for our fellowmen, as so many of our contemporaries here in India itself and elsewhere have done.

      Reflection: “To die for the truth is not to die merely for one’s faith, or one’s country, it is to die for the world. The blood of martyrs is shed in confirmation of the noblest claim — the claim to feed upon immortal truth, to walk with God, and be divinely free” (Cowper).
