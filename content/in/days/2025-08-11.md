    body: |
      Reflection: 1



      In today’s Gospel, very uncharacteristically Jesus works a miracle for himself and his disciples. The early Christians, still associated with Judaism, were doubtful if they should pay the Temple tax. Jesus makes it clear that they are not bound to pay it, but should still pay it so as not to offend the Jews. This is a practical lesson for us: in some situations, for the sake of peace and harmony, we should do want we are no strictly bound to do. This is not a question of doing what is forbidden. If we believe in God and trust in Him, He provides for us what we need: see how a fish the disciples caught brought in its mouth the money they needed.



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      The first part of today’s Gospel gives Jesus’ second prediction of His sufferings, death, and Resurrection. The second part is Jesus’ explanation of why He pays the Temple tax. Today’s Gospel tells us that Jesus’ disciples were “distressed” by their master’s repeated reminders of a coming shameful death as a heretic and lawbreaker. They were distressed because the reminders shattered their dream of ruling Israel after Jesus had conquered the Romans and re-established the Davidic kingdom. They did not understand that their master would be dying to liberate the whole of mankind from the bondage of sin. In the second part of today’s Gospel, Peter assures the Temple tax officials that the Master, Jesus, is a devout Jew and, hence, pays the Temple tax. All Jewish males 20 years old or older had to pay a half-shekel (roughly equivalent to two days’ wages), as Temple tax for the upkeep of the Temple and its sacrifices. When they reached Peter’s home, Jesus instructed Peter to go fishing, open the mouth of the first fish he caught and, with the coin he would find there, pay both Peter’s and his own tax. Jesus’ reason was that they were to give good example to others, even though, as the Son of God, Jesus was legally exempted from paying any type of tax to anyone. The Gospel passage foreshadows a dilemma that would be experienced by the first century Jewish Christians as to whether they should continue to pay the Temple tax meant for the Jews.



      <span class="subhead">Life messages: </span>



      1) Let us express our gratitude to Jesus our Savior for the price of suffering and death He paid for our sins. We can do this by avoiding all occasions of sin, by offering our pains and sufferings as atonement for our sins, and by helping others sacrificially.



      2) We should obey the laws of the Church and of our country as loyal Christians and loyal citizens and contribute to the needs of the Church and its mission by our tithing, while we help the government by paying our taxes.

      Would you believe that a fish you will be catching would have a coin in its mouth that will satisfy an external need? We may not, but small versions of these kinds of miracles do occur frequently in our lives. While not so extraordinary, what about an intuition that caused you to do something, then later you realized it had an unexpected benefit? Isn’t it highly possible that your faith influences your intuitions? Divinity is always ready to spiritually provide for those things that matter most in our lives. As children of God, couldn’t your belief include that Christ provides what we need for ourselves and others by being one step ahead on our journey? I believe He places beautiful gifts on our paths to discover, utilize, and fill us with joy.

    audio: null
    body: |
      When St Francis preached the Lenten sermons in the church of St George at Assisi (Italy) in 1212, he so inspired the 18-year-old Chiara (meaning “light” or “illustrious”) of the noble Favaronne family, that she determined to follow the Poverello in his life of Christian poverty. She secretly slipped out of her parental castle on the night of Palm Sunday (March 18-19), and, accompanied by her aunt Bianca and another companion, made her way through the forest to the little chapel of the Portiuncula in the valley, where she exchanged her rich clothing for a coarse tunic and veil, and, shorn of her long, golden hair, vowed her life to Christ’s service in utter poverty.



      Within a fortnight, Clare was joined by her younger sister (St) Agnes. The young Order of “Poor Clares” began to grow rapidly, and during the next few years her own mother, (Blessed) Ortolana, another sister, Beatrice, and her aunt Bianca also placed themselves under her direction. Foundations were established in many countries of Europe during her 40-years tenure of office, mainly for the care and education of poor girls.



      Cardinal Ugolini, the new Order’s protector, drew up a rule in 1219, based on the Benedictine one and forbidding communal poverty. Her firm insistence on St Francis’ ideal of absolute poverty and total dependence on alms, finally won from Pope Innocent IV, two days before her death, the solemn confirmation of the definitive rule.



      Clare’s funeral was attended by the Pope and his entire court, and two years later the holy foundress who was solemnly canonized in 1255.
