      Jesus sat down opposite the treasury, and watched the crowd putting money into the treasury. Many rich people put in large sums. A poor widow came and put in two small copper coins, which are worth a penny. Then he called his disciples and said to them, “Truly I tell you, this poor widow has put in more than all those who are contributing to the treasury. For all of them have contributed out of their abundance; but she, out of her poverty has put in everything she had, all she had to live on” (Mark 12:41-44). Like the widow, we are called to love God with all of our heart, all of our mind, and all of our strength. That is to love God passionately, thoughtfully and practically, as we reflected last Sunday. Like the widow in the gospel, God wants us to use our time, talents and treasure for His glory and for the benefit of our brothers and sisters. Do we do this? How do we spend our money? Do we give a fair share of our income to the needy and to charities? There are four different types of giving. The first is called grudge giving. I hate to part with this twenty dollars but I will. The second is shame giving. I must match whatever the Roberts family is giving. The third is calculated giving. We part with our money with a sense of favours to come. Bingos, Las Vegas nights, and raffle tickets fit in very nicely in this category. The final category is thanksgiving. I part with my funds precisely because God has been so wonderfully generous to me. The widow’s story also reminds us that we should give not only what we have but also what we are to ministries to fulfil the mission of the Church.



      *(Kindly your Spirit, Fr John Pichappilly)*
  - key: reflection
    title: Reflection
    ref: null
    audio: null
    body: |
      Jesus appreciates the offering of the poor widow over that of all the others as he was touched by her humble attitude. It makes a big difference when someone is ready to sacrifice their comfort, time, energy and money for the sake of others. As American author John C. Maxwell aptly says, “People may hear your words, but feel your attitude”. A similar attitude we see in the First Reading. The widow in Zarephath prepares bread for Elijah from a handful of flour and a little oil, trusting in God’s promise through Elijah. God so blessed her faith and generosity that her jar of meal and oil didn’t run out for many days. The purity of intention and quality of giving satisfy the receiver and multiply the resources of the giver. We see the total self-giving of Jesus for the sake of all. As chosen people, we represent Jesus in the world, and are responsible to give the best for the cause of the common good. Mother Teresa said, “A life not lived for others is not a life”. Every act of kindness has a redeeming effect. If we can make someone happy and reduce their sorrow, it would be a great offering. None of our achievements, possessions and even good works, have any lasting value if not directed by correct motivation and purpose. Let us then reflect on the quality of our giving, our service and contributions.

    audio: null
    body: |
      At a critical time in the Church’s history, when the Western Empire was disintegrating and the Eastern Church was profoundly disturbed by dogmatic controversies and speculations, God raised to the throne of Peter in 440 a farsighted and wise Pontiff, Leo I; he was to become one of the three saintly Popes whom we honour with the title of “the Great”, Gregory I in the 6th century and Nicholas I in the 9th being the other two.

      In order to maintain the purity of the Church’s doctrine, Leo took sharp measures against the subtle infiltration of heresies into Italy and Spain, and in the East condemned Monophysitism or Eutychianism. In his famous dogmatic letter “Tome to Flavian”, Archbishop of Constantinople, he clearly set forth the Church’s doctrine that Christ is one person but possesses two distinct natures, the divine and the human; an early example of an infallible Papal pronouncement. At the 4th Ecumenical Council of Chalcedon in 461, some 600 Eastern bishops, assembled under the presidency of the Papal legates, officially acclaimed this great document as the Church’s true teaching thus: “Peter has spoken by Leo.”

      Pope St Leo I looked upon his office as that of Christ himself and strove to become “all things to all”. Combining prayer with labour, he proved himself a true father to his people. That he saw himself chiefly as a teacher is evident from the fact that his teachings remain the greatest hallmark of his pontificate.
