  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Luke 3:1-6"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Today’s Gospel, on the calling of John the Baptist, invites us to consider how the world handles the Christmas season – preparing decorations, food and gifts. As Christians, we need to be careful that all the noise and business of the Christmas season does not drown out the voice in the wilderness, telling us to prepare for the Lord. It is in this season of Advent that each of us should be asking ourselves: Are we hearing what God wants us to hear? How do we prepare for the Advent of Jesus’ Birth? God is coming and we need to repent. That is what John the Baptist proclaimed. The problem is that many people do not feel the need to repent – they don’t know they need to turn to God. As Christians, preparing for the Birth of Jesus is to turn to God, to return to God. To repent of our sins and ask for forgiveness. To many people, they feel the judgement of God is condemnation, they feel guilty for their sins and the guilt has put a distance between them and God. Most of us have a tendency to flee from judgement – but the truth is, we cannot. The world judges and condemns and places that characteristic on God. But God is a God of Love and Grace! He wants us to return to Him. Through the cacophony of worldly Christmas celebrations, do we hear God calling us? One of the largest barriers to true repentance is many in the world do not believe God exists. There is no human reasoning that will lead someone to believe God exists and wants to save us. This skepticism is the same today as it was during the time of the Apostle Luke.



      *(Sermon Reflections, Pastor Dave)*

      Luke identifies a specific point in history for Jesus’ coming. He also stated explicitly that the Word of God came to one particular person, John, who preached a distinct message rooted in prophetic heritage.   - key: saint
    title: Saint of the Day
    saintName: "The Immaculate Conception (Solemnity)"
    ref: "The Immaculate Conception (Solemnity)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      “Although in the most pure womb of Mary there was but one grain of corn which was Jesus Christ, yet it is called a heap of wheat, because all the elect were virtually contained in it,” says Saint Ambrose. In the light of age-old tradition, it is significant to note Mary’s own assertion, “I am the Immaculate Conception” to Saint Bernadette Soubirous at Lourdes (1858).

      Thologians of medieval times argued that since “all men have sinned in Adam” (Rom 5:12), the concept of the Immaculate Conception detracted from the belief that Christ was universal Saviour. Conceding only that she was sanctified in the womb, they averred that if Mary was untouched by the stain of original sin even for an instant, she obviously did not need the redeemer—an assumption that clearly violates the premise that the Lord actually came to save entire humanity. Franciscan theologian John Duns Scotus from Oxford counters: “Mary more than anyone else would have contracted original sin . . . . if the grace of the Mediator had not prevented this. Just as others needed Christ so that the sin already contracted should be remitted for them through his merit, so Mary had even greater need of a prevenient Mediator lest there be sin to be contracted and lest she contract it” [3.1, Per illud patet” (Vives 14:171); cf. C. Balic, I.D. Scoti theologiae marianae elementa (Sibenik 1933)35]. Thus, the feast of the Immaculate Conception celebrates the dawn of the New Covenant and the gracious preparation of God’s people to receive their Saviour and Lord.



      <span class="subhead">Reflection:</span> *“We declare, announce, and define that the doctrine which states that the Blessed Virgin Mary was preserved, in the first instant of her conception, by a singular grace and privilege of God Omnipotent and because of the merits of Jesus Christ the Saviour of the human race, free from all stain of original sin, is revealed by God and must be believed firmly and with constancy by all the faithful” (Pope Pius IX: Ineffabilis Deus: 8 December 1854).*
