  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Mathew 1: 1-17"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Starting with a genealogy was the Jewish way of beginning a biography because the Jews gave importance to the purity of the lineage which made each of them part of God’s Chosen People. For a noble Jew, the line must be traceable back through five generations, and for a Jewish priest traceable back to Aaron. Matthew presents Jesus’ human ancestry, indicating that salvation history has reached its climax with the birth of the Son of God through Mary by the working of the Holy Spirit. The Jewish genealogies followed the male line. Hence, Joseph, as the husband of Mary, was the legal father of Jesus, and the legal father was on a par with the real father regarding rights and duties. Thus, it is through Joseph, His legal father, that Jesus became the descendant of David. Since the Jews generally married within their clan, the early Fathers of the Church believed that Mary also belonged to David’s family. As a legal son of David, Jesus is the fulfilment of prophecies. The genealogy of Jesus Christ in today’s Gospel is carefully arranged into three groups of fourteen generations each. The three groups are based on 1) the rise of Israel to a great kingdom under David and Solomon, 2) the fall of the nation in the Babylonian exile and 3) the raising of the nation after the exile. The three groups symbolically represent the creation of man in God’s image, the loss of man’s greatness in Adam’s sin, and the regaining of that greatness through Christ Jesus.



      <span class="subhead">Life messages:</span> 1) We need to accept, support, lift up, and correct the bad members of our family, acknowledging the truth that every family has some black sheep. Jesus’ genealogy mentions a harlot named Rahab, an adulteress named Tamar and a Moabite Gentile woman named Ruth. We need to remember that God can bring good out of the worst persons and circumstances. We need to appreciate our membership in the Divine family of God through Baptism and behave as holy children of a Holy God.

      The genealogy of Jesus contains the names of five women. However, in the case of Tamar, Rahab, Ruth, and the wife of Uriah, their sons are called the sons of the respective husbands. Only in the case of Jesus is He called the son of Mary whose husband was Joseph. Such importance given to Mary by Matthew demonstrates her place not just in the life of Jesus but in the life of the Early Church. Christmas is just seven days away, and the Gospel reading is zooming in our attention to the crib, and today it is specifically to Mary. Let us try to feel with this young girl who accepted the challenging and critical task of saying ‘yes’ to God’s angel. Shall we try to spend some time meditating on her emotions, feelings, yearning but also her questions she went through just seven days before giving birth to the Son of God?

    audio: null
    body: |
      Saint Wivina (also called *Vivina* or *Wiwina*) was a 12th-century Benedictine abbess and consecrated virgin from Oisy in Flanders (today in Belgium), born in 1103 and died around 1170. From a young age she felt a deep call to follow Christ and by age 15 had already determined to forsake worldly life. Although known for her beauty and sought in marriage by a nobleman, she steadfastly refused such offers, praying and fasting for his recovery when he became gravely ill after her rejection — and tradition holds that he was restored through her intercession.



      At 23, Wivina embraced a hermit’s life in the woods near Brussels. Her reputation for holiness drew disciples, and in 1120 Count Godfrey of Brabant granted land for a monastic foundation. There she established the Convent of Le Grand Bigard (Grand-Bijgaarden) and became its first abbess, placing the community under the guidance of the nearby Benedictine Monastery of Affligem. Under her leadership, the convent flourished as a center of prayer and religious discipline.



      After her death, many miracles were reported at her tomb. Her relics were eventually enshrined in Notre-Dame du Sablon in Brussels, and her cult was confirmed by Pope Urban VII in 1625, with liturgical celebration approved in the early 20th century. Her feast is traditionally observed on December 17.
