    ref: "John 6:52–59"

    body: |
      Reflection: 1



      It is only natural that the Jews wondered how Jesus, a man living and talking to them, can given them his flesh to eat. Jesus reconfirms that unless one eats his flesh and drinks his blood, one cannot have real life. But anyone who does so has eternal life, and Jesus will raise him or her up on the last day. Whoever feeds on him will because of him. The Eucharist is no mere symbol but truly the Body and Blood of Christ, and partaking of them makes us also partake in his everlasting life. The Holy Eucharist, received with deep faith, preserves us from eternal death. It is the Bread of life.



      *— Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      The Jewish audience for Jesus’ teaching on the Bread of Life were scandalized at his statement that he was going to give them his Flesh to eat, for it suggested to them cannibalism, forbidden in the Jewish Scriptures. Hence, they wanted to know how Jesus could give his Flesh to eat as a means to gain Eternal Life. Jesus asserted that it was a must for them to eat his Body and drink his Blood if they were to receive Divine Life, Eternal Life, and resurrection from the dead. There is no way to interpret Jesus’ words as “simply symbolic,” which would mean that receiving Communion is only a metaphor, and not really eating and drinking the Body and Blood of Christ. Jesus stresses very forcefully that it is necessary for us to receive him in the Blessed Eucharist in order to share in Divine Life and to develop the life of grace we have received in Baptism. *“We receive Jesus Christ in Holy Communion to nourish our souls and to give us an increase of grace and the gift of eternal life” (St. Pius X Catechism, # 289).“Really sharing in the body of the Lord in the breaking of the Eucharistic bread, we are taken up into communion with him and with one another.” (Vatican II, Lumen Gentium, 7).*Jesus adds that eating his Body and drinking his Blood are essential for abiding in him, which is, on this earth, the beginning of the Eternal Life of Heaven. Communion with Jesus enables us to start enjoying Eternal Life with God here on earth, while resurrection gives us eternal life with God forever. St. Thomas Aquinas gives this explanation: *“The Word gives life to our souls, but the Word made Flesh nourishes our souls.” (“Commentary on St. John, in loc.”).*Life message: 1) We need to receive Holy Communion with the full awareness that we are abiding in Jesus, carrying him wherever we go. Hence, we are expected to radiate to all around us the love, the mercy, the spirit of service, and the forgiveness of Jesus.



      *(Fr Tony: https://frtonyshomilies.com/)*

      Sometimes we interpret the Eucharist as merely symbolic and thus disconnect ourselves from truth. In the Gospel, Jesus does not mince words to state that in the Eucharist we truly, concretely partake of his body and blood. As we partake of him in his flesh, his hidden life within us sows the seed of immortality in us. Just as a spark of fire is placed in straw to preserve the fire, so Christ’s body and blood, received into our bodies, preserves us for eternal life. This Heavenly Bread has indeed the power to drive out death and also our weaknesses, and thus preserves us from temptations and cures us of our sinfulness. Is this the faith we bring to our reception of Christ’s body and blood?

    saintName: "St Peter Nolasco, Confessor (c.1182-1256)"
    ref: "St Peter Nolasco, Confessor (c.1182-1256)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Of noble birth in southern France near Toulouse, St Peter Nolasco, who was canonized in 1628 by Pope Urban VIII, was from his youth known for his sincere piety and his great charity towards the poor and the unfortunate. He took part, for some time, in the crusade against the Albigenses before migrating to Spain, where he was appointed tutor to the young King, James of Aragon and formed an intimate friendship with St Raymond of Penyafort, then a canon at Barcelona in Spain. In 1218, Our Lady directed him in a vision to found a new religious order specifically for the redemption of the Christian captives, many of whom, on account of the brutality experienced at the hands of the cruel Moors, were giving up their faith in despair.



      The first monks of the new Order which had the active backing of the King and of St Raymond, were some noblemen who belonged to a charitable confraternity in Barcelona. They now came to be known as the Mercedarians, or members of the Order of Our Lady of Mercy, membership in which was of three kinds: Knight, Priest and Lay Brother; their vows included, besides the three regular ones, a fourth one, viz., to go into infidel territory and if need be to become a hostage in Moorish hands in order to ransom the faithful. For many this ended in martyrdom.



      Peter, who remained a layman, was “Commander-General” until his resignation in 1249. His mission as “Ransomer” took him repeatedly into Moorish territory (including Africa), in order to arrange for the ransom of Christian prisoners, whereby, when he died in 1256, a total of 2,718 slaves had been liberated. His work was carried on later at such a pace that by 1632 the number had swelled to 490,736!
