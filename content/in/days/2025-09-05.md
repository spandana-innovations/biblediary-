  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Luke 5:33–39"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Reflection: 1



      We are at ease with our customs and practices and find it difficult to accept anything different. The Jews fast much more frequently than the Christians, and they ask Jesus why his disciples do not fast. In his answer Jesus points out that with him a new era has begun, the era of salvation. He, the bridegroom has come and the wedding feast has begun. This is not the time for fasting. When the wedding feast is over and the bridegroom is taken away, at the time of the suffering and death of Jesus, they will fast. With Jesus something new has begun and it cannot be put into old wine-skins which cannot hold it. Only those who are drunk with the old wine and have closed their minds will say, “The old is good” and eject the new.



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      Today’s Gospel passage gives Jesus’ reply to the question asked by a few disciples of John the Baptist about fasting and feasting. Prayer, fasting and almsgiving were three cardinal works of Jewish religious life. Hence, John’s disciples wanted to know why they and the Pharisees fasted while Jesus’ disciples were seen feasting with him and never fasting.



      Jesus’ reply: Jesus responds to their sincere question using three metaphors: the metaphor of the “children of the bridal chamber,” the metaphor of patching torn cloth, and the metaphor of wineskins. First, Jesus compares the apostles with the children of the bridal chamber, the selected friends of the bride and groom who feasted in the company of bride and groom during a week of honeymoon. Nobody expected them to fast. Jesus explains that the apostles will fast when Jesus, the bridegroom, has been taken away from them. In the same way, we are to welcome both the joys of Christian life and the crosses it offers us. Jesus uses the comparisons of the danger of using new, unshrunk cloth to make a patch for an old garment and of using old wineskins to store freshly fermented wine, to tell the questioners that they must have more elastic and open minds and larger hearts to understand and follow the new ideas they are hearing, which are in many cases different from the traditional Jewish teachings.



      <span class="subhead">Life messages: </span>



      1) We need to be adjustable Christians with open and elastic minds: The Holy Spirit, working actively in the Church and guiding the Church’s teaching authority, enables the Church to have new visions, new ideas and new adaptations and to replace old ways of worship with new. So, we should have the generosity and good will to follow the teachings of the Church.



      2) At the same time, we need the assistance of the Holy Spirit, who works through the Church’s *magisterium*to interpret and apply Scripture – the Old Testament revelations and the New Testament teachings — and Sacred Tradition to our daily lives.

    title: Reflection

    body: |
      For most of us, it is hard to accept changes and new ideas, because it may cost us a lot. We are happy with the old because it simply makes us feel more comfortable and more secure. The Pharisees criticized Jesus for not being ascetical enough, supposing that Jesus and his disciples should be fasting and praying more. This temptation to judge others based on what we “see and observe” is evident in today’s Gospel. Jesus invites His listeners to look at situations differently and be open to the “new”. This may be an uncomfortable invitation, as we feel comfortable with our usual ways of doing things. Jesus is challenging the Pharisees and us to be open to other ways of doing things and not to judge others too quickly. Let us pray to the Lord, to grant us a heart of understanding to accept others as they are.

    ref: "Saint Teresa of Kolkata (Religious, Founder) (1910-1997)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Teresa was born in Skopje, Yugoslavia and Christened Agnes Gonxha Bojaxhiu. The youngest of three children who lost their father in their childhood, she joined Loreto Abbey in Ireland at 18, taking the name “Teresa”, choosing St Therese of Lisieux as her patron. Five months later, i.e., in 1929, she came to Calcutta (now Kolkata), where she taught for sixteen years at St Mary’s School, Entally and mastered the local language, Bengali.



      In 1946 she got what she referred to as “the call within the call”, viz., “to be God’s love in action to the poorest of the poor”. In 1948, clad in a coarse blue-bordered white sari, a crucifix pinned at her shoulder and equipped with a 6-month intensive training course in ‘nursing’, she nursed the sick and the dying with utmost tenderness, lovingly cleansed their maggot-infested wounds, washed their naked bodies, cradled them into the Lord’s peace the way only someone divinely possessed could.



      In 1952, she started “Nirmal Hriday” (pure heart), a home for dying destitute, followed by the opening of “Shishu Bhavan” for children; in 1957, “Shanti Nagar” for lepers; in 1963, a parallel institute for men, the “Missionary Brothers of Charity”. Winner of the Nobel Peace Prize (1979) and the Bharat Ratna (1980), Teresa walked with royalty without losing the common touch and was comfortable with Pope, politician and pauper alike.



      She was canonized in 2016.
