      Reflection: 1



      The parable of the prodigal son is in fact the parable of the loving father who loved both his sons. </span></span></span></span>The younger one took his share of the property and all he had, went to a far country and </span></span></span></span>spent everything in reckless living. Then he realized his fault, repented and returned to the </span></span></span></span>father, considering himself unworthy to be called his son. The father welcomed him with a </span></span></span></span>great celebration. But the elder son who was always with the father refused to join the celebration. </span></span></span></span>He reacted to his father: “I am all right; he is not all right. Yet you celebrate his return this way, </span></span></span></span>while you never made even a little celebration for me.” </span></span></span></span>



      This is a rather common attitude of people, to justify oneself and not to recognize one’s </span></span></span></span>own failures, while finding fault with others. The younger son, after all his mistakes, loved and </span></span></span></span>respected his father; the elder son who never left the father never loved him or his own brother. </span></span></span></span>He stood angry outside, while the one who went away repented of his sin and is in the father’s </span></span></span></span>house and happy. That is to say, the former tax collector or sinner is now happy while the Pharisees </span></span></span></span>and scribes are grumbling outside. Staying with the father or simply doing his bidding is no guarantee </span></span></span></span>of loving the father or doing his will. In this matter, where do we stand? </span></span></span></span>



      *— Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP</span></span>*</span></span>



      Reflection: 2



      Chapter 15 of Luke’s Gospel has been called “the Gospel within the Gospel,” because it is the distilled essence of the Good News about our Heavenly Father. The whole chapter is essentially one complete parable, the “Parable of the Lost and Found,” with three illustrations: the story of the lost sheep, the story of the lost coin and the story of the lost son. These parables remind us that we have a God Who welcomes sinners and forgives their sins when they return to Him with genuine contrition, resolved to reform. In addition, He is always in search of His lost and straying children.



      The lost son: This parable speaks about the deep effects of sin, the self-destruction of hatred, and the infinite mercy of God. This is a story of love, of conflict, of deep heartbreak, and of ecstatic joy. The scene opens on a well-to-do Jewish family. With the immaturity of a spoiled brat, the younger son impudently extracts his share of the coming inheritance from his gracious father. He sells out his share and then squanders the money in a faraway city. Then, bankrupt and starving, the prodigal son ends up feeding pigs, a task that was forbidden to a Jew (Lev 11:7; 14:8). Finally, coming “to his senses” (v. 17), he decides to return to his father, asking for forgiveness and begging to be given the status of a hired servant. When he sees his son returning, however, the father runs to him, embraces him, kisses him and gives him a new robe, a ring and new shoes. The father also throws a great feast for him, to celebrate his return, killing the “*fatted calf’”* reserved for the Passover feast, so that all may rejoice with him at the wanderer’s return.



      Life messages: 1) We need to meet the challenge for self-evaluation: If we have been in sin, God’s mercy is seeking us, searching for our souls with a love that is wild beyond all imagining. God is no less ready to receive and welcome us back than Jesus was to welcome sinners in his time. 2) We should also ask God for the courage to extend this forgiveness to others who have offended us. 3) Let us confess our sins and regain peace and God’s friendship. The first condition for experiencing the joy and relief of having our sins forgiven is to see them as they are and give them up. We have to be humble enough to recognize that we need God’s forgiveness to be whole.

      In the parable of the Prodigal Son, we find a process involving conversion, confession and celebration. The moment the prodigal son comes to himself and decides to go back home, the conversion has already taken place. He then stands before his father confessing his unworthiness even to be treated as a hired servant. This leads to the father initiating a celebration in the whole household. The most important stage here is that of conversion. This is what happens at the Sacrament of Reconciliation. The most important thing in the sacrament of confession is the conversion of the heart. Many people think confessing one’s sins is the most important. But if a conversion of the heart does not follow confession, the exercise has no meaning.

      Do not believe that caring for the sick consists only in giving them medicine and food; there is another kind of care which you should never forget, that of the heart which seeks to adapt to the suffering person, going to meet his needs” — words of one whose mind and heart were fully seized of a mission, viz., Saint Maria Josefa of the Heart of Jesus. The mission: to be a “neighbour” to the sick and the suffering in the world around.



      Canonized by Pope John Paul II on 1 October in the Jubilee Year 2000, St Maria Josefa was the Foundress of the Institute of the Servants of Jesus of Charity. Conceived in the year 1871 in Bilbao, Spain, this Institute, totally dedicated to nursing the sick with a contemplative approach, has a presence of more than 1000 members in some 43 houses spread across the globe.



      As superior for the next 41years, Maria Josefa undertook long, gruelling visits to the growing number of communities until a protracted illness, obliging her to stay in bed or remain seated, confined her to the house at Bilbao, from where she maintained her contacts wholly via correspondence. An ardent adorer of the Mystery of the Redemption, her ideas are well expressed in her theses, *Directorio de Asistencias.*Sharing intimately in the sufferings of the crucified Lord, she passed away on 20 March 1912.



      Maria Josefa was the eldest daughter of Bernabe Sancho and Petra de Guerra. Born on 7 September 1842 in Vitoria, Spain, she lost her father when she was barely 15. Very early in life, she nurtured a strong devotion to the Eucharist, the Sacred Heart and our Blessed Mother.
