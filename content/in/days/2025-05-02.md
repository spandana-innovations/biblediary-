    body: |
      Reflection: 1



      Jesus knew what he was going to do in order to feed the large crowd that was coming to him, but He wanted to teach his disciples to trust in God and to plan how to make the best use what they had rather than worry about what they did not have. In addition to showing the compassion of Jesus and his divine power, the multiplication of loaves teaches us that Jesus wants our participation in solving our problems. When we surrender the little we have to him, he takes it and makes it much and returns it to us for the benefit of so many. Jesus also teaches us that no gift of God, material or otherwise, should be wasted, as he told the disciples to gather up the left over. And they gathered abundance of it. Give to God and to fellow humans generously, and gifts shall be yours in abundant measure. The multiplication of bread is also a prefigure of the Eucharistic meal in which Jesus gives to millions his own body as their nourishment.



      *— Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      Today’s Gospel describes one occasion when Jesus tried in vain to withdraw from the crowds at Capernaum. He travelled by boat to the other side of the Sea of Galilee to a remote village called Bethsaida Julius, where there was a small grassy plain. But when Jesus stepped ashore, He was faced with a large crowd of people. This was the scene of the miraculous feeding of the five thousand as described in today’s Gospel. This is the only miracle, other than the Resurrection, that is told in all four Gospels, a fact that speaks of its importance to the early Church. Today’s Gospel passage invites us to become humble instruments in God’s hands by sharing our blessings with our brothers and sisters. We may regard the incident in which Jesus multiplied loaves and fish in order to feed his hungry listeners, both as a miracle of Divine Providence and as a Messianic sign. The lesson for every Christian is that, no matter how impossible one’s assignment may seem, with Divine help it can be done: *"For with God nothing shall be impossible” (Lk 1:37).* Jesus used as his starting point for the miraculous meal a young boy’s generous gift of all the food he had, perhaps to remind us that love is the prime requirement for salvation, and selfishness blocks the life-giving action of the grace of God in us. The early Christian community especially cherished this story because they saw this event as anticipating the Eucharist.



      <span class="subhead">Life message: </span>



      1) As Christians we need to commit ourselves to share all we have and are, and to work with God in communicating His compassion to all. God is a caring Father, but He wants our co-operation. That’s what the early Christians did, generously sharing what they had with the needy.



      2) We, and others in our time, need to ask for the courage to share, even when we think we have nothing to offer. Whatever we offer through Jesus will have a life-giving effect in those who receive it.



      3) We are shown two attitudes in the Gospel story: that of Philip and that of Andrew (Jn 6:7-9). Philip said, in effect: "The situation is hopeless; nothing can be done." But Andrew’s attitude was: "I’ll see what I can do; and I will trust Jesus to do the rest." We need to have Andrew’s attitude*.(Fr Tony: https://frtonyshomilies.com/)*

      Each day in life brings its problems and challenges. There remains the choice to unduly worry about them or to look for solutions. A believer seeks solutions, discerning the will of God and in cooperation with fellow beings. In the event of the multiplication of loaves, Jesus would accept the available loaves, give thanks to God, and hand them over to the disciples. When we fix our concerns on God, God fixes our concerns. Our focus ought to be on God and not on problems; we need to listen to God and not concentrate on our insecurities; and we need to learn to rely on God rather than on our strength alone. Am I tied down by the problems of life or do I seek solutions to them together with my God and my brothers and sisters?

    body: |
      A native of Alexandria (Egypt), St Athanasius was probably the son of prominent Christians. He received a brilliant education in this city, a great commercial centre and most important emporium for ideas. Even as a young man he was conspicuous for his wisdom and acumen, as well as for his deep piety. When still only a Deacon, he was chosen by Bishop Alexander, the Patriarch of Alexandria, as his secretary and theological adviser, and in 325 taken to the great Council of Nicaea (Turkey) in Bithynia (Asia Minor), which condemned Arius as a heretic and reaffirmed Christ’s divinity. There he became a sincere and courageous mouthpiece for his aged Archbishop, who had remained crippled from the tortures endured on the rack during the Galerian persecution. When the latter died in 328, Athanasius, although only 33, was unanimously elected successor.



      During his 46-year reign, the Arian faction came to gain such high political influence through its intrigues at the imperial court that the saintly Patriarch had to spend a total of 17 years in banishment, being exiled from Alexandria on five occasions. Undaunted, he remained through it all the ardent and fearless “champion of Christ’s divinity”. Far from seeking revenge, he tried rather to win his enemies through kindness, and always retained his spontaneous sense of humour, which could also, on occasion, be penetratingly sharp.



      “Athanasius against the world!” well sums up the position of this religious genius, the Father of Orthodoxy and scientific theology. Throughout 50 critical years in Christian history he was the chief defender of the integrity of the Catholic creed. Although he longed for martyrdom, his last years were peaceful, and he died at the age of 78.



      The most famous of his many writings are *On the Incarnation*, which he composed in his early twenties while still a Deacon, and the *History of the Arians*written during his third exile among the Egyptian desert monks. His *Life of St Anthony* made an enormous appeal to men everywhere to lead the ascetical life.
