    body: |
      Reflection: 1



      Jesus curses the unproductive fig tree, and it withers. He cleanses the Temple because it has also become spiritually unproductive. Both the fig tree and the Temple are doomed to extinction. As the fig tree here is full of leaves but has no fruits, so the Temple, though magnificent, fails to produce fruits of prayer and true devotion. Both are deceptive in the impression they give. The people of Israel, and everyone in the world, will be judged by the fruits thy produce. The cleansing of the Temple by Jesus inaugurates the day foretold by Prophet Zechariah, “There will no longer be merchants in the house of the Lord, God of hosts” (Zech 14: 21). We must purify our prayer and worship from all profit-making business. The disciples of Jesus have to be fruitful always. They should worship God not merely in rituals but in spirit and in truth. </span></span>



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      Jesus’ driving out from the temple those engaged in buying and selling confront us with the ending of an era. The withering of the fig tree signals the end of the Jerusalem temple. Yet it is clear that life goes on and that our response must not be mere passive submission to events.</span></span></span></span></span>



      Mark’s gospel sets Jesus’ cleansing the temple in some association with his cursing the fig tree and its withering, since the story of the fig tree envelops the other incident, a style quite common in Mark. Jesus was doing more than cleansing the temple, for his words, drawn from the Old Testament, announce a new type of temple: “My house shall be called a house of prayer for all people.” In those days, non-Jews were forbidden under pain of death to advance beyond the outer court of the gentiles, and the Roman authorities ratified this prescription. But Jesus draws from an Old Testament passage (Isaiah, 56) from a tradition which was not dominant in the life of Israel, though the words give an understanding of God’s plans for the future of his people. Clearly, he wants them to live more prayerfully and more generously towards others, and allow outsiders to share in the Jewish prayerful awareness of God’s presence.</span></span></span></span></span>



      Sirach advises us to look to our ancestors in order to do the proper thing for our posterity: “Now will I praise those godly people, our ancestors, each in their own time…. And for all time their progeny will endure.” This text bridges past and future and is rooted in God’s covenant. Fidelity to God and to God’s bond with us will carry us over the deepest chasms and ruptures in our life. No matter what may happen – death of one’s spouse or children, financial loss, disturbance of one’s health – nothing can break the bond of God’s covenant that unites ancestors with descendants.</span></span></span></span></span>



      We can overcome our trials, and look to the future because of the bond of God’s love for us in Christ Jesus. No matter what may happen, the practical instruction of today’s texts is there to help us.</span></span></span></span></span>

    body: |
      Once in Jerusalem, Jesus spends the day in the Temple area and as the night approaches, He would retire to Bethany. Meanwhile, He observes that the Temple has become a marketplace. Activities like selling pigeons and changing money had some religious necessity but miss the point that the Temple is a place of prayer. Jesus tries to change the situation by driving out the sellers but comes to confrontation with the authorities. The authorities want to take action against Jesus but, fearing the people, they let Him go. The incident of the fig tree also points to the spiritual decadence of the time. A tree that does not produce fruit is barren and useless, and Jesus utters a curse on the tree. Jesus will bring renewal to the world through His death and resurrection. Only this self-sacrificing disposition of Jesus has the power to bring change and transformation. What steps do we take to bring renewal in our own lives?</span></span></span>

    body: |
      True to his mother’s training, Cyril when a still a child, would repeat the name of Christ at all times and confessed that the utterance of this name moved him strangely.



      His father, a heathen and an idolator, hated Christianity and would beat and revile him for the faith he professed. Young Cyril bore all with Christian joy and when he was driven away by his father from the shelter of his roof he said he had lost little and would receive a great recompense instead.



      It being a time of general persecution, the governor of Caesarea, learning about Cyril, had the child brought before him. The persecutors initially tried tempting him in various ways to apostatize and, later, even led him to the place of execution and showed him the horrible instruments of torture. But no threats could make him show the slightest sign of fear. The Governor pitying perhaps his tender years, offered him freedom and besought him to return to his home and inheritance. But the blessed youth replied: “I left home gladly, for I have a greater and a better reward which is waiting for me!”



      Cyril was then taken to the fires as if for execution but was brought out once again to be re-examined. Still, he remained filled with his unwavering heavenly desire and protested against the cruel delay of his executioners. Blandishments and added threats were of no avail in moving the little confessor, and the judge suffered him at last to win the eternal crown he craved for!
