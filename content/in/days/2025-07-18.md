    body: |
      Reflection: 1



      For the Jews, Sabbath was a day of rest and prayer, and not a working day. The Pharisees find fault with the disciples of Jesus who were hungry, for plucking grain and eating it, for they believed this was forbidden. Jesus reminds ham that it is more important to satisfy real human needs like the need for food, than to fulfill literally all legal prescriptions. He quotes to them Prophet Isaiah (6:6) through whom God says, “I desire mercy and not sacrifice.” Denying food to a hungry person is a merciless and cruel act, and not acceptable to God. On the day of the Last Judgement, the Judge would like to tell us, “I was hungry and you fed me” (Mt 25:35).



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      Today’s Gospel passage gives Jesus’ teaching on the purpose of the Sabbath and on its proper observance. This was his response to a criticism and a silly accusation made by Pharisees against his disciples who, on a Sabbath, to satisfy their hunger, plucked ears of grain from a field and ate the grains after removing the husks by rubbing the grains between their palms and blowing away the chaff. The Pharisees accused them of violating Sabbath laws by performing three items of work forbidden on Sabbath, namely, harvesting, threshing and winnowing.



      Counterarguments: Jesus gives three counterarguments from Holy Scripture defending his apostles. (1) Basic human needs, like hunger, take precedence over Divine worship and Sabbath observance. Jesus cites from the Scripture the example of the hungry David and his selected soldiers. They approached Ahimelech, the priest of Nob, who gave them for food the “offering bread” which only the priests were allowed to eat (1 Sam 21:1-6). (2) No law can stand against Divine worship. That is why the priests were not considered as violating Sabbath laws although they did the work of preparing two rams for sacrifice in the Temple (Nm 28:9-10). (3) God desires that we practice mercy: Jesus quotes the prophet Hosea to tell the accusers God’s words: “I*want mercy, not sacrifice”* (Hos 6:6).



      Life messages: Like the Jewish Sabbath, the Christian Sunday is to be



      1) a day for rest and refreshment with members of the family;



      2) a day for thanksgiving and the recharging of spiritual batteries through participation in the Eucharistic celebration for Catholics and through worship service for the Non-Catholics;



      3) a day parents should use for teaching religious Faith and Bible lessons for their children;



      4) a day for doing works of charity in the neighbourhood and in the parish;



      5) a day for socializing with family members, neighbors, and fellow parishioners.

      In today’s gospel we see Jesus and his disciples doing something that was forbidden to do on Sabbath. The hungry disciples of Jesus picked up corn from the field and ate. The religious leaders found fault with their simple action and accused them of breaking the law of the Sabbath. Jesus, understanding the agony of hunger very well, shows why it is more important to satisfy human needs than to be faithful to the rituals and regulations of the Sabbath. If a law stipulates the denial of food to a hungry person, we should do away with that law. Is our worship today helping us to become more sensitive and humane towards the hungry and the needy?

    ref: "St Frederick Bishop and Martyr (-838)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      When young, St Frederick was trained in piety and sacred learning among the clergy of the Church of Utrecht, Holland. After he was ordained priest, he was given the charge of instructing the converts by Bishop Ricfried. About the year 825, he was chosen to succeed Ricfried to the See of Utrecht. The new Bishop at once began to establish order everywhere, and sent St Odulf and other zealous labourers into the Northern parts to dispel the paganism that still flourished there.



      According to tradition Frederick became involved in the difficulties between the sons of the Emperor, Louis the Debonair, and their father and step-mother. He also fell out of favour with the Empress, whom he had taken the opportunity to admonish, though charitably, to turn to a life of morality.



      The inhabitants of Walcheren were barbarous and most averse to the Gospel. So, he took upon himself the duty of teaching and evangelizing them. According to tradition, on 18 July 838, after he had celebrated Mass and was about to make his thanksgiving, he was stabbed by two assassins. He died saying with the Psalmist, “I will praise the Lord in the land of the living”.
