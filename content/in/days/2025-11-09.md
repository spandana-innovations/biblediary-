      Reflection: 1



      Jesus found that the Temple was turned into a place of trade and profit-making. The high priest Caiaphas introduced this trade, and the Temple guards protected it. With their hypocritical worship they had already destroyed the sacred nature of the Temple. Jesus acted with authority and power and cleansed the Temple immediately with his prophetic action. Jesus teaches that unless our piety and worship are moved by true devotion and love of God, they are mere rituals. When the Jews questioned his authority, Jesus pointed to his own resurrection as a sure sign of it. Jesus, the Resurrected Lord, is the new Temple where we offer God rue worship in spirit and truth, through him, with him and in him. </span></span></span></span>



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      Today’s Gospel gives us the dramatic account of Jesus’ cleansing of the Temple in Jerusalem. He drove out its merchants and moneychangers with moral indignation at the unjust commercialization of God’s House of Prayer and the exploitation of the poor pilgrims in the name of religion. The merchants charged exorbitant prices for animals for sacrifices, and the moneychangers charged unjust commissions for the required exchange of pagan coins for Temple coins. Jesus cleansed the Temple in Jerusalem. Originally built by Solomon in 966 BC and rebuilt by Zerubbabel in 515 BC after the Babylonians had destroyed it, the Temple was still being renovated for the last time finishing the work begun in 20 BC by King Herod the Great. The abuses which infuriated Jesus were 1) the conversion of a place of prayer to a noisy marketplace and 2) the unjust business practices of animal merchants and moneychangers, encouraged by the Temple authorities. Hence, Jesus made a whip of cords and drove away the animals and the moneychangers, quoting Zechariah the prophet, “*Stop making My Father’s house a marketplace” (Zechariah 14:21).*</span>



      <span class="subhead">Life messages</span>: </span>



      1) We need to avoid the business mentality of profit and loss in Divine worship. Our relationship with God must be that of a child to his parent, one of love, respect and desire for the common good, with no thought of gain or loss. </span>



      2) We need to remember that we are the temples of the Holy Spirit. Hence, we have no right to desecrate God’s temple by impurity, injustice, pride, hatred, or jealousy.</span>



      3) We need to love our parish Church and use it. Our Church is the place where we come together as a community to praise and worship God, to thank Him for His blessings, to ask pardon and forgiveness for our sins, and to offer our lives and petitions on the altar. Let us make our Church an even more holy place by adding our prayers and songs to community worship and by offering our time, talents, and treasure in the various ministries of our parish. </span>

      Today, we celebrate the Dedication of the Lateran Basilica in Rome. The Readings remind us that the Church is more than just a building; it embodies God’s life-giving presence. In the First Reading, taken from Ezekiel, water flows from the temple, symbolizing new life. In the Second Reading, taken from 1 Corinthians, Paul calls our bodies temples of the Holy Spirit, reminding us to honour our bodies as sacred. In the Gospel, Jesus is upset by the unjust practices prevailing in the temple which harm the poor. He stands with the needy and the underprivileged. Can we also be a comforting presence to the poor and needy today? Our bodies, as recipients of the Eucharist, symbolize God’s presence within us. Let us honour this sacredness and, like the life-giving water flowing from the temple, share God’s abundant gifts with others, especially those less fortunate. Today, let us become channels of compassion and justice for all.

    body: |
      The Basilica of St John Lateran in Rome is “the Mother and Mistress of all the churches in the city and in the world”, for, being the Episcopal seat of the pope as bishop of Rome, it ranks even above St Peter’s.



      Fausta, the consort of Constantine the Great, gave her Lateran palace to Pope St Miltiades in about 313, and on 9 November 324, Pope St Sylvester I solemnly consecrated part of it as the Basilica of the Most Holy Saviour — the first instance of a public consecration of a church. What remained of the palace served as a residence for the Popes over the next 1000 years and today serves as a museum.



      Between the 4th and 16th centuries, 5 Ecumenical Councils and 20 Synods were held in this Basilica. Since the 12th century it has been known as St John Lateran, in honour of Sts John the Baptist and John the Evangelist. In fact, the Lateran was then to the Catholic world what the Vatican is today, the seat of judgement and the centre of government in the Church.



      The original structure and its successors have suffered the ravages of war, earthquake and fire. The full baroque remodelling and decoration of the present structure were executed by Francesco Barromini under Pope Innocent X in 1646. Among the outstanding relics preserved in this shrine are said to be the heads of Saints Peter and Paul, encased in silver busts. Beneath the high altar, rest remnants of the little wooden table on which St Peter used to celebrate Mass in the house of Pudens, and the sacred table of the Last Supper of Our Lord.
