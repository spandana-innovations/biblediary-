  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Mark 11:27–33"
    audio: null
    body: |
      After casting out animal merchants and money- changers from the Temple immediately after the Palm Sunday procession, Jesus started teaching in the Temple courts. Hence, the chief priests and elders of the people approached Jesus questioning his authority for entering the city in a triumphal procession, for allowing the children to acclaim him, for curing the sick, for casting out merchants and moneychangers, and for teaching in the Temple area. This was a trap. If Jesus claimed that his authority was Divine as the Messiah, they would arrest him as a blasphemer. If he claimed that his authority was human, he would be arrested as a mad zealot damaging the people’s simple Faith in the Temple and what it stood for. Since Jesus did not want to risk his life and mission a few days ahead of time, he silenced them by asking a challenging counter-question about John the Baptist and his message – was this from God or man? Was this Divine or human?



      If they answered that it was Divine, the questioners would be asked to explain why they did not accept John’s message and his witness-bearing that Jesus was the Messiah. If they answered that it was human, they would have to face the anger of the crowd who had accepted John as a prophet. Hence, they kept silent opting for a shameful self-humiliation.



      <span class="subhead">Life message:</span>



      In religious matters we should not ask whether our stand for Christ is safe or useful. Instead, we need to stand for Truth with the courage of our Christian convictions even if it costs our life.



      (Fr. Tony: https://frtonyshomilies.com/)
  - key: reflection
    title: Reflection
    ref: null
    audio: null
    body: |
      In today’s gospel, the issue of Jesus’ authority is raised by the religious leaders in relation to Jesus’ cleansing of the Temple. They questioned Jesus’ authority not because they did not know it, for they knew him to be the Son of God which he himself repeatedly claimed to be on different occasions. It was their unwillingness to give up their own authority and submit to his divine authority that had them turn against him. Like the religious leaders of Jesus’ time, we know the identity of Jesus and are well aware of the proofs of his authority. But how often are we tempted to dismiss or discredit it on account of our pride and prejudice? Let us acknowledge him as the Lord of our lives and yield to his divine authority in joy and trust!

      St Justin (Martyr) (c.100-166)

      St Justin was born of pagan parents in Samaria (Syria). Equipped with a good education, he earnestly sought instruction from various pagan philosophers, but none could satisfy his ardent thirst for truth or tell him anything definite about God. He once drew a venerable old Christian into a lengthy philosophical discussion which led him to take up the study of the Prophets.

      Having witnessed firsthand the trials and torments of Christian martyrs he was astonished at their fearlessness. And so it was that the living faith of the martyrs, combined with a study of the Scriptures, brought about his conversion.

      Leading an austere and holy life he travelled about Asia Minor (Turkey), preaching the truth about Christ. To rebut the novel ideas of the heretic Marcion, who was then troubling the Church, Justin wrote a number of treatises which were highly prized by St Jerome, but which are now unfortunately lost. Only the two apologias (defence of Christianity), which he addressed to the Emperors Anotoninus Pius and Marcus Aurelius, and his famous “Dialogue with the Jew Tryphon” are extant.

      “The greatest blessing I can wish you, is that you might come to realize that wisdom is given to every man through this Way of Life, and that someday you too might come to believe as we do that Jesus is the Christ of God!” wrote Justin.

      St Justin is the first great Christian apologist, and the Patron of Catholic philosophy.
