      Reflection: 1



      It is our duty to complete sincerely and with commitment whatever work is given to us. No selfish expectation should be the motive for the work we do. We have no claim to any credit for doing what we are commanded to do. We are servants of God, and any good work we do is done in His service. It is our privilege and a source of contentment to be employed in His service. </span></span></span></span>



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      Today’s Gospel gives Jesus’ warning on complacency in the spiritual life through a parable about a slave and his relationship with his master. The slave had the duties of a cook as well as those of a fieldworker. Jesus says that the master wanted him to do fieldwork till evening, then prepare the supper and serve him at the table. Since both were parts of his duty, he need not expect a “thank you” from the master. This parable was a warning also to the Pharisees, who thought that God was indebted to them because of their scrupulous observance of the Mosaic Law.</span>



      The Teaching: We are God’s servants because God owns everything, and all our possessions have been loaned to us by a generous God. Our efforts and accomplishments are nothing in comparison with the blessings of God which we have received. All our devotions, prayers and works of charity form only a negligible part of our return to God, and the power to do them is another of His gifts to us. We can never adequately repay the gifts God has given to us. The Blessed Virgin Mary was fully aware of this truth, and that is why she responded to God’s messenger saying: “*Behold I am the handmaid of the Lord”* (Lk 1:38). St. Bernadette of Soubirous (1844-1879), the girl to whom the Blessed Mother appeared in Lourdes, France lived a life in obscurity as a contemplative nun. A journalist was able to track her down and eventually got the permission of the Prioress to interview her. One question that the journalist asked of her was why she opted to live in obscure life when she was so well known all over the world. In answer Bernadette compared herself to a house broom which has been put aside in a closet after it had been used to clean the room.</span>

      The message from today’s Gospel is clear: we are called to do our duties diligently and wholeheartedly without becoming overly attached to the outcome. Being selflessly committed and not expecting anything in return is a fundamental trait of a Christian. Sometimes, we hold standards too high for others in fulfilling their responsibilities but forget about our commitments. Today’s Gospel reminds us to be faithful in our Christian duties to God, the Church, neighbour and society. Today’s First Reading, taken from the Book of Wisdom underscores what it means to be a Christian. It involves placing trust in the Lord, living in his love, and showing mercy to others, especially those who are in need, like the poor and the lost. This is the essence of our Christian duty. How can I practise this today?

    audio: null
    body: |
      At the time of St Martin’s birth, his father, a pagan, was a military tribune stationed in Pannonia (Hungary). Some years later, he was transferred to Pavia in Lombardy, where, at 15, Martin was recruited to the imperial cavalry at Amiens (Gaul).



      One day Martin passed a half-naked beggar, shivering in the cold. Moved with compassion, he split his cloak in two and gave one half to the poor man. Later that night, Christ reportedly appeared to him wearing the cloak. (The other half has long been preserved as “St Martin’s Cloak’” in the chapel of the Frankish Kings.) Not long after this famous incident, Martin received baptism, and, as soon as he was discharged from military service, became a disciple of St Hilary of Poitiers.



      In 361 with Hilary recalled by imperial decree, Martin once more returned to Poitiers. A great many monks gradually gathered around him there, and later formed the famous Benedictine abbey of Ligugé, the first Gallic monastery. St Martin would leave his cell from time to time to undertake missionary journeys through central and western Gaul where idolatry was still rampant among the rural folk. One day in 371, called into the city of Tours (in France) by a ruse, a highly reluctant Martin was forced by popular acclaim into becoming the new Bishop of the vacant See.



      Martin’s administration of his diocese was characterized by firmness and justice, and by a great zeal for the purity of the faith against the Priscillianist and Arian heresies, on account of which he made several journeys to Treves (Trier), then the Emperor’s residence.
