  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Mark 10:13–16"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Today’s gospel is often read at a child’s baptism – which is something deeper than just a naming ceremony. The reference to people bringing children to Jesus in the gospel is reflected in the parents who bring their children for baptism. The contrast between how the disciples reacted to the parents, on the one hand, and how Jesus responded, on the other, is very striking. The disciples wanted to turn the children away, whereas Jesus insisted that the children come to him and that nothing be placed in the way of their coming to him.

      Jesus suggests that we should do what we can to bring children to him, and support them in coming to him. He also suggests that apart from teaching them we have a lot to learn from children. We must learn to welcome the kingdom of God as children do. Children are very receptive to gifts, including the gift of the kingdom of God, the gift of the Lord. They are open to that gift; they are very receptive. We can easily lose that receptiveness or openness to the Lord, as we grow older. As adults we have to keep on recovering it, we need to keep learning to become like children so that we can welcome the gift of the Lord as openly as they do.

      The Jews loved and valued their children. However, socially children were considered powerless and insignificant. Jesus was a lover of children and ascribed utmost importance to them. His gentle and heart-warming treatment of children is in sharp contrast with the rash behaviour of his disciples who scolded the mothers and prevented children from coming to him. Those who perpetrate crimes – abuse, torture, trafficking, mutilation, abortion and exploitation – against children do not value children as Jesus did. Through his command “Let the children come to me” and his declaration “for to such belongs the kingdom of God”, he emphasizes that children have an important role in the community of faith. Today Jesus is indignant with those who neglect the Christian and spiritual upbringing of children in preference to academic and professional excellence.

      St Bede the Venerable Confessor, Doctor of the Church (673 -735)

      A native of Northumberland (England), Bede was educated from his 7th year on at the dual Benedictine monastery of Sts Peter and Paul at Wearmouth-Jarrow under its founder, St Benedict Biscop. In his own words, “From that time I spent my whole life in that Monastery, devoting all my efforts to the study of the scriptures, and amid the observance of the monastic discipline and the daily charge of singing in the church it has ever been my delight to study or teach or write.”

      His piety, learning, and gentle character drew to him hundreds of scholars to whom he imparted all the patristic riches of the former centuries as well as the treasures of science then available. He wrote more than 40 books on history, chronology, physics, music, philosophy, poetry and medicine. Undoubtedly the most learned man of his age. His general world history and his Ecclesiastical History of the English People have earned for him the title of “Father of English history”.

      Greatly noted for his holiness, Bede was at times so overcome with devotion that he would shed tears and, when not studying or teaching, spend long hours in prayer, coming to be called “Venerable” during his life-time itself. In his last years he was constantly ill, yet he worked and prayed right up to the end.

      Declared a Saint and Doctor of the Church by Pope Leo XIII in 1899, his relics are entombed in the Galilee chapel of Durham Cathedral.

      Reflection: “He alone loves the Creator perfectly who manifests a pure love for his neighbour” (St Bede the Venerable).



      St Gregory VII

      Pope Confessor (1020 -1085)

      B

      aptized Hildebrand at birth, St Gregory was the son of a

      humble carpenter of Tuscany (Italy). A Benedictine monk, he became, under Pope Leo IX, Cardinal Sub-Deacon and administrator of the patrimony of St Peter, and soon showed his genius for administration by securing the restitution of Church properties which had unduly slipped into the hands of the Normans and the Roman nobility. Largely responsible for inspiring Papal policies across the reign of five popes, he was, the day after Pope Alexander II’s demise, elected successor by spontaneous acclamation, and took the name, Gregory VII.

      A man of austere sanctity, he dedicated his entire genius and dynamism to a relentless purification of the Church in the matter of lay investiture, simony and clerical incontinency. His drastic enactments which threatened excommunication, met with the fiercest kind of opposition from those against whom they were directed. Emperor Henry IV of Germany proceeded to have his German Bishops “depose” Gregory, but when the Pope excommunicated him and released his subjects from their oath of allegiance, the German Princes insisted on Henry reconciling with Gregory. Defeated, the emperor went to Canossa and obtain absolution. But his repentance was short-lived for, not much later, marching to Rome he had himself crowned the anti-pope Clement III by his supporters. At Pope Gregory’s behest Robert Guiscard and his Normans drove Henry out, but their outrageous 4-day sack of Rome so enraged the Romans that the Pope himself had to flee to Salerno, where he died a year later, saying, “I have loved justice and therefore I die in exile.”

      He was canonized by Pope Paul V in 1606.

      St Mary Magdalen Dei Pazzi

      Virgin (1566 -1607)

      C

      atherine dei Pazzi felt an intense attraction to the Blessed Sacrament very early in her childhood. Making her First Communion at ten she pledged her virginity to God. At 16 she entered a strict St Carmelite convent in Florence where daily Communion was the rule, taking the name of Mary Magdalen.

      Though God allowed her to feel spiritually abandoned by Him during the “dark night of the soul”, her severe self-discipline and utter humility saw her safely through a 5-year period of purification; peace was finally restored to her, and her visions and ecstasies became more frequent and longer, those concerning Our Lord’s passion lasting as much as 16 hours. She also received the sacred Stigmata. It was during these ecstasies that she uttered those wonderful maxims of divine love and counsels of spiritual perfection which have come down to us in four large volumes. Taken down in writing even as she spoke, her sayings contained admonitions to Pope Sixtus V and his counsellors regarding the reform of the Church, thus earning her the title, “the ecstatic saint”.

      Mary Magdalen died at 41, having come to know the full value of suffering for the love of God and for the salvation of souls, and was canonized in 1669.

      Reflection: “Go to your adoration as one who would go to heaven, to the divine banquet. Tell yourself: “In four hours, in two hours, in one hour, our Lord will give us an audience of grace and love. He has invited me, he is waiting for me; he is longing for me” ” (St Peter Julian Eymard).



      St Madeleine Sophie Barat

      Virgin (1779 -1865)

      Born of peasant stock on 12 December 1779 in Burgundy, Madeleine was instructed in Latin, Greek, Italian and Spanish by her Priest¬brother, Louis and grounded in the faith by means of the strictest discipline and penances by him. After the “Reign of Terror” had ended, she co-founded with Fr Joseph Varins, a Jesuit, a religious society of nuns devoted to the Sacred Heart of Jesus in prayer and sacrifice, and to the education of young girls. The first religious in the new congregation took their vows in the year 1800 and its first house was opened at Amiens in 1801.

      Sophie’s personal charm, her prayerful reliance on God and her enterprising zeal —she was Superior General for over 60 years—resulted in the establishment of 105 widespread foundations with some 4,000 religious, even during her lifetime and in spite of the anti-clerical governments of the day, boarding and day schools, retreat houses for women of the world (an innovation), and training schools for teachers and aspirants.

      St Madeleine died on 25 May 1865 and was canonized in 1925.
