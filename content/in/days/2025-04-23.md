      Reflection: 1



      Two disciples of Jesus were going to Emmaus, not far from Jerusalem, when Jesus reached them and walked with them. As he talked with them on the way, he gave them a new understanding of the Scriptures. They urged him to stay with them that night, and he obliged. At table, as he broke bread for them, they recognised him.



      It is through the Word of God and the Eucharist that we recognise Jesus for who he is. The two disciples immediately returned to Jerusalem and met the eleven Apostles. The Apostles in turn told them “The Lord has risen indeed and appeared to Simon.”



      Then the two told the eleven what happened on the road and how they recognised him at the breaking of bread. To be in communion with Jesus we have to deepen our devotion the Word of God and the Eucharist.



      *— Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      The Emmaus episode, which is found only in Luke’s Gospel, describes how Jesus shared the sorrow and frustration of the disciples at the sad and shameful death of their Master. Their hopes of Jesus’ conquest of the Romans and establishment of the glorious Davidic kingdom with his Divine power had been shattered. The risen Lord, unrecognized, joined them as they walked along. He explained the Scriptures and reminded them of the prophecies about the Messiah’s death and Resurrection in order to show them how the events that had happened were the fulfilment of Messianic prophecies. But it was only at supper, when Jesus blessed and broke the bread and gave it to them, that they recognized Jesus, who promptly vanished from their sight. The phrase, “the Breaking of the Bread”, is used repeatedly in the Acts of the Apostles (also written by Luke), to refer to the ritual meal of the Christian community, the Eucharist (Acts 2:42, 46; 20:7; 27:35). Disciples in every century have continued to recognize Jesus in “the Breaking of the Bread.”



      <span class="subhead">Life messages: </span>



      1) The risen Lord is with us in both the joyful and the sad moments of our lives. Too often, we have our hopes and dreams shattered by the untimely deaths of our dear ones, by a split in family relationships, by the worsening of our illnesses, or by the loss of jobs. On such occasions, we have to learn to experience the risen Lord’s consoling and supporting presence in our lives.



      2) As the disciples met the risen Lord on their way to Emmaus, we too must recognize and appreciate his presence in the Holy Eucharist, in the Word of God, in the praying community, at home, and in our Churches. Jesus is still beside his followers. Often, he is only dimly recognized and only by few. “You were with me,” wrote St. Augustine some centuries later, “but I was not with You” (*Confessions*, X, 27). Let us learn to talk to Jesus whenever we are alone and listen to him when we read the Bible, hear it preached or have it explained to us.

      Jesus gently rebukes the Emmaus disciples for their spiritual blindness and “foolishness” in not believing the prophets’ words about the Messiah’s suffering and glory. Their ignorance of the Scriptures demonstrates how a lack of knowledge or understanding can contribute to spiritual blindness. Without a foundation in God’s Word, we may fail to recognise His hand at work in our lives. Thus, we reflect today on the themes of spiritual blindness, revelation, and the transformative power of recognizing the resurrected Christ. On our journey of faith, Jesus is often present with us, even when we may not initially recognise Him. Through Scripture, the breaking of bread, and moments of revelation, He opens our eyes to His presence and His redemptive work in our lives.

    saintName: "Saint Adalbert of Prague - Bishop, martyr (956-997)"
    ref: "Saint Adalbert of Prague - Bishop, martyr (956-997)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Christened Vojtech at birth in the princely Slavnik family of Libice in East Bohemia, Adalbert was trained in theology at Magdeburg under its first Archbishop, Saint Adalbert, whose name he eventually took at his “confirmation”.



      The first Czech occupant of the See of Prague, Adalbert was a man of vibrant energy and zeal and, much given to austerity, strove to reform the clergy, suppress abuses of various kinds and eliminate pagan practices, as also to spread Christianity throughout Bohemia and neighbouring Hungary. However, distressed by the superficial attitude evident towards the Faith, Adalbert left Prague in 982 for Rome, where he became a Benedictine monk.



      Persuaded by the Pope he returned to Prague in 992; but disillusioned with the lack of change in the situation, he left Prague again in 995 only to learn of the brutal massacre of the Slavnik family and its supporters that followed. Now refused permission to return to his See, he obtained Papal release from his episcopal obligations and dedicated himself entirely to missionary activity, becoming a close friend and trusted adviser of the Roman Emperor, Otto III, on matters concerning the progressive Christianization of the Slavs.



      True to the meaning of his baptismal name, Adalbert was to truly prove “a consolation to the army” of Jesus Christ. Invited by the Polish king, Boleslas I the Great, he set about organizing missionary work among the heathen Prussians on the Baltic coast where he was martyred in 997. His body, ransomed by Boleslas, was buried at Gniezno in Poland and later transferred to Prague in 1039.
