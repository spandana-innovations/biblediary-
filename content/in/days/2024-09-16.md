    ref: "Luke 7:1–10"

    body: |
      Jesus’ healing of the centurion’s slave, described in today’s Gospel, shows us how God listens to our Faith-filled prayers and meets our needs. Centurions were reliable, commanding officers, brave captains in charge of 80 soldiers in the first century AD. They were the backbone of the Roman army. According to Luke’s account (Lk 7:1-10), this centurion loved the Jews, respected their religious customs, built a synagogue for them, loved his sick servant, trusted in Jesus’ power of healing, and was ready to face the ridicule of his fellow-centurions by pleading before a Jewish rabbi.</span></span></span></span>






      The remote healing: The centurion asked Jesus to shout a command, as the centurion did with his soldiers, so that the illness might leave his servant by the power of that order. Jesus was moved by the centurion’s Faith-filled request and rewarded the trusting Faith of this Gentile officer by performing a telepathic healing. When we ask for the intercession of the saints, we are like the centurion, acknowledging that we are not worthy, by our own merits, to stand before the Lord and bring Him our request, trusting in His healing love and forgiveness.</span></span></span></span>






      Life message:</span></span> We need to grow to the level of the Faith of the centurion by knowing and personally experiencing Jesus in our lives. We do so by daily meditative reading of the Bible, by our daily personal and family prayers and by frequenting the Sacraments, especially the Eucharistic celebration. The next step to which the Holy Spirit brings us is the complete surrender of our whole being and life to Jesus whom we have experienced, by rendering loving service to others seeing Jesus in them. </span></span></span></span>

      The Centurion who approached Jesus was not only courageous but faith-filled as well. By seeking help from an itinerant preacher from Galilee, he risked the ridicule and mockery from his contemporaries. Nonetheless, he approached Jesus with confidence and humility. He, though a Roman soldier with power and authority under him, was friendly towards Jews and built their temple. He was a high-ranking official with 100 soldiers under him, but he was sensitive enough to love his servant boy. He confessed his unworthiness before Jesus whom he considered a great man of God with divine power. He trusted that Jesus could even heal his servant from a distance with just one word. Jesus commends him for his faith and immediately grants his request. Am I willing to undergo shame and ridicule for the sake of my faith in Christ? Am I willing to approach Jesus with faith, trust, confidence and humility for my own healing and that of others?</span></span></span></span>

      St Cornelius (Pope, Martyr) (- 253)



       A</span></span></span></span>bout a year after Pope St Fabian’s martyrdom, the Decian persecution was sufficiently relaxed due to the Emperor’s absence from Rome, so that 16 Bishops were able to assemble and elect Cornelius Pope (c.March 251). He reigned for two years, and during this time excommunicated the first anti-Pope, Novatian, and his followers because of their excessively rigid stand which denied readmission under any condition to all who had apostatized under the strain of the persecution, no matter how sincere their repentance. This would have been placing a limit on God’s mercy. We are told that at that time there were in Rome a limited number of clergy caring for some 50,000 Christians, among whom were 1,500 minors wholly dependent on charity. With the renewal of the persecution of 253, St Cornelius was exiled to Civitavecchia, where he died either of exhaustion or decapitation.</span></span></span></span>












      St Cyprian (*Bishop, Martyr) (c.190-258)*</span></span></span></span>








       S</span></span></span></span>t Cyprian, the famous orator of Carthage, Africa, was a man of considerable wealth, and endowed with remarkable energy and administrative ability. He was in his fifties when a chance encounter with an old priest named Caecilian convincingly exposed him to the truth of Christianity. As a result, he embraced Christianity and sold his properties for the benefit of the poor. Within a few years he was ordained Priest and, on popular insistence, consecrated Bishop of Carthage, at that time the very centre of the Church in Africa. Then came the Decian persecution, and Cyprian, in order to ensure that the organization of the Church remained intact, governed his See from a hiding place. When the persecution abated during the following year, he returned to Carthage; but his compassionate leniency in readmitting to communion, under certain conditions, some sincerely contrite penitents (*lapsi*) who had apostatized under the terrible tortures, was attacked by the Priest Novatian and even created a schism. </span></span></span></span>









      In 257 a new persecution broke out under Valerian, and St Cyprian was beheaded on 14 September 258. The detailed official account of his fearless profession of faith before the Roman judge and of his condemnation and death that has come down to us throws abundant light upon Catholic practice of the day. Sixty-two of his letters are still extant.</span></span></span></span>
