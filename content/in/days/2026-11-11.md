  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Luke 17: 11-19"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Jesus was on the border between Galilee and Samaria when he was met by a band of ten lepers, both Jews and Samaritans. By describing Jesus’ miraculous healing of the ten lepers from a physically devastating and socially isolating disease, today’s Gospel presents a God Who desires only gratitude from us for the many blessings we have received from Him, and Who feels pain at our ingratitude. The Gospel story tells of a single leper (a “*Samaritan*”), who returned to thank Jesus for healing him, while the others went their way, the Jews perhaps under the false impression that healing was their right as God’s chosen people. They did not seem to feel indebted to Jesus for the singular favour they had received. Instead, they hurried off to obtain a health certificate from the priests. “*Where are the other nine*?” Jesus asked the Samaritan leper. *“Has none but this foreigner returned to give thanks to God?’”*Today’s reading also presents Faith and healing going hand in hand, as do Faith and reconciliation.



      <span class="subhead">Life messages:</span> 1) We need to learn to be thankful to God and to others. Often, we are ungrateful to God. Although we receive so much from Him, we often take it all for granted without appreciating His gifts. Often, we are ungrateful to our parents and consider them a nuisance, although in the past we were dependent on them for literally everything. Similarly, we owe a great debt of gratitude to our friends, teachers, doctors, and pastors — but we often fail to thank them. Hence, in the future, let us be filled with daily thanksgiving to God and to others for the countless gifts we have received. Let us pray: *“Please, God, heal my heart of ingratitude.”* 2) We need to celebrate the Holy Eucharist as the supreme act of thanksgiving. When we celebrate Holy Mass together, we are thanking God for the great gift of His Son, whose sacrifice formed us into the People of God*.*We thank God for the gift of the Spirit, through whom we bring the presence of the Lord to others. 3) We all need healing from our spiritual leprosy. Although we may not suffer from physical leprosy, when we suffer from the “spiritual leprosy” of sins, Jesus, our Saviour, wants to heal us through the Sacrament of Reconciliation.



      *(Fr Tony: https://frtonyshomilies.com/)*

    title: Reflection
    ref: null

    body: |
      In today’s Gospel, Jesus heals ten lepers and sends them to the priests. Only one of them returns to thank Jesus, prompting Him to ask, “Where are the other nine?” Jesus praises the attitude of gratitude displayed by the one who returned, saying, “Your faith has made you well.” This story reminds us of the importance of returning to God with a grateful heart after receiving blessings. As people of God, we are called to give Him praise, worship, and thanks, not just to seek Him in times of need. Gratitude deepens our faith and makes us true worshippers, acknowledging God’s constant goodness in our lives. Each day offers us blessings, both big and small, for which we should return to God with thanks. Like the grateful leper, let us honour God daily with hearts full of praise and appreciation for His grace.

    saintName: "Saint Martin of Tours, Bishop, Confessor (c.316-c.397)"
    ref: "Saint Martin of Tours, Bishop, Confessor (c.316-c.397)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      At the time of St Martin’s birth, his father, a pagan, was a military tribune stationed in Pannonia (Hungary). Some years later, he was transferred to Pavia in Lombardy, where, at 15, Martin was recruited to the imperial cavalry at Amiens (Gaul).



      One day Martin passed a half-naked beggar, shivering in the cold. Moved with compassion, he split his cloak in two and gave one half to the poor man. Later that night, Christ reportedly appeared to him wearing the cloak. (The other half has long been preserved as “St Martin’s Cloak’” in the chapel of the Frankish Kings.) Not long after this famous incident, Martin received baptism, and, as soon as he was discharged from military service, became a disciple of St Hilary of Poitiers.



      In 361 with Hilary recalled by imperial decree, Martin once more returned to Poitiers. A great many monks gradually gathered around him there, and later formed the famous Benedictine abbey of Ligugé, the first Gallic monastery. St Martin would leave his cell from time to time to undertake missionary journeys through central and western Gaul where idolatry was still rampant among the rural folk. One day in 371, called into the city of Tours (in France) by a ruse, a highly reluctant Martin was forced by popular acclaim into becoming the new Bishop of the vacant See.



      Martin’s administration of his diocese was characterized by firmness and justice, and by a great zeal for the purity of the faith against the Priscillianist and Arian heresies, on account of which he made several journeys to Treves (Trier), then the Emperor’s residence.
