      Reflection 1



      Jesus had compassion on the invalid who was beside the pool of Bethesda for thirty-eight years, </span></span></span></span>and asked him if he wanted to be healed. Surely, he wanted to be healed, but did not expect Jesus to heal him by a miracle but only to help him to enter the pool. Jesus gave him so much more and asked him to get up, take his mat and walk. At once the man was healed and he did what Jesus told him. This happened on a Sabbath and the Jews told the man what he was doing was not lawful on the Sabbath. </span></span></span></span>



      For the Jews the Sabbath rest was more important than any act of kindness done to human beings. But not for Jesus. The Lord used his divine power and offered the man healing, salvation and grace. </span></span></span></span>



      Jesus was not bound by rigid legal observances. He highlights that all laws are meant for the benefit of man and not otherwise. It is not right to use the law as an instrument to oppress the people or as a means to avoid helping them.</span></span></span></span>

      Reflection 2



      The Jews had three major feasts – the Feast of Tabernacles, the Feast of Passover, and the Feast of Pentecost. Although only adult male Jews living within a 15 mile-radius of Jerusalem were bound to participate in the Passover feast, Jesus went to Jerusalem as a practicing Jew. Today’s Gospel passage describes how Jesus healed a paralyzed man who had been lying near the “*Probatic” pool* of Bethesda (also called Bethzatha), for 38 years, hoping for a healing when the water was miraculously stirred by an angel. Before granting the healing, Jesus asked the paralytic if he wanted to be healed. The man expressed his intense desire for healing and confessed his inability to crawl to the pond in time. At once, Jesus gave the healing command, “*Take up your pallet, and walk,”* and the man obeyed. The Pharisees sternly told the healed man that he shouldn’t be carrying his mat as that the day was the Sabbath. The man responded that his healer had told him to but was unable to identify Jesus as that healer when they asked. Later, when Jesus caught up with former paralytic in the Temple and warned him, “*See, you are well! Sin no more, that nothing worse befall you!”* the former paralytic recognized that it was Jesus who had healed him and reported the fact to the Jews who had earlier questioned him about carrying his mat.



      Life message: 1) We, too, will experience miracles in our lives when we approach God with trusting Faith in His power to do the impossible and in His mercy for His children. But we need to express our desire to Him with persevering and fervent prayers. 2) This miracle challenges us to give up the blindness of our heart and the lameness of our mind and the paralysis of our spirit and to focus on the positive of God’s unconditional healing and love made visible in Jesus.

      Jesus heals a man who was invalid for thirty-eight years. This healing incident reminds us of the profound and transformative nature of Jesus’ ministry and his role as the source of spiritual and physical healing. It challenges us to move from a place of brokenness to one of wholeness through our encounter with Christ and his transformative grace. The incident serves as a powerful symbol of spiritual healing by illustrating the disabled man’s condition as a symbol of humanity’s spiritual brokenness. The Pool of Bethesda is a symbol of worldly attempts to find spiritual relief. Jesus is the divine healer, offering grace and salvation. The healing word of Jesus points to God’s transformative power. The Sabbath controversy challenges us to go beyond legalism. The man’s faith and response show the steps involved in spiritual renewal. This passage invites us to consider our spiritual condition and our need for the healing and transformation that only Jesus can provide.

    audio: null
    body: |
      It was at the early age of 25 and while still a layman that Hugh, a native of the Dauphine (south-eastern France), was made Canon at Valence. An active defendant of the reforms promulgated by Pope Saint Gregory VII, he was consecrated Bishop of Grenoble two years later and laboured zealously, though unsuccessfully, to eradicate the evils of simony and concubinage in his diocese. In 1082, convinced of his own ineffectiveness, he resigned from the Bishopric and proceeded to become a Benedictine monk at the austere abbey of La Chaise-Dieu in Auvergne; at the express orders of the Pope, however, he returned to his post the following year and there remained until he was called to his reward on 1 April 1132, what with each of the four succeeding Popes turning down his plea to be relieved of his duties. He was canonized two years later by Innocent III.



      In 1084 Hugh personally conducted Saint Bruno and his six companions to the wild Alpine solitude near Grenoble, called Chartreuse, and presented them with the land on which they went on to construct their first Carthusian monastery, the Grande Chartreuse (Great Chartre-house). Here he frequently shared in the monks’ life of poverty, prayer and solitary study. His personal austerity and excessive fasts that affected his health were only moderated at the insistence of Saint Bruno, his spiritual director.



      To relieve the distress of the poor, Hugh, who always travelled on foot, is said to have disposed of his golden chalice and Episcopal ring and was famed for his equitable judgements, which were readily accepted by both litigants.
