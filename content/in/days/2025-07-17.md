  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Matthew 11:28–30"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Reflection: 1



      Jesus invites us to go to him with all our burdens and hardships, and promises to refresh us. He is gentle and humble of heart, and he wants us to learn from him to be like him. We should learn how to follow him in our lives and imitate him. If we let him place his yoke on us, he will give us the strength and ability to carry it. He assures us that his yoke, that is, his teaching, is easy and his burden is light. Jesus is different from all other rulers; his only law is hat of love. We can trustingly unburden ourselves to him, and he will give rest and comfort for our souls.



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      In today’s Gospel, Jesus offers *rest to those who labour and are burdened,*if they are ready to accept his *easy yoke and light burden.*For the Orthodox Jew, religion was a matter of burdens, namely, 613 Mosaic laws and thousands of oral interpretations, which dictated every aspect of life. Jesus invites the overburdened Israel, and us, to take his yoke upon our shoulders. In Palestine, ox-yokes were made of wood and were carved to fit the ox comfortably. The yoke of Christ can be seen as the sum of our Christian responsibilities and duties. Jesus’ yoke is light because it is given with love. It is the commandment to love others as Jesus did. Besides, the yoke of Christ is not just a yoke *from* Christ but also a yoke *with* him. So, we are not yoked alone to pull the plow by our own unaided power. We are yoked together with Christ to work with him using his strength. Jesus is inviting each one of us to be yoked with him, to unite our life with him, our will with his will, our heart with his heart. By saying that his “*yoke is easy,”* Jesus means that whatever God sends us is made to fit our needs and our abilities exactly.



      The second part of Jesus’ claim is: *“My burden is light.”* Jesus does not mean that his burden is easy to carry, but that it is laid on us in love. This burden is meant to be carried in love, and love makes even the heaviest burden light. By following Jesus, one will find peace, rest, and real refreshment. We are burdened with many things: business, concerns about jobs, marriage, money, health, children, security, old age, and a thousand other things. Jesus is asking us to give him our burdens and take on his yoke. By telling us, “*Take my yoke . . . and you will find rest*,” Christ is asking us to do things the Christian way. When we are centred in God, when we follow God’s commandments, we have no heavy burdens.



      Life messages:



      1) We need to be freed from unnecessary burdens: Jesus is interested in lifting off our backs the burdens that drain us and suck the life out of us, *so that* he can place around our necks his own yoke and his burden, that bring to us, and to others through us, new life, new energy, new joy.



      2) We need to unload our burdens before the Lord. One of the functions of worship for many of us is that it gives us a time for rest and refreshment, when we let the overheated radiators of our hectic lives cool down before the Lord. This is especially true when we unload the burdens of our sins and worries and evil addictions on the altar and offer them to God during the Holy Mass.

      The yoke, Jesus refers to, is the rules and regulations people are made to observe. The religious leaders and the occupying colonial powers of the time had created innumerable rules and regulations and imposed them on people. It made life a heavy burden for them. Jesus noticed how people were getting crushed under such a heavy yoke of the rules and regulations. Jesus came as a leader with a difference. He gently unburdens them and invites them to come to him, learn from him, experience the sweetness of the law of love and enjoy the joy of rest.

    saintName: "Saint Alexius, Confessor (Fifth Century)"
    ref: "Saint Alexius, Confessor (Fifth Century)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Alexius, the only son of his parents, and who was educated in Rome, learnt from the charitable example of his parents that the riches given away to the poor remain with us forever, and that alms-deeds are a treasure transferred to heaven, with the interest of an immense reward. While yet a child he was intent on the relief of all whom he saw in distress, and thought himself obliged to those who received his charity and regarded them as his benefactors.



      Fearing lest the distraction of earthly honour separate or distance him from what is more noble or godly, Alexius, by divine inspiration, decided to renounce the world. On his wedding night, with his bride’s consent, he secretly left Rome, and giving away to the poor in the far East all that he had with him, spent several years at the gate of Our Lady’s church in that city, living on alms, until an image of Mary spoke and revealed his holiness to the people as “the man of God”. Thereupon he fled back home; but his father, failing to recognize him, received him as a beggar and employed him, allotting a corner under the staircase where he lived for seventeen years, unknown, bearing patiently the demeaning attitude of his own father, spouse and slaves.



      Eventually, when death ended this cruel martyrdom, they learned, albeit late, the truth from some of his own writings, even as God testified to his servant’s sanctity by way of many miracles.
