  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Mark 12:13–17"
    audio: null
    body: |
      What the Pharisees ask Jesus in today’s gospel is not a sincere question. It is an effort to trap him in a dilemma. If he replied “Yes, pay taxes to Caesar,” he would be hated by many people who resented the Roman presence. And if he said, “No, do not pay taxes to Caesar” he would be liable to arrest and trial by the Romans. Jesus was asked about many things in the course of his public ministry and when the question came from one was genuinely searching he took it seriously. On this occasion, however, Jesus the questioners were clearly out to trap him. Yet he gave an important answer to this question, insincere though it was. In a succinct, enigmatic fashion he declared that people should give back to Caesar what belongs to Caesar and give to God what belongs to God. The coin can be given to Caesar because it belongs to him, but what is to be given to God is something much more fundamental.



      Later in that same chapter of Mark’s gospel, Jesus spells out what is due to God — God is to be loved with all our heart, soul and mind. God is to be our first and greatest love, our primary commitment. That certainly can never be said of any human authority, be it political or otherwise. Jesus is saying that no Caesar, no political institution, no human institution can ever take the place of God in our lives.

    title: Reflection
    ref: null
    audio: null
    body: |
      “Give to Caesar what belongs to Caesar, and give to God what belongs to God” (v 17). This response of Jesus challenges us to take responsibility for honouring God and living our earthly citizenship in honourable ways. Being a Christian does not prevent us from being good citizens of our country. Our faith in Christ demands that we act as responsible citizens. Our faith requires that we fulfil our obligations to the civil government and authorities. At the same time, as believers we ought to love God in an unreserved manner by giving ourselves totally to Him in response to His love and ownership of us. We become truly God’s children when we fulfil our obligations to His kingdom precisely by fulfilling our obligations to His earthly kingdom.

      St Francis Caracciolo (Confessor) (1563 -1608)

      Baptized Ascanio at his birth on 13 October 1563 in the Abruzzi, Italy, this scion of a noble family in Naples, fell victim in his youth to a terrible skin disease akin to leprosy. However, at 22, when he dedicated his life exclusively to God’s service, he found himself suddenly cured, went on to study theology at Naples, and was ordained to the priesthood in 1587.

      In 1589, together with St John Augustine Adorno, he founded the Minor Clerks Regular, a religious Congregation (both contemplative and active) which prohibited its members from aspiring to ecclesiastical dignities, called for perpetual adoration of the Blessed Sacrament in rotation and for continuous mortification in addition to the three usual vows. At his profession, Ascanio took the name of Francis. He became the first superior of the Naples congregation, and towards the turn of the century made three trips to Spain to found houses in Madrid, Valladolid and in the famous university city of Alcala, where his Clerks were to teach science.

      Francis Caracciolo was an indirect descendant of St Thomas Aquinas on his mother’s side, and like his illustrious ancestor, was known for his angelic purity. A friend of the poor, he practised great austerities, always travelled on foot, and begged for crusts for his sustenance. God granted him the gifts of prophecy and of reading hearts. As an author he is famed particularly for his “Seven Stations of the Passion of Our Lord Jesus Christ”.

      Francis died on 4 June 1608 and was canonized by Pope Pius VII on 24 May 1807.
