      Reflection: 1



      Jesus makes the first prophesy of his passion which will be followed by several prophesies of it. Jesus knows the suffering and death that await him, and goes willingly ahead to accept them. He is going to be rejected and opposed by the leaders of the Jews who would get him killed. Jesus also knows and predicts that he will be raised from the dead on the third day. From the Gospel narratives we know how these prophesies literally came true. Through his suffering, death and resurrection Jesus completes the work the Father gave him.



      Jesus also insists on one necessary condition to follow him: self-denial and acceptance of one’s daily cross. The one who wants to follow Jesus should take the path Jesus took. If anyone loses his life for the sake of Jesus, he will in reality save it because through death he will rise in the newness of life with the risen Christ. At the same time anyone who is unwilling to sacrifice his life for the sake of Christ will actually lose it.



      Jesus has gone ahead as leader and done what he is asking us his followers to do. All earthly gain, however great, is of no profit to anyone if he loses his soul. Earthly sacrifices made for Christ paves the way to salvation and eternal life.



      *— Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      After Peter had made his famous declaration of Faith in Jesus as God and the Messiah, Jesus plainly warned his disciples about his suffering death and Resurrection. But the Apostles were unwilling to accept such a fate for their master. Hence, Jesus declared the three conditions of discipleship which he expected from his followers, as given in today’s Gospel.



      The Three Conditions: 1) Deny yourself. 2) Take up your cross. 3) Follow Me.



      1) Denying oneself involves a) cleansing of the heart by the eviction of self from the heart and the removal of all evil tendencies and addictions from the heart, with the help of the Holy Spirit, b) the enthronement of God in the heart and the dedication of oneself to Him, and c) the surrendering of one’s life to the enthroned God through the loving, selfless service of others for God’s glory.



      2) Taking up one’s cross means, not only accepting gracefully from God our pains and suffering, but also accepting the pain involved in serving others, in sharing our blessings with them, and in controlling our evil tendencies. Carrying one’s cross becomes easier when we compare our light crosses with the heavier ones given to terminally ill patients and to exploited people living under subhuman conditions. The realization that Jesus carries with us the heavier part of our cross also makes our cross-bearing easier and more salvific.



      3) ‘Follow Me’ means to follow Jesus by obeying the word of God and adjusting one’s life accordingly. The disciple should be ever ready to obey as Jesus directs him or her through His words in the Bible and through the teaching authority He has instituted in the Church.



      The paradox of saving/losing and losing/saving life: According to Bible commentators, the word “life” is here used, clearly, in a double sense: the earthly life of man in flesh and time and his eternal Life of happiness in Heaven. Hence, what Jesus means is that whoever wishes to save his (earthly), life will lose his (eternal), Life. But whoever loses his (earthly), life by spending it for Jesus and the Gospel, will save his (eternal), Life.



      Life message: We need to love the cross, wear the cross, carry the crosses we are given, and transform these God-given crosses of our life into the instruments of our salvation by working with the Holy Spirit.



      *(Fr Tony: https://frtonyshomilies.com/)*

      Jesus redeemed humanity through his sufferings and death on the cross, in keeping with which, his disciple is expected to do three things: deny himself, take up his cross daily and follow him. Jesus walks ahead on the way the disciples have to tread. By asking his disciples to take up their cross ‘daily’ Jesus wants them to know that the cross is not something that comes at death. It is a daily affair. Everyday life involves many sacrifices and the ultimate sacrifice is that of the total self which takes place at death. After challenging the disciples to walk his path, he poses a question to them to ponder – “For what does it profit a man if he gains the whole world and loses or forfeits his soul?”

    ref: "St Colette, Virgin (1381-1447)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Colette’s mother was 60 years old when she gave birth to the Saint in Picardy, France, on 13 January 1381. Her father was a carpenter at the abbey of Corbie. When still in her 11th year, she showed signs of her religious leaning by secretly repairing to the Benedictine Abbey at night to assist at Matins. Orphaned at 17, she tried the quasi-cloistered life of a Beguine; then, in search of greater austerity, she worked as a servant for the Poor Clares and Benedictines. Finally, on her director’s advice, giving away all she had, she became a Franciscan Tertiary and walled herself up as a recluse alongside the church of Our Lady at Corbie.



      Here began her celestial visions, her “voices” and visitations during which it was revealed to her that she should restore the primitive, austere spirit in the Franciscan Order, a task for which she felt herself entirely unfitted. God’s will seemed to become unmistakably clear, however, when a saintly priest and a noblewoman from far-off Savoy, complete strangers, sought her out in response to divine inspiration, and Pope Benedict XIII made her Abbess over the Poor Clares with full authority to establish reforms in all the convents.



      The nuns are discalced, strictly cloistered, maintain continuous fast and abstinence, and possess no property, not even in common. St Vincent Ferrer gave her his processional Crucifix as a sign of his esteem.



      Like St Francis, she was an ardent lover of animals and birds, and was greatly renowned for her sanctity and a life of unceasing prayer for the whole world. Taking critically ill while visiting her convents in Ghent, Belgium, she breathed her last on 6 March 1447 and was canonized in 1807.
