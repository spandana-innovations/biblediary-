  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Mathew 7: 1-5"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Abram’s call by God to leave home and move to another country marks the beginning of Israel’s salvation history. His response, when this rugged nomad migrated to the land of promise eventually brought a great blessing for everyone on earth. The mystery of divine providence cannot be explained in any logical way, why some are chosen and others seem (relatively) unchosen. The classic explanation is offered by Deuteronomy, where Moses says: “It was not because you are the largest of all nations that the Lord set his heart on you and chose you, for you are the smallest of all nations. It was because the Lord loved you, that he brought you out with his strong hand from the place of slavery” (Deut 7:7-8). From this text we see that God’s basic requirement of them was a sense of humble gratitude.</span></span></span></span></span>



      Abram left Haran in upper Syria, going into an unknown land and leaving behind his relatives and his home and everything he knew, for the sake of a promise and a blessing. Even the new land was also to remain promised, never completely possessed. God said to Israel, “The land is mine; you are but aliens who have become my tenants” (Lev 25:23). Land was to be shared, so that no one would be homeless among God’s people. Never to possess it absolutely, but always to receive as a gift meant that Israel was to be “the smallest of all nations.”</span></span></span></span></span>



      The ideals of love and humility are implicit in today’s Gospel. The humble person cannot be judgmental towards others. If we dare to judge their neighbours, they may turn out to be more righteous than ourselves. Humble people will not lose the promised land, the divine blessing promised to Abraham. Humility towards God sparks in us a spirit of kindliness towards our neighbour. To inherit what was promised to Abraham we need to respect the earth and preserve the environment, be disposed to think well of our neighbour, and be generous in sharing with them the blessings of God that we have received.</span></span></span></span></span>

  - key: reflection
    title: Reflection
    ref: null
    audio: null
    body: |
      In the Gospel, Jesus warns against hypocritically judging others. Instead, he encourages self-reflection and self-scrutiny without passing judgement on others. As stated by Wayne Dyer, “Judgements prevent us from seeing the good that lies beyond appearances”. When we judge others, we may overlook our own flaws. Self-awareness allows us to empathise with others and recognise our own shortcomings. While the Scripture urges us to offer guidance and correction, it also emphasises that passing judgement is not our role. Only God comprehensively knows us, while our understanding remains partial and subjective. As we make an effort to understand others, we can learn to love and accept them with their limitations. Every time we pass a judgement, let’s remember, “We must all appear before the judgement seat of Christ” (2 Cor 5:10).</span></span></span>

      St Paulinus of Nola (*Bishop, Confessor) (c.353 -431)*</span></span></span></span>



      Pontius Meropius Anicius Paulinus was born near Bordeaux (France), the son of a wealthy Roman patrician who at that time held the post of praetorian prefect of Gaul. He received his education from the poet Ausonius and at 25, already a noted orator and poet, was made senator. He was appointed prefect of Rome and governor of the province of Campagna by the Emperor Valentinian.</span></span></span></span>



      Not long after his marriage to Therasia, a Spanish Christian, he was himself baptized in 385, and when their only son died in infancy, they went to live in Spain for some years, and here the inhabitants of Barcelona insisted on his being ordained a priest. Therasia thereupon sold her Spanish possessions for the relief of the poor and became a ‘nun’, while Paulinus, after spending some time with St Ambrose in Milan, settled down to lead a hermit’s life at Nola near Naples (Italy). Here he had, years before, witnessed certain miracles performed at the tomb of St Felix, the martyred Bishop of Nola, and had a hospital erected nearby at his own expense. He also contributed lavishly to the erection of a great basilica in honour of St Felix who had by then become his special patron. In 409 he was elected Bishop of Nola, a See he governed with admirable success for over 20 years. He is gratefully remembered for the aqueduct he built to the town to give the people ample fresh water.</span></span></span></span>



      One of the most cultured men of his age, Paulinus became famed for his scientific knowledge, his ecclesiastical writings and Christian poems. He had a miraculous power over the forces of evil.</span></span></span></span>






      St John Fisher, </span></span></span>*Cardinal Bishop and Martyr (A.D. 1469-1535)</span></span>*</span></span></span>



      At 14, St John Fisher went to Cambridge University (England), where he distinguished himself in his studies, was elected a fellow of Michaelhouse, and was ordained priest with special permission since he was only 22. He became successively Senior Proctor, Doctor of Divinity, Master of Michaelhouse, Vice-Chancellor of Cambridge and chaplain to Lady Margaret Beaufort, mother of King Henry VII.</span></span></span></span>



      In 1504, he was elected Chancellor of the University and was nominated Bishop of Rochester by King Henry VIII. Observing strict austerity, he carried out his pastoral duties with great zeal, held visitations, administered the sacrament of confirmation, disciplined the clergy, visited the sick poor in their hovels, distributed alms, and limited his sleep to four hours, keeping a skull before him at meal times to remind himself of death. Books were his only pleasure.</span></span></span></span>



      Staunchly upholding the sanctity of marriage, he courageously championed for the rights of the Church and the supremacy of the Pope, even speaking against accepting the king as supreme head of the Church of England. Confined to the Tower for a year without trial, he was questioned repeatedly, Fisher standing his ground, stating unequivocally that the “king was not, nor could be, by the law of God, Supreme Head” of the Church. Summarily condemned to death, he was executed in 1535; his body was given a rough burial in the churchyard of All Hallows near the Tower while his head was displayed on London Bridge.</span></span></span></span>



      As his place of burial attracted many who venerated him, his body was reburied in the Tower church of St Peter-ad-Vincula. Some 450 years later, i.e., in 1935, he was canonized by Pope Pius XI.</span></span></span></span>

      St Thomas More (*Martyr*)*(1477-1535)*</span></span></span></span>



      Described as “a man for all seasons” by Erasmus, Thomas was the son of a London Judge named John More. A lawyer by profession, he was deeply religious, and followed the Spiritual exercises of the Carthusian monks for four years before, at 27, marrying Jane Colt who bore him three daughters and a son. Jane having died in the 6th year of their happy union, Thomas married the widow Alice Middleton, who proved herself an excellent housewife and loving foster-mother to his four young children.</span></span></span></span>



      Elected to Parliament at 26, Thomas, as under-sheriff of London, was “the best friend the poor ever had”. In 1529 he was appointed Lord Chancellor of England. But neither honour nor power lessened his interest in the Faith and the value-based education of his children. </span></span></span></span>



      When Henry VIII, having had no male heir by his Queen, Catherine of Aragon, was declined an annulment by Rome in order to marry Anne Boleyn, he had himself declared head of the Church of England. Thomas promptly resigned his chancellorship in protest and prepared himself and his family for the consequences.</span></span></span></span>



      Sentenced to death, More, prematurely aged and bent from 15 months of imprisonment, while ascending the scaffold, humorously cautioned the lieutenant who was to behead him thus: “See me safely up; as for my coming down, I can shift for myself!”</span></span></span></span>



      Canonized in 1935, Thomas was declared patron of political leaders in 2000.</span></span></span></span>
