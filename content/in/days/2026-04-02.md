      Reflection: 1



      Jesus came to serve and not to be served. At the Last Supper he who is Master and Lord offers the humble service of washing the feet of his disciples. His purpose in washing their feet was not merely to clean them, but to purify their hearts and minds to receive the Bread of Life. Jesus is the one who purifies, and all of us have to humbly allow him to purify us and wash our sins away. He manifests his humility and mercy through this striking gesture. And he asks us to continue to do what he has done. The foot-washing Jesus does is a prophetic action symbolizing his self-emptying that culminates in his salvific death on the Cross. Truly he loves us to the end, never withdraws his love from us even when we are sinful and unfaithful to him. Are we ready to love others as he loves us, and to offer humble service to them?



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      We celebrate three anniversaries on Holy Thursday: 1) the anniversary of the first Holy Mass; 2) the anniversary of the institution of ministerial priesthood in order to perpetuate the Holy Mass, to convey God’s forgiveness to repentant sinners, and to preach the Good News of salvation; 3) the anniversary of Jesus’ promulgation of his new commandment of love: “*Love one another as I have loved you” (Jn 13:34).*



      First, we remember how Jesus transformed the Jewish Passover into the New Testament Passover. The Jewish Passover was, in fact, a joint celebration of two ancient thanksgiving celebrations. The descendants of Abel, who were shepherds, used to lead their sheep from the winter pastures to the summer pastures after the sacrificial offering of a lamb to God. They called this celebration the “*Pass over.*” The descendants of Cain, who were farmers, held a harvest festival called the *Massoth* in which they offered unleavened bread to God as an act of thanksgiving. The Passover feast of the Israelites (Ex 12:26-37) harmoniously combined these two feasts in a ritual meal instituted by God, to be celebrated yearly, thanking Him for His miraculous liberation of their ancestors from Egyptian slavery, their exodus from Egypt, and their final arrival in the Promised Land. (A homily starter anecdote may be given)



      Scripture lessons summarized: In the first reading, God gives the Hebrews two instructions: prepare for the moment of liberation by a ritual meal, and make a symbolic mark on your homes to exempt yourselves from the coming slaughter. In the second reading, Paul teaches that the celebration of the Lord’s Supper was an unbroken tradition from the very beginning of the Church, by which Christians reminded themselves of the death and Resurrection of Jesus.



      Today’s Gospel describes how Jesus transformed the Jewish Passover into the Eucharistic celebration. After washing the feet of his apostles and commanding them to do humble service for each other, Jesus concluded the Seder meal with its roasted Paschal lamb by giving his apostles his own body and blood under the appearances of bread and wine as spiritual food and drink.



      <span class="subhead">Life Messages:</span> 1)A challenge for humble service. Our celebration of the Eucharist requires that we wash one another’s feet, *i.e.,* serve one another, and revere Christ’s presence in other persons. In practical terms, that means we are to consider others’ needs to be as important as our own and to serve their needs, without expecting any reward.



      2)A loving invitation for sacrificial sharing and self-giving love. Let us imitate the self-giving model of Jesus who shares with us his own Body and Blood and who enriches us with his *Real Presence* in the Holy Eucharist. It is by sharing our blessings – our talents, time, health, and wealth – with others, that we become true disciples of Christ and obey his new commandment: “*Love one another as I have loved you.”*3)An invitation to become Christ-bearers and Christ-conveyers: “*Go forth, the Mass is ended*,” really means, “Go in peace to love and serve one another.’’ We are to carry Jesus to our homes, our places of work, our schools, and our communities, conveying to others around us the love, mercy, forgiveness, and spirit of humble service of Christ whom we carry with us.

      The Holy Thursday evening Mass of the Lord’s Supper is a deeply moving celebration that marks the beginning of the Easter Triduum. This Mass commemorates Jesus’ institution of the Eucharist and the priesthood, as well as His profound act of humility in washing His disciples’ feet. The liturgy is solemn yet joyful, focusing on love, service, and sacrifice. When Jesus broke bread and shared wine with His apostles, He offered Himself as spiritual nourishment, symbolising His love for humanity. The washing of the feet is a powerful reminder of our call to serve one another with humility and compassion. The Mass concludes with the procession to the altar of repose, inviting us to accompany Jesus in His agony in the Garden of Gethsemane. This evening, we are drawn into Jesus’ final hours, reflecting on His selfless love and the journey that lies ahead toward the cross and resurrection.

    body: |
      The belief of Robert Martolella’s parents that his birth, after years of childlessness, was the result of the intercession of Saint Francis d’Assisi was further reinforced with the healing they obtained through his intercession for their young infant’s eye. The boy was 13 when they fulfilled their vow of having him spend a year in a Franciscan monastery, donning a Franciscan habit, in accordance with medieval practice, where he proved himself a model of extraordinary humility, abnegation and love of prayer.



      Returning from a pilgrimage to Rome, Assisi and other places, he took the name of Francis and began the austere, solitary life in a lonely cave on the sea-coast where, in 1435, he built a chapel and three cells when two companions joined him as anchorites, thus forming the nucleus of the Order of the Hermits of Saint Francis, later known as ‘Minims’, “the least in the House of God”. By 1454 there were monasteries established in southern Italy and Sicily, apart from a Second Order for nuns and a Third Order for those who would live out his charism in the world. Central to the severity of their life was charity, humility and poverty, abstinence covering all meat and animal products.



      Outspoken in reproving even the King of Naples for his evil ways, Francis’ fame reached France where the dying Louis XI sought him out before dying a penitent death. Resultantly, he spent 25 years in France as counsellor to Charles VIII and Louis XII, successive occupants of the throne, restoring peace between France and Spain.



      Francis died on Good Friday 1507, aged 91, leaving behind a legacy of 400 convents, the Order’s five provinces covering Italy, France, Germany and Spain, and was canonized in 1519.
