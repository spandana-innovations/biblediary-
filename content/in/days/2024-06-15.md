  - key: homily

    ref: "Matthew 5:33–37"

    body: |
      Spiritually, we are often like adolescents, old enough to be responsible but capable of blundering; far enough along to have a clear grasp of ethical and moral guidelines and yet often slipping backward and needing God’s forgiveness and patience. In various ways, Pope Francis manages to convey both idealism and a gritty awareness of our flawed, human condition. Despite our awareness of personal imperfections, he says, we should reach out to the future in hope, full of the joy of the Gospel.



      A sense of being part of a new creation appear in both readings. Elijah threw his cloak over the young man Elisha, showing that an older generation was passing on to a new generation the task of interpreting God’s will in changed times. A similarly radical challenge is heard from Jesus in today’s gospel: “Do not swear at all. Take no oaths, but say Yes when you mean Yes and No when you mean No.”



      The kingdom of God is a wonderful ideal and dream for humanity, but are Jesus’ directives in the Sermon on the Mount literally possible in this world of ours? Some Christian groups try to follow them literally, keeping their speech simple and exact, never exaggerating or embroidering or purveying fake news. Most people, however, feel the need to say more than a crisp “Yes” or an absolute “No.” We consider it fair that others need to check out our ID card and our driver’s license, and we are willing in court to swear on the Bible that our words are true. We and our world are not yet fully there, in kingdom mode!

      (Association of Catholic Priests: Homily Resources).
  - key: reflection
    title: Reflection
    ref: null
    audio: null
    body: |
      Jesus is vehemently against the practice of taking oaths in the name of God or even any earthly object. To invoke the name of God in an oath when our statement is false or when we do not intend to honour our words is a direct violation of the Third Commandment. God expects us to be persons of our word. A person of honesty doesn’t need to punctuate his/her sentences with playful oaths and promises. All speech should be such as to indicate an obligation to tell the truth and a commitment to carry it out. It doesn’t matter what the cultural view of truth and reliability is, we are to be truthful before God and with each other. After all, God knows our intentions and our actions.

      St Germaine Cousin (Virgin) (c.1579 - 1601)

      The daughter of a peasant named Laurent Cousin in Pibrac near Toulouse, France, Germaine was born with a deformed hand and suffered from scrofula (swelling of the glands). While still little, her mother died, and when her father remarried, her stepmother treated her with the utmost cruelty and insisted on her being ostracized, compelling her to sleep with the sheep in the stable. God rewarded the humility and patience with which she bore all manner of suffering and neglect, by granting her a marvellous sense of his presence, and a deep love for the Holy Eucharist and the Blessed Virgin.

      Germaine used to assemble the village children and teach them to love Jesus and Mary; from her scanty allowance of food which consisted of leftovers from the family table she managed to distribute portions to those who appeared to be in still greater misery than herself.

      In reparation for the sacrileges of which the heretics in neighbouring towns were guilty, Germaine subjected herself to additional austerities; people were just beginning to realize her deep-rooted sanctity when God called her to himself in her 22nd year. She was canonized by Pope Pius IX on 29 June 1867. Every year an annual pilgrimage takes place on 15 June to the church in Pibrac where her incorrupt body still rests.
