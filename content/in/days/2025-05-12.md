      Reflection: 1



      Anyone who enters the flock through any way other than the door is a thief and a robber. But the Real shepherd enters through the door and the sheep hear his voice and follow him as he goes before them. They will not follow any stranger. Many came before Jesus, pretending to be shepherds, but they were thieves and robbers, and the sheep did not listen to them. Jesus is the door, the sure way to enter the safety of the sheepfold. Jesus has come that those who follow him may have life in abundance. When we enter through Jesus, we enter into eternal salvation. Through Jesus we come to God and to eternal life. Jesus sacrificed himself for the benefit of his sheep, unlike the Jewish leaders who came to steal and to kill. No one can have salvation except through Jesus.



      *— Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      Jesus fulfils Old Testament promises from several of the prophets that God himself will come to shepherd His people. Isaiah 40:11 declares, “He will feed his flock like a shepherd,” and in Ezekiel 34:15 we read, “I myself will be the shepherd of my sheep, says the Lord.”



      For Jesus to use the example of being a good shepherd makes sense, because that was one of the major occupations throughout Palestine in his day. His listeners knew about them. People that cared for flocks of sheep were of the lowest socioeconomic level, with little to no education. They had an important responsibility, however, of providing for and protecting sheep, sometimes sacrificially.



      “The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep” implies that this kind of shepherd stands ready to sacrifice his total self if need be. Three times in these eight verses we hear Jesus make this prophetic statement. It alludes, of course, to his sacrificing his own life for us as the means of our reconciliation to God.



      In these days of 2021, my guess is that few of us know any individuals who actually work as a shepherd, but it’s quite likely that we do know people who give of themselves to shepherd others along the right path. This too requires providing what’s needed (direction, encouragement, friendship) plus offering protection if circumstances warrant. And yes, there could be some sacrifice involved, especially in the giving of personal time.



      How willing are we to intentionally shepherd others toward a life of loving God and our neighbours, thereby helping to bring them into the fold so they can “listen to Jesus’ voice”? This is the voice of the one Good Shepherd, who “knows his own and his own know him.”

      Jesus draws a metaphor from the imagery of a sheep pen, an enclosure usually made of stone walls. Historians have found evidence that the shepherds would lie across the front of the pen and would act as a guard. They were effectively a door or gate for the sheep pen. This was not only to prevent predators from entering but also to prevent sheep from leaving. Jesus invites us to acknowledge him as our door. We enter and exit through him alone. There are many possible doors before us to enter and thus end in perdition. When we enter through him, i.e., live according to his Gospel values, we enter into salvation, and he will not let any enemy enter into our lives.

      1. Sts Nereus, Achilleus and Domitilla *Martyrs (1st Century)*



      The veneration of Sts Nereus, Achilleus and Domitilla is very ancient, going back to the 4th century. It was on the occasion of their festival, which was observed with some solemnity in Rome 200 years later, that Pope St Gregory the Great delivered his 28th homily— “These saints, before whom we are assembled, despised the world and trampled it underfoot when it offered them riches and pleasures”. The church in which he spoke was built over their tomb in the cemetery of Domitilla, on the Via Ardeatina.



      Nereus and Achilleus were members of the Praetorian Guard, the elite corps of soldiers entrusted with guarding the very person of the Roman emperor. According to an inscription found in the tomb over their remains in 1874 — in the family vault in the cemetery of St Domitilla, in the underground church built by Pope St Siricius in 390 — they were ordered by Emperor Domitian to slay his niece Flavia Domitilla on account of her allegiance to the Christian Faith. But moved by her example they became Christians themselves and refused to carry out the order. They were thus banished to Ponza and later, under the reign of the Emperor Trajan, to the island of Terrecina. Here they were martyred — Nereus and Achilleus were beheaded and Domitilla, whose banishment St Jerome describes as one, long, martyrdom, was burnt alive.



      2. St Pancras *Martyr (A.D. -304)*



      In the persecution under Diocletian this 14 year-old saint, inspired by the example and words of his uncle, Denys, who was in bonds for the faith, offered himself voluntarily as a martyr. Supposed to have been a Syrian or Phrygian orphan who was brought up by Denys, together with whom he was converted to Christianity, he defended boldly before the Emperor the cause of the Christians.



      He was beheaded by the sword on the Via Aurelia in Rome and his body was buried in the cemetery of Calepodius which afterwards took his name. And about the year 508 Pope Symmachius built a basilica over his tomb near the Potra de San Pauerazion.



      In the 7th century, Pope St Vitalian sent some relics of St Pancras to England and within a short period of time, the young saint became very popular. The first church erected in Canterbury by St Augustine was dedicated to him. There is also a railway station in London named after him. It was under his invocation that St Gregory the Great had previously built a monastery for Benedictines.
