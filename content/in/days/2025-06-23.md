    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Matthew 7:1–5"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Reflection: 1



      None of us is perfect, and therefore none of us is capable of making just and right judgement on others. We are inclined to notice even the small defects of others and exaggerate them, while we easily ignore our own major defects or even justify hem. If that is the case, pretending to help others to correct their mistakes is not an act of charity but of hypocrisy; it is an act of self-promotion to show others that we are better than they are. Having a positive approach to others and speaking well of them can produce good results. Fault-finding and nagging will invite God’s judgement upon us. We must be wise enough to distinguish between good and evil, but should not make ourselves judges over others. Since all of us are sinners, none of us has the right to condemn anyone. </span></span></span></span>



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP</span></span>*</span></span>



      Reflection: 2



      In today’s passage, taken from the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus condemns our careless, malicious, and rash judgments about others’ feelings, motives, behaviour or actions.



      Reasons why we should not judge others: 1) No one, except God, is good enough, and only He has the right and authority, to judge us, because only He sees the whole truth, and only He can read the human heart.



      2) We do not see all the facts or circumstances, nor the power of the temptation, behind a person’s evil deed.



      3) We have no right to judge others because we have the same faults as the ones we are judging and often to a higher degree (remember Jesus’ funny example of a man with a wooden beam in his eye trying to remove the dust particle from another’s eye?) St. Philip Neri commented, watching the misbehaviour of a drunkard: “There goes Philip but for the grace of God.”



      4) We are often prejudiced in our judgment of others, and total fairness cannot be expected from us.



      Life message: 1) Let us leave the judgment to God and refrain from being critical and judgmental. Let us remember the advice of saints: “When you point one finger of accusation at another, three of your fingers point at you. Let us also heed the Jewish rabbi’s reminder, “He who judges others favourably will be judged favourably by God.”



      *(Fr Tony: https://frtonyshomilies.com/)*
  - key: reflection
    title: Reflection
    ref: null
    audio: null
    body: |
      It is said that God does not judge anyone. We earn our judgement by our deeds or misdeeds, especially our acts of judging and condemning others. God does not want any of us to assume the role of a judge for the very reason that no human being is perfect and capable of judging. It is a common tendency to notice and exaggerate the ‘speck’ in others’ eyes while ignoring the ‘log’ in one’s own. Most often the person who tends to find fault with others is the one guilty of those very faults. Jesus never passed judgment on others, even when he knew they were at fault. If we are generous in being positive and speaking well about others, we get good things in return. On the other hand, if we indulge in judging, condemning and gossiping, we get a harsh judgement from God. What is my approach and attitude to others who are imperfect like me?
  - key: saint
    title: Saint of the Day
    saintName: "St Joseph Cafasso (Confessor) (1811-1860)"
    ref: "St Joseph Cafasso (Confessor) (1811-1860)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Born of deeply religious parents at Castelnuovo, Italy, Joseph Cafasso was, as a boy called the “little saint”, and though poor, his talents, industry and piety saw him ordained priest at the early age of 22. He was made prefect, then professor of moral theology, and finally rector of the seminary of Turin. Fr Cafasso, the “Pearl of the Italian clergy”, though small of stature and somewhat deformed, had been the source of blessing not only for the clergy and people of Turin, but the whole world as the spiritual director of the great apostle of charity, St John Bosco. Cafasso had the confidence of all, young and old, rich and poor, even as clergy, bishops, princes and, indeed, all classes of people benefited from his ministrations in the confessional and his Retreats.



      He evinced keen interest in the work of St Joseph Cottolengo, the indefatigable advocate of the orphans, the poor and the distressed of all kinds and founder of the “Little House of Divine Providence”.



      Joseph Cafasso, who died in 1860, was beatified in 1895 and canonized in 1947.
