      Reflection: 1



      Jesus wants us to be perfect as the heavenly Father is perfect. No one can be perfect in the same degree as He is, or else there would be many gods! But God’s perfection is His mercy (Lk 6: 36), and we are called to be perfect because our Father is perfect. Perfection for us consists in imitating the Father. Jesus wants us to overcome our antagonisms, prejudices and hostilities which are the opposite of mercy. Our love must be extended to every human being, even to our enemies, and reflect God’s unconditional love. We have to always keep on going beyond our personal interests and participate in God’s universal love. Praying for our enemies is one way of doing so. We should learn to love others in the same way as God loves us in spite of our defects and failures. Let us examine our life and attitudes on the basis of this standard, and make the necessary corrections.



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      Today’s Gospel passage is perhaps the central and the most famous section of the Sermon on the Mount. It gives us the Christian ethic of personal relationship: love one’s enemies, as well as one’s neighbours, and show one’s love for one’s enemies by forgiving them and praying for them. Above all, it tells us that what makes Christians different is the grace with which we interact with others, treating them with loving kindness and mercy, especially when those others seemingly don’t deserve it. The Old Law never said to hate enemies, but that was the way some Jews understood it. Jesus commands us to love our enemies and pray for those who persecute us in order todemonstrate that we are children of a merciful Heavenly Father. From the cross, Jesus, living what he preached, did as he commands us to do, praying for Mercy to God His Father for all of those who were responsible for the Crucifixion – which includes all fallen humankind, and so ourselves — saying, ‘*Father forgive them; they know not what they do.’” (Lk 23:34).* A Christian has no personal enemies. If we only love our friends, we are no different from pagans or atheists.



      We need to love our neighbours and our enemies, too: The Greek word used for loving enemies is not *storage*(= affection or natural love towards family members), or *philia (= friendship,*love of close friends), or *eros (= romance) (*passionate love between a young man and woman*),*but *agápe (= unconditional love)*which is the invincible benevolence, or good will, for another’s highest good. Since*agápe,* or unconditional love, is not natural, so practicing it is possible only with God’s help. *Agápe* love is a choice more than a feeling. We choose to love our enemies because Jesus loved them enough to die for them, and they, too, are the children of our God. We have in the Acts of the Apostles the example of St. Stephen, the first Christian martyr, who, like Jesus on the cross, prayed for those who were putting him to death.



      <span class="subhead">Life Messages: </span>We are to try to be perfect, to be like God: 1) We become perfect when we fulfill God’s purpose in creating us: with His help, to become God-like. 2) We become perfect when, with His ongoing help, we try to love as God loves, to forgive as God forgives, and to show unconditional good will and universal benevolence as God does. Perfection means we are striving to live each and every moment doing God’s will, using or cooperating with the grace of God.

    body: |
      At the heart of today’s Gospel is the concept of God-level forgiveness, a forgiveness that goes beyond human instinct and reflects the heart of God. Forgiving our enemies is one of the most difficult things to do, but it is the path to spiritual maturity. By forgiving those who have wronged us, we participate in the divine love of God, which heals and restores both us and those we forgive. One practical way to extend love to our enemies is by praying for them. When we pray for someone who has hurt us, it shifts our heart and perspective, allowing us to see them through the lens of God’s compassion and mercy. When we strive to forgive like God, we get transformed, and through our love, we can transform the world around us.

    body: |
      The Martyrs of Alexandria belong to the earliest generations of Christians who suffered for their faith in one of the most influential cities of the ancient world. Alexandria, a center of philosophy, science, and religion, also became a place of intense persecution when Christians refused to worship the Roman gods and the emperor.



      During the persecutions under Emperors Decius (c. 250 AD) and Diocletian (c. 303–311 AD), many believers in Alexandria—bishops, priests, consecrated virgins, teachers, and ordinary lay people—were imprisoned, tortured, and executed. Some were burned alive, others beheaded, and many died in public arenas as witnesses to Christ.



      The Church remembers them not as victims, but as faithful witnesses (martyrs) whose blood became the seed of Christianity in Egypt. Their courage strengthened the Alexandrian Church, which later gave the world great saints, theologians, and monks.



      Their memory calls us today to remain firm in faith, to resist injustice, and to bear witness to Christ through lives of integrity, compassion, and hope.
