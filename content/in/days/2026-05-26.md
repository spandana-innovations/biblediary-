  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Mark 10: 28-31"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Reflection: 1



      Jesus speaks of the rewards of discipleship. By renewing their natural families, the disciples of Jesus become members of the new family of God, and they will enjoy his superabundant blessings. Jesus adds that his disciples will have to endure persecutions; it will be part of their life. And passing through persecutions, they will receive eternal life. Jesus himself faced persecution and crucifixion, and his disciples follow him. Eternal life encompasses a total reversal of earthly values: many who are first will be last, and the last, first. Those who are considered the last and the least will be first in the kingdom of heaven by following Jesus and giving themselves to him. Peter and the other disciples have “left everything” and followed Jesus. Peter’s words express a desire for some reward. Jesus answers him that whatever is given up for the sake of Jesus is never lost.



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      A rich young man approached Jesus asking how to gain eternal life. Jesus asked him to sell his possessions share the money with the poor and then become his disciple. But the rich man went away sad, unable to accept Jesus’ terms and conditions. Watching this scene, Peter declares that he and his fellow Apostles, all Jesus’ followers, have left everything and followed Jesus, and he asks what their reward will be.



       Jesus’ warning and promise: Jesus wants every Christian to embrace the virtue of poverty of spirit by practicing real and effective austerity in the possession and use of material things. But those who are specially called to Christian ministry, particularly the Apostles and their successors in priestly and religious ministry, should practice absolute detachment from property, time, family, etc. so that they can be fully available to everyone, imitating Jesus himself. Such detachment gives them lordship over all things. They are no longer the slaves of things and the burden things involve. They will be able to share St. Paul’s attitude and live, "As having nothing, and yet possessing everything" (2 Cor 6:10). Jesus also considers persecutions and troubles as rewards because they help us to give powerful witness to the Good News and offer us opportunities to grow in maturity and responsibility. Jesus assures Peter and the Apostles (and us), that anyone who has generously left behind his possessions will be rewarded a hundred times over in this life and will have eternal bliss in the next life. By shedding their selfishness in this way, they will acquire charity, and, having charity, they will gain everything. In place of material wealth, Jesus promises all his disciples the blessing and joy of rich fellowship with the community of believers. These words of our Lord particularly apply to those who by Divine vocation embrace celibacy, giving up their right to form a family. They will become members of every family, and they will have many brothers, sisters and spiritual children.



      Life message: 1) Let us try to become true disciples of Jesus by sacrificially sharing our blessings with those around us, thereby inheriting additional blessings from a generous God. Let us not refuse anything to him or hold back anything from him.

      Jesus promises rewards for those who leave everything to follow Him. Those who leave family and dear ones and property will receive a hundredfold in this life. They are also going to receive a new family of brothers and sisters. Christians are spiritually bonded together. When we leave our natural families, we are going to get a new family of believers. As for the future reward, Jesus promises eternal life to those who follow Him. But persecution is inevitable for the followers of Christ. Christians of all ages have faced opposition and persecution. They are well aware that Jesus too faced persecution, and so suffering is part of the Christian vocation. Christians are not afraid to face suffering, for it is part of their vocation as disciples of Jesus. They are working for the establishment of God’s Kingdom, which is done through hard work and suffering.
  - key: saint
    title: Saint of the Day
    saintName: "Saint Philip Neri, Confessor (c.1515-1595)"
    ref: "Saint Philip Neri, Confessor (c.1515-1595)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Giving up his commercial activities at the age of 26, Philip Neri went to Rome to seek his own sanctification and that of his fellowmen. He led a disciplined life of prayer and penance and studied philosophy and theology; he frequently visited the Seven Churches—a 12-mile pilgrimage which he would perform on foot after sundown, often spending entire nights in the catacomb of St Sebastian. In 1548 he founded a confraternity to care for poor pilgrims and started the monthly adoration of the Blessed Sacrament.



      Ordained priest in 1551, he went on to become an outstanding confessor and counsellor, combining as he did popularity with deep piety against the background of a corrupt Rome and a disinterested clergy. He would celebrate Mass with loving delight and devote his afternoons to engaging young men and boys in talks, games or visits to the Seven Churches, bringing home to the minds of people spiritual truths in the most unusual but efficacious ways. From 1564, several of his followers became priests and settled down with him at the Church of San Giovanni de Fiorentini, praying and eating in common, celebrating the Eucharist and preaching regularly. This sowed the seed of what was to become known as the “Oratory”, whose distinctive feature was the regular afternoon session of informal talks held in a little room above the Church concerning spiritual matters.



      An outstanding mystic of the Counter-Reformation, Philip passed away on the Feast of Corpus Christi, 1595 and was canonized in 1622.
