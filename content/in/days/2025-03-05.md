      Reflection: 1



      Doing good things like praying, fasting, giving alms etc. to be seen and admired by people and to </span></span></span></span>be considered by them as pious and holy persons is of no spiritual advantage to the doers. They </span></span></span></span>are doing such things not out of love for God or neighbour but out of a desire for self-glorification. Their devotion is mere hypocrisy. Their only reward is the appreciation they receive from those who see them; they do not receive any reward from God. What is done for a show ends up as a show and brings no spiritual advantage. Good things have to be done with good intentions, and not to win the hero worship of those around. </span></span></span></span>



      God will never fail to see the good intention of the doer and never fail to reward him or her. Doing good things only for public view is one of the games people play while in private they refuse to do such good deeds. But what a person really is what he or she is on the inside, in heart and conscience. And that is what God takes count of. Therefore, we should not publicize our good deeds but do them sincerely in the presence of God. </span></span></span></span>



      *— Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP</span></span>*Reflection: 2



      Ash Wednesday (*dies cinerum*), is the Church’s Yom Kippur or the “Day of Atonement.” Its very name comes from the Jewish practice of doing penance wearing “sackcloth and ashes.” The Old Testament tells us how the people of Nineveh, King Ben Hadad of Syria, and Queen Esther fasted, wearing sackcloth and ashes. In the early Church, Christians who had committed serious sins were instructed to do public penance wearing sackcloth and ashes. The Church instructs us to observe Ash Wednesday and all Fridays of Lent, including Good Friday, as days of full fast and abstinence. Fasting is prescribed to reinforce our penitential prayer during the Lenten season.



      Scripture lessons summarized: The prophet Joel, in the first reading, insists that we should experience a complete conversion of heart and not simply regret for our sins. Today’s Responsorial Psalm, (Ps 51), offers us an act of contrition with a plea for mercy and a prayer for a “clean heart” with which to live holy lives. Saint Paul, in the second reading, advises us “to become reconciled to God.” Today’s Gospel instructs us to assimilate the true spirit of fasting and prayer.



      The Blessing of the Ashes and the Significance of the Day: The priest dipping his thumb into ashes (collected from burnt palms of the previous year’s Palm Sunday), marks the forehead of each of us with the sign of the cross saying the words, “Remember that you are dust and to dust you will return,” or “Repent and believe in the Gospel.” By marking the sign of the cross with ashes on the foreheads of her children, the Church gives us: 1- a firm conviction that a) we are mortal beings, b) our bodies will become dust when buried and ashes if cremated, and c) our life-span is very brief and unpredictable; 2- a strong warning that we will suffer eternal misery if we do not repent of our sins, become reconciled with God, asking His pardon and forgiveness, and do penance; and 3- a loving invitation to realize and acknowledge our sinful condition and return to our loving and forgiving God with true repentance and a renewal of our life as the prodigal son did.



      Ash Wednesday messages:



      # 1: We need to purify and renew our lives during the period of Lent by repentance, which means expressing sorrow for sins by turning away from occasions of sins and making a right turn to God. We need to express our repentance by becoming reconciled with God daily, by asking for forgiveness from those whom we have offended and by giving unconditional forgiveness to those who have offended us.



      # 2: We need to do prayerful fasting and acts of penance for our sins, following the example of Jesus before his public ministry. Fasting reduces our “spiritual obesity” or the excessive accumulation of “fat” in our soul in the form of evil tendencies, evil habits and evil addictions. It also gives us additional moral and spiritual strength and encourages us to share our blessings with the needy. It offers us more time to be with God in prayer. It encourages us to share our food and goods with the needy. Fasting also makes our minds clearer and more receptive to receive the sacred nourishment of God’s word and Holy Eucharist.



      *(Thomas Merton, OCSO : https://frtonyshomilies.com/)*

      Jesus not only asks his disciples to practise righteousness but also tells them to practise it in the secret way which is the right way. Fasting, almsgiving and prayer need to be practised in secret. These are actions performed solely before our God. They should not be advertised or publicized. Any advertisement or publicity will rob us of the reward. The instruction “do not let your left hand know what your right hand is doing” shows how significant it is to maintain due secrecy of these acts. The Church, by presenting this teaching on the very first day of Lent, i.e. Ash Wednesday, wants us to practise all the spiritual acts of the Lenten season without making a show of the same. These are actions to be performed out of genuine love for God and neighbour.</span>

    body: |
      Christened Carlo Gaetano at his birth on 15 August 1654, St John Joseph was a native of Ischia, an island near Naples, Italy. Even as a child, he possessed a remarkable religious zeal and love for the poor. At 16 he became the first Italian member of the extremely strict Alcantarine branch of the Franciscans, founded in Spain in 1524 by St Peter of Alcantara. That was when he took the name John Joseph.



      Severely ascetic by nature, John Joseph fasted continuously, never partaking of anything but bread and fruit, and limited his sleep to three hours a night. Having accepted priestly ordination strictly under obedience in 1677, he was made novice master at 24, and later guardian of his monastery. In 1702 he became Provincial of the Alcantarine Reform in Italy.



      St John Joseph was noted for his healing touch, for reading hearts, levitation during his ecstasies, bilocation and for multiplying food during a famine.



      He died on 5 March 1734 at the Friary of Santa Lucia del Monte. Beatified by Pope Pius VI in 1789, he was canonized by Pope Gregory XVI in 1839.
