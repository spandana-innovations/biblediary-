  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Luke 17: 7-10"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Today’s Gospel gives Jesus’ warning on complacency in the spiritual life through a parable about a slave and his relationship with his master. The slave had the duties of a cook as well as those of a fieldworker. Jesus says that the master wanted him to do fieldwork till evening, then prepare the supper and serve him at the table. Since both were parts of his duty, he need not expect a “thank you” from the master. This parable was a warning also to the Pharisees, who thought that God was indebted to them because of their scrupulous observance of the Mosaic Law.</span></span></span>



      The Teaching: We are God’s servants because God owns everything, and all our possessions have been loaned to us by a generous God. Our efforts and accomplishments are nothing in comparison with the blessings of God which we have received. All our devotions, prayers, and works of charity form only a negligible part of our return to God, and the power to do them is another of His gifts to us. We can never adequately repay the gifts God has given to us. The Blessed Virgin Mary was fully aware of this truth, and that is why she responded to God’s messenger saying: *“Behold I am the handmaid of the Lord” *(Lk 1:38). St. Bernadette Soubirous (1844-1879), the girl to whom the Blessed Mother appeared in Lourdes, France lived a life in obscurity as a contemplative nun following the apparitions of our Lady. A journalist was able to track her down and eventually got the permission of the Prioress to interview her. One question that the journalist asked of her was why she opted to live in obscurity when she was so well known all over the world. In answer Bernadette compared herself to a house broom which has been put aside in a closet after it had been used to clean the room.</span></span></span>



      <span class="subhead">Life messages:</span> 1) We need to offer our prayers of thanksgiving and acts of charity to God so that we may grow in God’s grace. 2) We need to consider our service to our neighbours as a sacred duty we owe to God in return for His innumerable blessings. 3) God’s love and generosity should compel us to give Him the best that is in us as our acts of thanksgiving.</span></span></span>

  - key: reflection
    title: Reflection
    ref: null
    audio: null
    body: |
      In today’s Gospel, Jesus describes the relationship between a master and servant. Even when the servant is tired, the master expects him to continue serving, and the servant accepts this without complaint, knowing his role. This example reminds us to approach God, our ultimate Master, with a similar attitude. We are called to be humble, seeing ourselves as unworthy servants who willingly and joyfully fulfil our duties. When we adopt this servant mindset, we become less resentful and more grateful. It’s not the amount of work that matters, but our attitude towards it. A humble and grateful heart lightens our burdens, improves our relationships, and fills us with positivity. By serving with dedication, we honour God and find joy in our tasks.</span></span></span>
  - key: saint
    title: Saint of the Day
    saintName: "Saint Leo I, the Great, Pope Confessor, Doctor of the Church (-461)"
    ref: "Saint Leo I, the Great, Pope Confessor, Doctor of the Church (-461)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      At a critical time in the Church’s history, when the Western Empire was disintegrating and the Eastern Church was profoundly disturbed by dogmatic controversies and speculations, God raised to the throne of Peter in 440 a farsighted and wise Pontiff, Leo I; he was to become one of the three saintly Popes whom we honour with the title of “the Great”, Gregory I in the 6th century and Nicholas I in the 9th being the other two.</span></span></span></span>



      In order to maintain the purity of the Church’s doctrine, Leo took sharp measures against the subtle infiltration of heresies into Italy and Spain, and in the East condemned Monophysitism or Eutychianism. In his famous dogmatic letter “Tome to Flavian”, Archbishop of Constantinople, he clearly set forth the Church’s doctrine that Christ is one person but possesses two distinct natures, the divine and the human; an early example of an infallible Papal pronouncement. At the 4th Ecumenical Council of Chalcedon in 461, some 600 Eastern bishops, assembled under the presidency of the Papal legates, officially acclaimed this great document as the Church’s true teaching thus: “Peter has spoken by Leo.”</span></span></span></span>



      Pope St Leo I looked upon his office as that of Christ himself and strove to become “all things to all”. Combining prayer with labour, he proved himself a true father to his people. That he saw himself chiefly as a teacher is evident from the fact that his teachings remain the greatest hallmark of his pontificate.</span></span></span></span>
