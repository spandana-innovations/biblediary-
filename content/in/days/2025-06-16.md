    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Matthew 5:38–42"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Reflection: 1



      Jesus teaches us to go far beyond avoiding retaliation, to love our enemies and help them. Our response to those who treat us badly should be inspired by love, even when they insult, humiliate or hurt us and do injustice to us. Jesus proposes that we concede more than what our adversaries demand. That places us in a strong position which may help to convert them. Non-violent resistance does not try to destroy the offender but to save him. Jesus has taught us that unless we forgive those who offend us, our heavenly Father will not forgive our offences. It is therefore obligatory for us to give generously to those who ask from us, and to forgive those who hurt us. </span></span></span></span>



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP</span></span>*Reflection: 2



      During their captivity in Egypt, the Jews became familiar with the crude tribal law of retaliation called *Lex Talionis (*=Tit-for-Tat) given by the ancient lawmaker Hammurabi during the period 2285-2242 BC. In the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus rejects even the concession of milder retaliation allowed by Moses. In its place, Jesus gives a new law of love and grace — and no retaliation.



      “An eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth.” Moses instructed the Israelites to follow tit-for-tat retaliation, rather than to wreak total destruction upon their enemies. That is, instead of mutilating or murdering all the members of the offender’s family or tribe, they should discover, then punish by an equal mutilation or harm, only the offender. Later, a milder version of this law was substituted. It demanded monetary compensation, as decided by a judge, in place of physical punishment. Moses also gave the Israelites several laws commanding merciful treatment for the enemy if he also was a Jew (e.g., Lv 19:18).



      The true Christian reaction: For Jesus, retaliation, or even limited vengeance, has no place in the Christian life. Jesus illustrates the Christian approach by giving three examples:



      Turn to him the other cheek*:*Striking someone on the right cheek (with the right hand), requires striking with the back of one’s hand, and, according to Jewish concepts, the blow inflicts more insult than pain. Jesus instructs his followers to forgive the insult gracefully and convert the offender.



      1) *“Let him have your cloak as well.”* Jesus instructs his followers that they should show more responsibility and a greater sense of duty than to fight over possessions.



      2) *Go with him two miles.* A Christian has the duty of responding, even to seemingly unjust demands by helping or serving gracefully not grudgingly*.* </span>

    body: |
      “Do not resist the one who is evil,” is a piece of advice from Jesus that sounds unrealistic. The natural tendency in anyone is to resist, take revenge, retaliate or finish off the offender. Among the warring nations, they practise it by retaliating a hundredfold for every offence by the opponent. However, Jesus showed by his own life, sufferings and death, that it is possible and even imperative to ‘turn the other cheek’ if we are to be children of our heavenly Father. Saint Paul describes how he endured great afflictions, hardships, calamities, imprisonment, etc., with the weapon of righteousness. The natural reaction of a follower of Christ should be to give and forgive, for these are the characteristics of our heavenly Father. What is my reaction towards those who oppose or offend me?

    audio: null
    body: |
      Very early in life, under the gaze of his devout parents, John Francis Regis Jean, a developed a keen interest in study and prayer. He began formal studies at the Jesuit College at Beziers at 14 and joined the Jesuits at 19, was ordained to the priesthood at 33, and proceeded to spend himself in caring for the plague stricken of the city of Toulouse. Thenceforth, until his untimely death at 43, he laboured selflessly and with extraordinary success at missions among the ignorant farming population, and the children of the poor in that section of southern France, corrupted by the religious wars.



      He converted numerous Huguenots, established confraternities of the Blessed Sacrament, and saved wayward women by providing refuge where they could learn an honest trade. In the interest of the poor he broke the stranglehold which a few greedy men had on the grain market and got certain exorbitant taxes which threatened to ruin the local lace-making industry, removed.



      His own life was one of great and continuous mortification as well as of absolute obedience to his superiors, who had him give up some of his most cherished and ambitious charities because they were unable to share his own optimism and utter reliance on Providence. He died in 1640 and was canonized in 1737.



      The mortal remains of St John Francis Regis lie entombed in the Basilica at La Louvesc. It was to a pilgrimage to his shrine that St John Baptist Marie Vianney, the Cure d’Ars, ascribes his vocation and his accomplishments.
