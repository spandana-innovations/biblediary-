    ref: "Mark 12: 28b-34"

    body: |
      Reflection: 1



      A scribe asks Jesus, what the greatest commandment is. Jesus combines two principles of the Law and makes of them one dual commandment. For him, love of God must be expressed in love of human beings. He personifies in himself the commandment of love. He loved God and the people even unto death. The response of Jesus shows that the love of God and the love of neighbour are the greatest principles that guide all our life. To love God with all our heart, soul, mind and strength demands total dedication and firm decision. We are challenged to verify our love for God by our love for neighbour. Do we give in our life the place of primacy to God?



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      A scribe who believed in both the written Law and the oral tradition was pleased to see how Jesus had defeated the Sadducee who had tried to humiliate him with the hypothetical case of a woman who had married and been widowed by seven husbands in succession. Out of admiration, the scribe challenged Jesus to summarize the most important of the Mosaic Laws in one sentence. In the Judaism of Jesus’ day, there was a double tendency: to expand the Mosaic Law into hundreds of rules and regulations and to condense the 613 precepts of the Torah into a single sentence or few sentences.



      Jesus’ novel contribution: Jesus gave a straightforward answer, quoting directly from the Law itself and startling all with his profound simplicity and mastery of the Law of God and its purpose. He combined the first sentence of the Jewish *Shema* prayer from Deuteronomy 6:4-5: *Hear, O Israel, The Lord your God is Lord Alone! Therefore, you shall love the Lord, your God, with all your heart, and with all your soul, and with all your strength”*with its complementary law from Leviticus 19:18: “*You shall love your neighbour as yourself.”* Thus, Jesus proclaims that true religion is to love God both directly and as living in our neighbour. Jesus underlines the principle that we are to love our neighbour as we love ourselves because both of us bear God’s image. For, to honour God’s image is to honour both Him Who made it and Him Whom it resembles. Besides, our neighbours, too, are the children of God our Father, redeemed by the Blood of Jesus. Love for our neighbour is a matter, not of feelings, but of deeds by which we share with others the unmerited love that God lavishes on us. This is the *agape* love for neighbour that God commands in His Law. Jesus then uses the parable of the Good Samaritan, as reported in Luke’s Gospel, to show them what God means by “neighbour.”



      <span class="subhead">Life Messages:</span>1) We need to love God whole-heartedly: Loving God with all our heart, soul, mind, and strength, means that we should place God’s will ahead of our own, seek the Lord’s will in all things, and make it paramount in our lives. It also means that we must find time to adore Him, to present our needs before Him, and to ask His pardon and forgiveness for our sins. 2) God’s will be that we should love everyone, seeing Him in our neighbor. This means we have to help, support, encourage, forgive, and pray for everyone without regard to color, race, gender, age, wealth, social status, intelligence, education, or charm.

      In our journey of faith, we often face the piercing question: ‘Do YOU love God?’ This inquiry isn’t just a fleeting thought; it’s a challenge that demands reflection. Jesus calls us to love God with our ALL – our heart, soul, mind, and strength. This total commitment transcends mere emotion; it embodies dedication and decision. Life may test us with hardships, ridicule, or injustice, but true love persists, despite challenges. When our lives are centred on love for God, we naturally express this love toward our neighbors through concrete actions. As we navigate the ‘train of life,’ let us remember: the quality of our faith is determined by our answer to that profound question. Are we truly loving God in our daily lives?

    audio: null
    body: |
      St Euphrasia was the daughter of Antigonus, a senator of Constantinople (Turkey), who was related to the Emperor Theodosius I. Antigonus having died shortly after the birth of his daughter, Theodosius took the widow and the child under his protection and, as was the custom, arranged a betrothal for Euphrasia at the age of five, to the son of a wealthy senator. But the widow herself began to be sought in marriage, whereupon she withdrew from the court and, with Euphrasia, went to Egypt where they settled down near a convent of nuns.



      At seven, Euphrasia was greatly drawn to the convent life and begged her mother to allow her to stay there. Reluctantly, her mother agreed and the abbess told her that the grace of God was working in Euphrasia.



      Meanwhile she declined her betrothed who by then desired the marriage. Now 12 and an heiress, she wrote to Emperor Arcadius, seeking to be allowed to follow her vocation and requesting him to use her parents’ property for the good of the poor and to enfranchise all her slaves. Arcadius complied with her requests. Euphrasia, for her part, took up hard and humbling tasks to divert her attention from the world, to which she was sometimes tempted to return. She complied cheerfully with whatever was expected of her.



      Years later, as she lay on her death-bed, Julia who shared her cell, besought her to obtain for her the grace of being with her in heaven. The Saint died and three days after, Julia followed suit.



      Russian usage has St Euphrasia named in the preparation of the Byzantine Mass.
