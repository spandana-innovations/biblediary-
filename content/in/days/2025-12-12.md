  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Matthew 11:16–19"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Reflection: 1



      Jesus is saddened by the childish and immature bahaviour of the people of his generation. They did not accept John the Baptist who lived an ascetic and austere life. They did not accept Jesus, the Son of Man, who ate and drank. The contemporaries of John and Jesus found fault with both of them, but God in his wisdom does only what is just. John and Jesus were mature and deep-rooted in their convictions, and did not give way to childishness. We should seek not childishness but mature and simple humility in the presence of God.



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      The message of John the Baptist and the message of Jesus fell on deaf ears and met with stiff resistance from the self-righteous Scribes and the Pharisees because of their jealousy, prejudice, and spiritual blindness. Hence, they attributed the austerities of John the Baptist to the devil and Jesus’ table fellowship with sinners as the behaviour of a glutton and a drunkard, evidence contraindicating any Messianic possibility. In today’s Gospel, Jesus compares these Scribes and Pharisees to irresponsible street-children.



      Dog-in-the-manger attitude: Jesus compares the attitude of the Scribes and the Pharisees to that of street-children who want to entertain themselves by playing wedding and funeral songs. They divide themselves into two groups. But when one group proposes to sing wedding songs and asks the other group to dance, the second group will propose funeral songs and ask the first group to carry one of them on their shoulders as they act out a funeral procession. In the end both groups will be frustrated. Jesus states that the Scribes and Pharisees act exactly like these irresponsible and immature children because of their pride and prejudice. Jesus criticizes the unbelieving Jews for not listening either to John the Baptist, who preached a message of austerity and repentance, or to Jesus preaching the Good News of love, mercy, and salvation.



      <span class="subhead">Life messages:</span>



      1) Jesus’ parable about disappointed playmates challenges us to examine ourselves to see if we are buffet Catholics with selective hearing, so that we hear only what we want to hear. Jesus’ message of the kingdom of God is *Good News* and it produces true joy and spiritual freedom for those who will listen, but it is also a warning for those who refuse to listen and close their minds.



      2) *Hearing*the Gospel implies the total acceptance and assimilation of what we hear and the incorporation of it into our daily lives. Like the generation of Jesus’ time, our age is marked by indifference and contempt, especially in regard to the things of Heaven. Indifference dulls our ears to God’s voice and to the Good News of the Gospel. Only the humble of heart can find joy and favour in God’s grace.

      Child-like innocence holds spiritual importance; spiritual childishness hinders growth. Jesus advocates child-like faith, distinct from immature behaviours. Childishness in spirituality can make people suit teachings for one’s desire, at the cost of the Truth. This is what we come across in the Gospel Passage: the exposition of the childishness of the people! Two persons, totally committed to the cause of the Kingdom are under the scanner today – John the Baptist and Jesus. Both would have been high on the popularity charts if they had compromised on the Kingdom values! But, both did not budge! They prevented spiritual childishness from overpowering them. We are called to be aware of the ‘subtle and sly’ vice of childishness and seek to be child-like in the Presence of God!

    title: Saint of the Day
    saintName: "Saint Jane Frances de Chantal - Religious (1572-1641)"
    ref: "Saint Jane Frances de Chantal - Religious (1572-1641)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Born on 28 January 1572, Jane was barely eighteen months old when she lost her mother, Marguerite de Berbisey. Educated privately at home, she grew up into a comely woman with a keen sense of judgement and a lively temperament under the influence of her father, Bénigne Frémyot, President of the Burgandy Parliament.



      Married at 21 to Baron Christophe de Rabutin-Chantal, she was widowed barely seven years later. Piety and austerity marking her lifestyle, Jane worked diligently at educating her three young daughters and a son. When visiting her father at age 32, she encountered Francis de Sales to whose spiritual care she entrusted herself, vowing not to remarry and to always obey him in all things. She deferred her own plans for entering religious life until, in 1607, he disclosed his plans for an institute that’d be a haven for women who would live the virtues exemplified in Mary’s Visitation.



      On 10 June 1610, having ensured that her children were provided for, Jane, together with two others, accepted the rule of the Order of the Visitation of Our Lady from Father Francis. They pronounced their First Vows a year later. Revising the rule in 1613, the Bishop of Lyons replaced the external works of charity with the “cloister” and in 1618, Paul V made the institute a religious order.



      When she died on 13 December 1641, there were over eighty convents standing testimony to her zeal for Christian perfection. Jane whose name is the feminine version of John, which in Hebrew means “God has mercy”, encountered great sufferings, chiefly the death of Francis in 1622, the loss of her only son in battle in 1627 and torments of the dark night of the soul.



      Buried close to her mentor [at Visitation House, Annecy] who called her “the perfect woman”, Jane was beatified in 1751 and canonized in 1767.



      <span class="subhead">Reflection:</span>“In keeping their lives to do God’s will, martyrs of love experience sufferings that are a thousand times greater than the sufferings they would incur if they died a thousand times to bear witness to their faith, charity, and fidelity” (Saint Jane Frances de Chantal).
