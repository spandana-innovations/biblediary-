      Reflection: 1



      In chapter 6 of John’s Gospel, Jesus repeatedly affirms that he is the bread of life. He says plainly, “The bread that I will give for the life of the world is my flesh.” The “flesh” or “body” of Jesus signifies the whole person of Jesus. In the Old Testament language, “flesh and blood” meant human life. Therefore when Jesus insists on the necessity of eating his flesh and drinking his blood, he is pointing out the need of receiving the risen Jesus through the sacrament of the Eucharist. When we participate in the Eucharist with faith, we encounter the living Christ in person who renews our life. He becomes for us food of eternal life. When we eat this living Bread, Jesus transforms us, uniting us with himself. Are we looking for temporary satisfaction – like manna – or for the Bread of eternal life?



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      Today’s Gospel passage is the continuation of Jesus’ teaching on the Bread of Life. Jesus declares that he has seen God his Father because he has come from Heaven. Jesus also states that we hear God the Father’s Voice through him and through the Holy Spirit because the Father draws us to Jesus through the Holy Spirit.




      Jesus reminds the Jews that they cannot be his disciples unless God his Father draws them to him and teaches them. The *Magisterium* of the Church has repeated this teaching in Vatican II: "Before this Faith can be exercised, man must have the grace of God to move and assist him; he must have the interior help of the Holy Spirit, Who moves the heart and converts it to God, Who opens the eyes of the mind and makes it easy for all to accept and believe the truth" (Vatican II, *Dei Verbum*, 5). Once they become his disciples, Jesus will feed their souls with the Bread from Heaven, and this Heavenly Bread is his own Body, Blood, Soul, and Divinity. Eternal Life is reserved for such disciples. This Eternal Life is a Life of love, fellowship, communion, and union with God.

      Life message: 1) Holy Communion is the wonderful banquet at which Christ gives himself to us: “*The Bread which I shall give for the life of the world is My Flesh.)* Hence, let us receive the glorified Body and Blood of the Risen Lord Jesus in the Holy Eucharist with a repentant heart, proper preparation, reverential fear, and grateful joy.

      Today’s readings remind us that being a disciple of Christ means being drawn into the love of the Father. Jesus tells us that no one can come to Him unless the Father draws them. In the story of the Ethiopian eunuch, we see how the Scriptures reveal God’s love and lead him to faith. Just as the eunuch eagerly accepts the message, we too are invited to embrace God’s love. However, it is not easy to accept His teachings and to experience the depth of His love. It is a choice for us to open our hearts to this love. The psalmist praises God for not rejecting him, recognising the grace of being drawn to God’s presence. Like the eunuch, we are called to respond to God’s love, allowing it to transform our lives and guide us on our journey of faith.

    ref: "Saint Adalbert of Prague - Bishop martyr (956-997)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Christened Vojtech at birth in the princely Slavnik family of Libice in East Bohemia, Adalbert was trained in theology at Magdeburg under its first Archbishop, Saint Adalbert, whose name he eventually took at his “confirmation”.



      The first Czech occupant of the See of Prague, Adalbert was a man of vibrant energy and zeal and, much given to austerity, strove to reform the clergy, suppress abuses of various kinds and eliminate pagan practices, as also to spread Christianity throughout Bohemia and neighbouring Hungary. However, distressed by the superficial attitude evident towards the Faith, Adalbert left Prague in 982 for Rome, where he became a Benedictine monk.



      Persuaded by the Pope he returned to Prague in 992; but disillusioned with the lack of change in the situation, he left Prague again in 995 only to learn of the brutal massacre of the Slavnik family and its supporters that followed. Now refused permission to return to his See, he obtained Papal release from his episcopal obligations and dedicated himself entirely to missionary activity, becoming a close friend and trusted adviser of the Roman Emperor, Otto III, on matters concerning the progressive Christianization of the Slavs.



      True to the meaning of his baptismal name, Adalbert was to truly prove “a consolation to the army” of Jesus Christ. Invited by the Polish king, Boleslas I the Great, he set about organizing missionary work among the heathen Prussians on the Baltic coast where he was martyred in 997. His body, ransomed by Boleslas, was buried at Gniezno in Poland and later transferred to Prague in 1039.
