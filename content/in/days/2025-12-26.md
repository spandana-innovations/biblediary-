      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      When, centuries before, the priest Zachariah was put to death by stoning, his last words were a shouted curse, “may the Lord see this and avenge me!” (2 Chron 24:22) Contrast this with the dying words of Saint Stephen: “Lord, do not charge them with this sin.” The Old Testament “Eye for an eye” we can readily understand, since crimes of violence and injustice cry out to heaven for vengeance. In light of the injustice done to him, we can only be amazed by Stephen’s final words, praying to God to pardon his killers. His crime was to have spoken some hard truths that his audience did not want to hear. As a result, the frenzied mob put him to death by stoning. Under a rain of rocks that crushed his bones, Stephen commended his spirit to Jesus, and with his dying breath prayed for his killers.



      How do we get from Zachariah’s “*May God punish them*!” to Stephen’s “*Lord forgive them*” ? What or rather who has made the difference? The one who speaks in today’s gospel, Jesus himself. He had foretold that those sent out to spread his message would be rejected and chased from one town to another and that some would be killed. One would expect his next words to be words of woe to the killers. Instead, they are words of divine mercy: “Jerusalem, Jerusalem, the city that kills the prophets and stones those who are sent to it! How often would I have gathered your children together as a hen gathers her chicks under her wings.”



      Father, forgive them. Jesus himself made this prayer from the cross; he knew what it was to suffer unjustly, to be betrayed, abandoned, mocked, scourged, and nailed to the cross. His blood did not cry out to heaven for vengeance. Instead, it cried out to God for mercy. Unconquered by hatred, Jesus conquered hatred with love — and so did Stephen, the first of many Christian martyrs, who echoed those saving words: Father, forgive. Yesterday we celebrated the joyful birth of a child. Today we honour the martyrdom of an innocent man. In some ways, the birth of Jesus led to the death of Stephen. Stephen was martyred for witnessing to Jesus, for publicly calling him the glorious Son of Man, now standing at the right hand of God. Luke describes Stephen dying with two prayers on his lips, first, a prayer of surrender, “Lord, Jesus, receive my spirit,” and then a prayer of petition for his executioners, “Lord, do not hold this sin against them.”



      In his Gospel, Luke had described Jesus himself as dying with two similar prayers on his lips, a prayer of surrender, “Father, into your hands I commend my spirit” and a prayer for his executioners, “Father, forgive them for they know not what they are doing.” Whereas Jesus prays to the Father, Stephen prays to the risen Lord Jesus. Mary’s child is now risen Lord and can be prayed to as we would pray to God. In the church we often pray to the Father through Jesus, but we are also invited to pray directly to Jesus. Stephen died as Jesus died “filled with the Holy Spirit.” We have been given the gift of the same Holy Spirit, who empowers us to live like Jesus and die like Jesus. On this feast of Saint Stephen, we pray for a fresh outpouring of that Spirit into our lives.

      The day after Christmas sees a shift from joyous celebration to solemn remembrance. Yesterday we hailed the birth of our eternal King; today we commemorate the triumphant suffering of His loyal soldier. Why does the Church follow this tradition? It is not to diminish the joy of Christmas but to underscore its essence: embracing discipleship in Christ by walking the path of the Cross. Christmas signifies life given up, to regain life in fullness. When Jesus is born in our hearts, transformation happens! St Stephen lived true Christmas – a life where Christ’s birth led to a bold witness. Does the celebration of Christmas fuel our commitment to be true disciples? St Stephen’s blood sings the carols of Jesus born within him. Just as Jesus applauded St



      Stephen’s faith, may we too become dynamic soldiers for His Kingdom, echoing the passion and courage of the new-born King.

    audio: null
    body: |
      Within a year of Jesus’ death and resurrection the young Church at Jerusalem already numbered over 5000, and the Apostles felt the need to delegate some of their duties—like preaching, baptizing, and the fair distribution of alms—to ‘deacons’ (helpers). The first of the seven to be ordained for this purpose was Stephen, “a man full of faith and the Holy Spirit”.



      Stephen, “full of grace and fortitude”, performed “great wonders and signs” in the name of Jesus. A pupil of the great Gamaliel, he preached fearlessly in the “synagogue of the Freedmen” (descendants of Jews who had been carried off to Rome as children in 63 B.C. and there freed), and in the Synagogues of the Hellenist Jews from Cyrene, Alexandria, Cilicia (Beirut) and Asia, who preferred to worship by themselves. He insisted that Jesus came to save all and not merely the “Chosen People”, and that the Church was separate and distinct from the synagogue, reproving the Pharisees for their obstinacy that prevented them from recognizing the long awaited Messiah and, instead, crucifying him.



      Accused of blasphemy before the Sanhedrin, his fiery and eloquent defence of the Church, his enumeration of God’s past mercies toward Israel and the latter’s ingratitude, and finally his declaration that he saw “the heavens opened and the Son of Man standing at the right hand of God”, so raised their fury that they dragged him outside the city and stoned him to death. All through the fracas, Stephen’s thoughts, as a true follower of Jesus, were for his persecutors—“Lord, lay not this sin to their charge.” He earned himself the distinction of becoming the first martyr of the fledgling Church, exclaiming, “Lord Jesus, receive my spirit!” thus living up to the meaning of his name which, in Greek, signifies “crown”!
