  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "John 1:35–42"
    audio: null
    body: |
      In Mathew’s Gospel, Jesus called the fishermen Andrew and his brother Simon from their fishing boat. But John the Evangelist gives a slightly different story. According to him, Andrew and he (John, son of Zebedee) were disciples of John the Baptist. John the Baptist wanted them to join the true Messiah, Jesus, as His disciples. So, one day when Andrew and John (according to tradition) were standing with their master, John the Baptist, Jesus happened to pass in front of them. John the Baptist promptly introduced Jesus to them as the Lamb of God. It was natural for Andrew and John to guess what their master, John the Baptist, wanted them to do. So, they followed Jesus. Since Sabbath rest was about to begin when travel was forbidden, Jesus cordially invited them to come and stay with Him and learn more about his life and mission till the Sabbath was over.



      When the Sabbath rest with Jesus was over, Andrew and John went home. Andrew was so fascinated with Jesus and his contact with him the previous day that he promptly told his brother Simon about Jesus: “We have found the Messiah.” Without wasting time Andrew brought his brother to Jesus. Jesus surprised Simon by calling him by his name, Simon, and changing that Hebrew name to the Greek name, Cephas (Peter),meaning rock, and accepting him as His disciple.



      Life message: We need to be missionaries like Andrew. Just as a day’s contact with Jesus transformed Andrew into a missionary, leading his brother to Jesus, we are expected to experience Jesus in our lives by Bible reading, personal prayers and sacramental life and acts of charity. Once we experience Jesus personally, we too must start leading others to the same experience of Jesus as their Lord and Savior, enabling them to surrender their lives to Jesus, too.



      (Fr Tony: https://frtonyshomilies.com/)
  - key: reflection
    title: Reflection
    ref: null
    audio: null
    body: |
      The distinctive mark of the children of God is that they do what is right by loving their brothers and sisters and do not fall into sin. The coming of the Son of God was to destroy the works of the devil and to keep the children of God righteous. The Gospel narrative focuses on the call of the first disciples. A second time John the Baptist declares that Jesus is the Lamb of God pointing to the sacrificial aspect of Jesus’ passion and death in order to eradicate sin from the world. Andrew recognizes Jesus as the Messiah and shares the revelation to his brother Simon who is renamed by Jesus as Cephas or Peter. It is the responsibility of every Christian to share the revelatory experience of the person of Jesus as the Saviour with our brothers and sisters.

    title: Saint of the Day
    saintName: "Saint Elizabeth Ann Bayley Seton - Religious (1774-1821)"
    ref: "Saint Elizabeth Ann Bayley Seton - Religious (1774-1821)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Born of distinguished Protestant parents of New York City [28 August 1774], Elizabeth Ann Bayley Seton lost her mother when she was three and was brought up by her father with a sound education and impeccable moral training. Since her childhood, she nursed such a special concern for the poor and the sick and ministered to them so tenderly that she was called “Protestant Sister of Charity”. Her non-Episcopalian practices included wearing a crucifix, daily general examen of conscience, and reading The Imitation of Christ along with the Bible.

      Her husband, William Magee Seton, a young wealthy merchant, died in the 9th year of marriage, leaving her a young widow with five children. During her first month as widow in the Catholic Filicchi household, Elizabeth came to know and appreciate the Catholic Faith, the family’s special devotion to the Blessed Virgin rubbing off well on her. Her reception into the Catholic Church [1805] resulted in her ostracization by her Protestant relatives and friends.

      Proceeding to Canada, in response to a request from the Superior of the Baltimore Sulpicians, Father Dubourg, she founded a school for girls which her students solught to convert into a convent, whereupon the Bishop of Baltimore gave her a set of rules to be observed by them with her as Superior.

      In Emmitsburg (Maryland), she started the Sisters of Charity [1809], under the rule of Saint Vincent de Paul. Laying the foundations for the American Parochial school system, she trained teachers, prepared textbooks and wrote many spiritual treatises. She helped the sick and the poor, led many to the Faith, and opened orphanages in Philadelphia and New York.

      Following her death [1821], she was canonized in 1975 by Blessed Paul VI who officially designated the day as the Holy Year “Women’s Day” because “she was a woman who has passed all stages of womanhood: youth, maidenhood, widowhood and religious life.”

      <span class="subhead">Reflection:</span>*“Her (Elizabeth’s) distinguishing characteristic was compassion and indulgence for poor sinners. Her charity made her watchful never to speak evil of others, always to find excuses or to keep silence”*(Antonio Filicchi).
