    body: |
      Reflection: 1



      Most people take for granted various kinds of help and support those who live around and about them offer them, and do not even think of thanking those benefactors. Of the ten lepers cured by Jesus, only one, a non-Jew, returned to give him thanks. He put his faith in the person and presence of Jesus and began a new relationship with him. Many of those who received different favours directly from Jesus in his days did not arrive at the saving faith and a personal relationship with him. How thankful are we to God for the numerous favours He grants us day by day? Do we use these favours as the door to a deeper faith in God and His Son Jesus?



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      Jesus was on the border between Galilee and Samaria when he was met by a band of ten lepers, both Jews and Samaritans. By describing Jesus’ miraculous healing of the ten lepers from a physically devastating and socially isolating disease, today’s Gospel presents a God Who desires only gratitude from us for the many blessings we have received from Him and Who feels pain at our ingratitude. The Gospel story tells of a single leper (a “Samaritan heretic”), who returned to thank Jesus for healing him, while the others went their way, the Jews perhaps under the false impression that healing was their right as God’s chosen people. They did not seem to feel indebted to Jesus for the singular favour they had received. Instead, they hurried off to obtain a health certificate from the priests. “*Where are the other nine*?” Jesus asked the Samaritan leper. *“Did only one come back to say ‘thank-you?’”*Today’s reading also presents Faith and healing going hand in hand, as do Faith and reconciliation.



      <span class="subhead">Life messages:</span>



      1) We need to learn to be thankful to God and to others. Often, we are ungrateful to God. Although we receive so much from Him, we often take it all for granted without appreciating His gifts. Often, we are ungrateful to our parents and consider them a nuisance, although in the past we were dependent on them for literally everything. Similarly, we owe a great debt of gratitude to our friends, teachers, doctors and pastors — but we often fail to thank them. Hence, in the future, let us be filled with daily thanksgiving to God and to others for the countless gifts we have received. Let us pray: “Please, God, heal my heart of ingratitude.”



      2) We need to celebrate the Holy Eucharist as the supreme act of thanksgiving. When we celebrate Holy Mass together, we are thanking God for the great gift of His Son, whose sacrifice formed us into the People of God*.*We thank God for the gift of the Spirit, through whom we bring the presence of the Lord to others.



      3) We all need healing from our spiritual leprosy. Although we may not suffer from physical leprosy, when we suffer from the “spiritual leprosy” of sins, Jesus, our Savior, wants to heal us through the Sacrament of Reconciliation.

      Gratitude bridges the gap between God and our hearts, transforming our lives into miracles! In today’s Gospel, Jesus singles out and affirms the sole individual who returned to express gratitude upon realizing he had been cured of his leprosy. Jesus poses a poignant question: “Were not all ten cleansed? Where are the other nine?” Pope Francis urges us to maintain a vocabulary of “thank you” and “sorry” in a world often marked by distrust. We too often take for granted the contributions of those around us, even our own families and communities. Gratitude possesses the potential to heal our ailments, soothe our emotional wounds, repair fractured relationships, and infuse our existence with the essence of joy. It is essential to ask: How often do I genuinely say “Thank you”?

    saintName: "Saint Josaphat Kunsevich, Bishop Martyr"
    ref: "Saint Josaphat Kunsevich, Bishop Martyr"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Saint Josaphat was born in Lithuania at a time when the Ruthenian Church, having separated from Rome, had fallen on evil days. Named John Kunseyvc at birth, he was a lad of 15 when the promising “Union of Brest-Litowski” withdrew some ten million Christians from allegiance to the Orthodox Patriarch of Constantinople and reunited them to Rome, a step which was to a large extent to become void by persecution in Russia and Poland.



      Ordained priest in 1609, Josaphat proved to be a great influence for good in the reform of his Order and the regeneration of the clergy. He rose rapidly to be Abbot, Coadjutor and finally, at 38, Archbishop of Polozk, which was one of the Sees where rivalry between the Catholic and Orthodox Clergy was in sharp evidence.



      His personal life was one of austerity and mortification. In the constant strife between the Uniates and Schismatics, he showed indefatigable zeal for converting souls to union with Rome. He entreated, preached, heard confessions, not only in the churches but in the hospitals, prisons and in the fields. As a result, personages of importance got converted by this “Thief of Souls”! Indeed, so fruitful were Josaphat’s endeavours that the hatred of the schismatics reached the point where, in 1623, they finally martyred him and threw his mangled body into a river. Consequently, the conversions marked a steady rise, not excluding his murderers!



      Canonized in 1867, Josaphat became the first Easterner to have been formally raised to the honours of the altar by Rome.
