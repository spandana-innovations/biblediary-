    body: |
      Reflection: 1






      The scribes and the Pharisees used to keep commandments of the Law according to the letter, not according to the spirit. Jesus teaches that not only murder is a punishable sin, but being angry with one’s brother and saying to him, You fool!” are also serious and punishable sins. Jesus came to fulfil the Law far beyond its literal sense. The letter kills, the spirit gives life. Jesus teaches the necessity of love and respect for one another and of living in good relationship with all.



      Before we appear in front of the judge, we should amicably settle the issue and make peace with our opponent. Once the judgement is passed and we are sent to punishment, there is no more opportunity to seek reconciliation; we will have to suffer the full punishment. Being reconciled with one’s enemy takes priority over offering gifts at the altar. Only those who make peace will be called children of God, as Jesus teaches in the beatitudes. The disciples of Jesus should practise righteousness not merely according to the prescription of the Law but according to the demands of love of God and neighbour.



       *— Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      For the Scribes and the Pharisees, the external fulfilment of the precepts of the Mosaic Law was the guarantee of a person’s salvation. In other words, a man saved himself through the external works of the Law. Jesus rejects this view in today’s Gospel passage, taken from the Sermon on the Mount. For Jesus, justification or sanctification is a grace, a free, strengthening gift from God. Man’s role is one of cooperating with that grace by being faithful to it, and using it as God means it to be used. Jesus then outlines new moral standards for his disciples.



      Control of anger: Anger is the rawest, strongest, and most destructive of human emotions. Describing three stages of anger and the punishment each deserves; Jesus advises his disciples not to get angry in such a way that they sin.



      1) Anger in the heart (“brief stage of insanity” Cicero): It has two forms: a) a sudden, blazing flame of anger which dies suddenly. b) a surge of anger which boils inside and lingers, so that the heart seeks revenge and refuses to forgive or forget. Jesus prescribes trial and punishment by the Village Court of Elders as its punishment.



      2) Anger in speech: The use of words which are insulting (“*raka*“=“fool”), or damaging to the reputation (“*moros*” = a person of loose morals). Jesus says that such an angry (verbally abusive) person should be sent to the Sanhedrin, the Jewish religion’s Supreme Court, for trial and punishment.



      3) Anger in action: Sudden outbursts of uncontrollable anger, which often result in physical assault or abuse. Jesus says that such anger deserves hellfire as its punishment. In short, Jesus teaches that long-lasting anger is bad, contemptuous speech or destroying someone’s reputation is worse and harming another physically is the worst.



      Life messages: 1) Let us try to forgive, forget, and move toward reconciliation as soon as possible. St. Paul advises us “*Be angry* (righteous anger), *but do not sin”* (Eph 4:26). 2) When we keep anger in our mind, we are inviting physical illnesses like hypertension, and mental illnesses like depression. 3) Let us relax and keep silence when we are angry and pray for God’s strength for self-control, and for the grace, first to desire to forgive, and then actually to go and forgive those who have injured us.

    body: |
      The Scribes and the Pharisees used to speak about the 613 commandments of the Old Testament. Among them, 365 commandments were positive commandments while 248 were negative commandments. Jesus summed up all the commandments into just two – Love of God and love of neighbour. Seemingly, the demands of Jesus are simpler. But this is not the truth. Jesus came to fulfil the Law and the Prophets. While the Old Testament considered murder as sinful, Jesus considered anger as equally sinful. While the Old Testament considered adultery to be sinful, Jesus considers looking at a woman lustfully as equally sinful. According to Jesus being reconciled with others is the sine-qua-non (an essential condition) for offering gifts at the altar. Jesus advocates a clean heart and pure intentions.

    body: |
      Daughter of Count Dietrich of Westphalia and Reinhild of Denmark, St Matilda was brought up in the Benedictine convent at Herford (central Germany), where her grandmother was Abbess. Here she became known for her rare talents and virtues. At 14 she married Henry the Fowler of Saxony who later was chosen to succeed Conrad as King of Germany. As Queen she proved herself a model of piety and prudence, and was ever intent on succouring the oppressed and the poor.



      Left a widow in 936, Matilda redoubled her prayers, mortifications and almsgiving, and founded numerous hospitals, churches and Benedictine abbeys. But this liberality so exasperated two of her sons, Otto and Henry, that they banished her from court and deprived her of all her property until misfortune overtook them in turn and they became reconciled with her and begged for forgiveness.



      Of her children, the eldest son became Otto the Great, Emperor of Germany; another son was St Bruno, Archbishop of Cologne; a daughter Gerberga married Louis IV of France, and Hedwig became the mother of Hugh Capet, the founder of the Capetian dynasty.



      She died on 14 March 968 at the monastery of Quedlinburg and was buried there with Henry.
