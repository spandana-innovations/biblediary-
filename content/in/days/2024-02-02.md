    body: |
      Mary and Joseph bring baby Jesus to the Temple of Jerusalem, present him and offer him to the Lord God, as prescribed by the Law. Mary’s days of purification were already completed, and now the mother was required to offer a holocaust to God. If she was poor, as in the case of Mary, it was enough that she offered two turtle-doves or two pigeons. Thus the first-born child was sanctified, that is, consecrated and offered to Yahweh in memory of the great Exodus from Egypt, and redeemed by payment of five shekels.



      Simeon welcomes the Baby and explodes in a hymn of praise and blessing revealing that the child is sent by God as the redeemer; he is light and salvation to the Gentiles and glory to the people of Israel.



      Today we remember the persons and institutions that offer themselves to God and consecrate themselves to him. The Church and the society benefit immensely from the silent and sustained work of thousands of religious Priests, Brothers and Sisters all over the world in various fields like education, care of the sick, care of the orphans and of the aged, and carry out other forms of social service. Like Jesus and Mary, they consecrate themselves to God and participate in the mission of the Saviour. We thank and praise the Lord for them.



      These consecrated persons are engaged in different ways in the work of evangelisation. Their life of chastity, poverty and obedience are eschatological signs pointing to the life to come and the kingdom of heaven. They are in the world, but not of the world. Those who have given up everything for the sake of Jesus will have, as the Lord himself promised, hundred times as much in this world and eternal happiness in the next.



      Today we pray for all the consecrated persons that they may be docile instruments in the hands of God and remain faithful to Him till the end.



      (Excerpts from: Fr Dr George Kaitholil, Feast Day Homilies, ST PAULS, Mumbai, India)

      Simeon, who had yearned to see the Messiah, had spent years waiting. He finally recognized the Messiah when Jesus was brought by his parents to be presented in the Temple. Simeon did not waver in his faith and trust in God’s promise which was made for the salvation of the people of Israel. Simeon knew that the prophecy of the Prophet Malachi was now being fulfilled before his eyes and he was overjoyed that he had encountered the promised Saviour in his lifetime. Simeon referred to Jesus as “a light for revelation to the Gentiles and for the glory of Israel.” Like Simeon do we wait upon the Lord patiently and do we possess a heart like him that desires the salvation of all God’s people? Lord Jesus, give me a heart like that of Simeon, a heart that loved you and longed to meet you.

      (1) Presentation of the Lord

      Under the Mosaic Law, a woman was ritually “unclean” for 40 days after childbirth, when she was to present herself to the priests at the temple and offer sacrifice in purification of her uncleanness and redeem her firstborn son. Furthermore, contact with anyone who had brushed against mystery — birth or death — automatically excluded a person from Jewish worship. Obviously, Jesus being the Son of God Almighty, this law was not binding on Mary. Yet, it was in obedient conformity with the law that Mary humbly submitted to the ritual, offering, in keeping with her economic status, two turtledoves, i.e., two young pigeons, the sacrifice of the poor.

      Incidentally, this ancient Jewish ritual has no parallel in the Christian Faith today. The Catholic Church firmly holds that no taint whatsoever calling for purification of any kind is incurred by a mother at childbirth.

      The feast of the Presentation of the Lord is very old, its earliest recorded mention dating back to the first half of the fourth century. It was introduced into the whole of the Eastern Empire, by Justinian I in the year 526 A.D., in gratitude for the cessation of the great plague at Constantinople. Celebrated 40 days after Christmas, this feast came to be called “Candlemas” when, in the 8th century, Pope Sergius introduced the solemn procession of clergy and laity carrying blessed lighted candles into the church as a tacit reminder of the “Light to the Gentiles” and “the entry of the Son of God into the Temple”, a title the feast bears in the Armenian Church.

      The facets of Christian faith and practice that this feast strives to highlight for our edification are: the silent yet tender love of the protective Joseph, the strong and constant faith of old Simeon, the unstintingly prayerful fidelity of the aged Anna, and above all, the complete and absolute availability of Mary, the protagonist together with her son in this mystery of salvation.

      <span class="subhead">Reflection:</span>*“Our lighted candle is a sign of the divine splendour of the one who comes to expel the dark shadows of evil and to make the whole universe radiant with the brilliance of his eternal light. Our candle also shows how bright our soul should be when we go to meet Christ”* (St Sophronius).





      (2) St Adalbald of Ostrevant, Nobleman, Martyr (d. 652)

      St Adalbald was a nobleman who faced death at the hands of some of the most vicious “in-laws” ever recorded. Coming from a saintly family, the holy life he led was a source of edification to many and also brought him much veneration following his death.

      Incidentally, sanctity ran deep in Adalbald’s family. Born in Flanders, he was the grandson of St Gertrude of Hamage, husband of St Rictrude and brother-in-law of St Bertha. The four children born to Adalbald and Rictrude – Adalsind, Clotsind, Eusebia and Mauront – all went on to become saints. Adalbald himself, when at the court of King Dagobert I of France (r. 629-639), was an embodiment of all that was both Christian and noble.

      Adalbald and his wife — a daughter of a nobleman named Enold—whom he had met and fallen in love with on one of his military expeditions to Gascony, were renowned for their works of charity.

      <span class="subhead">Reflection:</span>*“We are created for heaven and the Lord has given us all the means to attain it. Now what is required is that we make use of them and profit by them. And we hope that we may have sufficient goodwill to avail ourselves of the means and of the grace the Lord offers us”*(Blessed James Alberione).
