  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Mathew 18: 21 - 19: 1"
    audio: null
    body: |
      The lessons taught by the parable</span></span>: (1) We must forgive so that we may be forgiven. Jesus explains this truth after teaching the prayer, “Our Father.” He warns us, “*For if you forgive men their trespasses, your Heavenly Father also will forgive you; but if you do not forgive men their trespasses, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses”* (Mt 6:14-15). As James states it later, *“For judgment is without mercy to the one who has shown no mercy”* (Jas 2:13). Clearly, Divine and human forgiveness work together.</span></span></span></span></span>



      (2) We represent the greater debtor in the parable; that is, we owe God the ten thousand talents of the parable. We commit sins every day and, hence, we need God’s forgiveness every day. The sum total of all the offenses which our brothers and sisters commit against us is equivalent to the small debt of the second debtor in the parable, namely 100 denarii. Yet, shockingly and sadly, we are merciless towards our fellow human beings. The moral of Jesus’ story is that, as members of a community, we must treat one another as God has treated each of us. Here is a Divine call to throw away the calculator when it comes to forgiveness. We must choose the more honourable path and forgive one another “from the heart.” We have been forgiven a debt beyond all human paying – the sin of man which God forgave through the willing, sacrificial death of His own Son, Incarnate in human flesh. Since that is so, we must forgive others as God has forgiven us. Otherwise, we cannot hope to receive any mercy ourselves.</span></span></span></span></span>



      <span class="subhead">Life messages</span></span></span>: 1) We need to forgive: Having experienced forgiveness at the hands of God and God’s people, we are then called to make it possible for others to experience the same forgiveness. Let us forgive the person who has wronged us before hatred eats away at our ability to forgive.</span></span></span></span></span>



      2) Forgiveness will not be easy, but God is there to help us. We can call on God’s help by offering that individual to God, not by sitting in judgment, but simply by saying, “Help so-and-so and mend our relationship.” We may never forget the hurt we have experienced, but we can choose to forgive. 3) We need to remind ourselves that with God’s grace we have already forgiven the one that hurt us. As life goes on, we may remember the incident or occasion that was hurtful. Then let us offer the offender to God’s mercy and pray for God’s blessings on him or her.</span></span></span></span></span>

    body: |
      Jesus emphasises the call for unending forgiveness, challenging His disciples to move beyond the instinct for revenge and embrace mercy without limits. Peter’s question about the limits of forgiveness prompts Jesus to reveal a profound truth: forgiveness should be boundless, reflecting God’s own merciful heart. Just as we receive God’s abundant forgiveness, we are invited to extend that same grace to others, especially those who wrong us repeatedly. This passage reminds us that forgiving others isn’t merely a duty but a pathway to truly receiving God’s forgiveness ourselves. When we forgive genuinely from the heart, God’s mercy flows through us, transforming not only our own lives but also our relationships. By choosing forgiveness over retaliation, we become active participants in God’s love, allowing His grace to work within us and in the world around us.</span></span></span>

    saintName: "Sts Hippolytus and Pontianus"
    ref: "Sts Hippolytus and Pontianus"
    audio: null
    body: |
      St Hippolytus, whose name means “a horse turned loose”, was a learned priest in Rome and the most important theological writer of his time. He claimed to have been a disciple of St Irenaeus, while St Jerome refers to him as “a most holy and eloquent man”. </span></span></span></span>



      At first, however, he was known for his “holier-than-thou” attitude towards the Church, unafraid of censuring even the Pope. He criticized Pope St Zephyrinus for not being prompt in detecting and denouncing heresy. Attacking the contemporary Trinitarian Modalism (an assertion that the three Persons of the Trinity are merely modes, or self-manifestations of one and the same God) and Patripassianism (a theory that God the Father himself suffered on the cross) he propounded a doctrine on the Logos that was close to subordinationism (viz., the Son and the Spirit merely proceed from the Father and are therefore subordinate to him). </span></span></span></span>



      In 235, Hippolytus was banished to Sardinia by Emperor Maximus. Here, sharing his exile was none other than Pope Pontianus himself, who, during his reign (230-235), had held a synod which confirmed the excommunication of Origen in Alexandria. </span></span></span></span>



      The election of St Anterus ended the schism but his near instant martyrdom was followed by the election of Pope St Fabian (r.236-250) who brought the mortal remains of Hippolytus and Pontianus to Rome and accorded them a solemn burial — to Hippolytus in the cemetery of the Via Tiburtina and Pontianus in the crypt of St Callistus. </span></span></span></span>

She does not reject discovery, knowledge, the natural and divine sciences, but rather examines, adopts and uplifts these so that they can make a precious contribution to her teaching” </span></span>*(Blessed James Alberione).</span></span></span></span>
