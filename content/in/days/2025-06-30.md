      Reflection: 1



      A teacher of the Law, impressed by the miracles Jesus worked, said to him, “ Master, I will follow you wherever you go.” Jesus cautioned him that to follow him and be his disciple was a costly affair; it demanded sacrifice. Jesus did not even have a home of his own. When a disciple said to him, “Lord, let me go and bury my father first,” Jesus simply told him to follow him, and not to worry about the dead. This sounds strange to us. But the disciple had not told Jesus that his father was dead and he had to bury him. He was telling Jesus, in the oriental way of saying, that after his father’s death he would follow Jesus; when that would happen he did not know. Jesus does not publicise his leadership, or promise comfort and prosperity to his would be followers. They should not look back at what they are leaving behind, but follow him immediately and wholeheartedly, and should not give greater importance or urgency to family relationships. </span></span></span></span>



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP</span></span>*Reflection: 2



      Today’s Gospel passage explains the cost of Christian discipleship and the total commitment, wholehearted constancy, and sacrificial ministry that the Christian mission requires.



      It was quite unexpected for a learned scribe to volunteer to become Jesus’ disciple. But Jesus offered him no false promises, telling him, “*Foxes have holes, and birds of the air have nests, but the Son of Man has not whereon to lay his head.”*Jesus was simply being honest about the demands and the cost of a commitment the scribe might make too lightly and an arduous journey he might be undertaking too easily. Being a Christian is not an easy or comfortable affair. It calls for a lot of self-control and self-denial, putting God before everything else. Jesus’ response to another would-be disciple who asked for more time before becoming a disciple sounds harsh: “*Let the dead bury their dead.*” But this man’s father was not dead or sick. The man had simply asked to stay with his father until the father’s death. Jesus knew that later he would find another reason to delay answering the call.



      <span class="subhead">Life messages: </span>



      1) We need to honour our commitments: Today, more than ever, people make marriage commitments too easily and then break them. The problem today is that the couples do not have the courage to *make* the commitment of marriage. We all know there is a tremendous shortage of priests and religious. Our young people are unwilling to make commitments to God by committing themselves to life-long celibacy, obedience to a Bishop or religious superior or to the vowed life of a religious community.



      2) We need to pray for strength to honour our commitments. We are here this morning because, in one way or another, we have said to Jesus, “I will follow You.” Sometimes we have been faithful to Jesus, and other times we have not. Hence, we need to pray for strength to honour our commitments, we need to ask for forgiveness when we fail, and we need to renew our determination to walk with Jesus by being loyal to our spouse and family, earning our living honestly, and living not only peacefully, but lovingly, with our neighbours.

    title: Reflection
    ref: null

    body: |
      Today’s gospel explains the cost of discipleship. Jesus does not advertise his brand of leadership by performing populist gimmicks or promising wealth and prosperity to those who wish to follow him. In human terms, following Jesus and his ideals in life would be equal to taking a huge risk. He is an itinerant preacher with no place to lay his head. Those who choose to follow him should expect no worldly comforts and personal gains. But the one who takes the risk of following him should never look back at what has been left behind. Following him should be immediate, total and radical. His followers should not be in two minds and two loyalties. No material object or personal relationship should outweigh one’s discipleship of the Lord. But such a person will be blessed by God with hundredfold joy in this world and eternal life in the next. Am I willing to follow Christ, who is poor, shunned and rejected?

    body: |
      In the early years of the Church, persecutions kept breaking out like rash across the vast Roman Empire. But given the present situation, we hardly give those terrible persecutions so much as a thought. One of the first major persecutions began under Nero in the year 64 A.D. and was followed by one under Domitian in 95 A.D. In 107 A.D., Trajan tested the faith, hope and love of the first Christians with another terrible ordeal by fire, prison and the sword. In 135 A.D., Hadrian followed his example and Marcus Aurelius followed suit in 180 A.D. In 222 A.D., Septimus Severus did his best to shake the constancy of the early Christians, followed by Decius in 250 A.D. and Valerian in 257 A.D., the last attempt being that of Diocletian in 303 A.D.



      The Church today offersus this Commemoration of the First Martyrs to help us always bear in mind that the blood of martyrs is first and foremost the seed of faith; secondly, to have us prove, as they did, that our faith is a living, vibrant reality; and thirdly, consider the possibility of someday being called upon to bear witness as they did—to our faith and our hope, to our love for God and for our fellowmen, as so many of our contemporaries here in India itself and elsewhere have done.



      <span class="subhead">Reflection:</span>*“To die for the truth is not to die merely for one’s faith, or one’s country, it is to die for the world. The blood of martyrs is shed in confirmation of the noblest claim — the claim to feed upon immortal truth, to walk with God, and be divinely free”*(Cowper).
