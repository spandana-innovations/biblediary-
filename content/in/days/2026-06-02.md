  - key: homily
    title: Homily Tip
    ref: "Mark 12:13–17"
    audio: null
    body: |
      Today’s Gospel describes how Jesus ingeniously escaped from a trap set for him by the Pharisees. The tax issue: The Jews were forced to pay three separate taxes to the Roman emperor: the ground tax, the income tax, and the census tax. Here, the question concerned the census tax. If Jesus said that it was unlawful to pay the tax, the pro-Rome Herodian’s and their allies would report him to the Roman officials who would then arrest him as a revolutionary. If Jesus said that it was lawful to pay the tax, the insurgents and their supporters would turn against him, and he would be discredited in the eyes of the people who were against paying taxes to a pagan emperor on religious grounds.</span></span></span>



      The defence goes on the offensive. Jesus defeated their scheme by asking his challengers to show him “the coin of tribute” – the coin they would give to the tax-gatherer. Rather than answering their question directly, Jesus asked them a question, thus turning their trap inside out and upside down: “*Whose image* [*eikon *in Greek]*and inscription are these?”* “Caesar’s,” they said. Jesus then said, *“Give back to Caesar what belongs to Caesar — and to God what belongs to God.”* In other words, we give to the emperor the coin because his image is on it, and we give to God our own selves because we are created in the image of God (Gn 1:26). By this answer Jesus reminds his questioners that if they are so concerned and careful about paying taxes to the state, they should be much more concerned and careful about their direct service of, and obligations to, God, their Creator and Lord.</span></span></span>



      <span class="subhead">Life messages: </span>The episode teaches us the nature of our obligations to God and to our country, and it shows us how, with God’s help, we can be ideal citizens of both earth and Heaven. 1) Since everything is God’s, we must give ourselves to Him 100%, not just 10% on Sundays. 2) We should be generous in fulfilling our Sunday obligations and find time every day for prayer and worship in the family, for the reading of the Bible and the proper training of our children in Faith and morals. 3) As citizens of a country, it is the duty of Christians to pay for the services and the privileges that government provides, like paved roads, police and fire departments, banks, and other necessities. 4) Another way of giving to Caesar what is Caesar’s is to participate actively in the running of the government, electing the most suitable candidates and influencing them through frequent contacts. </span></span></span>

    title: Reflection
    ref: null
    audio: null
    body: |
      Justice is defined as giving each person his or her due. In the Gospel, Jesus responds to the question of the Pharisees and Herodians with an additional reference to God. In the Bible, God is presented as the Supreme Giver, and we see it in the life of Jesus, who gave even until His last drop of blood and water. This He expects from us, “give, and it will be given to you” (Luke 6:38). We can never fully repay for the graces received, but we can generously give our praise and worship and serve Him in charity: “as you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me” (Mt 25:40). Am I generous in giving to God what is truly His due?</span></span></span>
  - key: saint
    title: Saint of the Day
    saintName: "Saints Marcellinus and Peter (Martyrs) (A.D. - 304)"
    ref: "Saints Marcellinus and Peter (Martyrs) (A.D. - 304)"
    audio: null
    body: |
      St Marcellinus was a Priest during the reign of Diocletian in Rome and St Peter an exorcist, i.e., someone authorized by the Church to deal with cases of demonic possession. They were said to have been condemned and cast into prison for their faith. In prison the two strengthened the faith of those of their fellow-prisoners who were Christian, and succeeded in making new converts, too, among whom were the jailer Arthemius, his wife and their daughter.</span></span></span></span>



      When they were taken out for execution, Marcellinus and Peter were privately brought to a wood called the Silva Niagra and there beheaded. Their persecutors had already dug the graves before the execution so that the place of their burial would not be known to the other Christians. However, the secret was divulged by the executioner himself who later became a Christian. Two devout women, Lucilla and Firmina, rescued their relics and interred them honourably in the catacomb of Tiburtius on the Via Lavicana. Emperor Constantine had a basilica built over the crypt in which they were buried. He later buried his own mother, St Helena, there, too.</span></span></span></span>



      Pope Damasus I composed a poetic epitaph for Sts Marcellinus and Peter that was apparently founded on the report of their executioner. They are commemorated in the Canon of the Mass (Eucharistic Prayer I).</span></span></span></span>
