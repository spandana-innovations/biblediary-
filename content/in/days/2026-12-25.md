      *How Jesus our Saviour was born</span></span>*</span></span></span></span>

      No Room for Them</span></span></span></span></span>



      Christmas cards feel a bit old-fashioned in our digital age. Apart from their conventional triteness, the problem is deciding who to send them to. In order to ensure you write to those who are likely to send you a card, the lines can get blurred and the list can expand beyond the bounds, along with the cost of the stamps. In the end, you find yourself including everybody who might possibly send you seasonal greetings. The two lists never quite match and there is a last minute rush to fill the gaps. But whatever the defects of the cards, the thought behind them is undeniably good. Also, they are a yearly exercise in handwriting, where otherwise we tend to use just a keyboard.</span></span></span>



      It is a pity that Christmas cards so rarely reflect an authentic message about what the birth of Christ means. What about a simple black and white line drawing of a street with a row of houses, with a few touches – a milk bottle outside the door, an open window with a fluttering curtain – indicating that the houses are lived in. In the centre would be a man knocking at a door. His head is turned towards the street, where a woman is waiting. The street should be recognisable to us all, as the very street where we live. The stranger knocking at the door of your home. ..: *NO ROOM*!</span></span></span>



      The major test for Christmas is easy and fool proof. When last did I/you last stretch out a helping hand to someone in need? open heart or home to somebody in want? Any unanswered knock on my door may be an ignoring of Christ. If HE is not born in my heart and in my home this Christmas, what happened in Bethlehem long ago has not really taken root in my heart.</span></span></span>







      Grafted into the Tree of Life</span></span></span></span></span>



      Words are often a weak method of communication. However, we have to use words, and today’s gospel is an attempt, in simple language, to describe what happened on that extraordinary day, so long ago. It speaks of Jesus being born, and of the second meeting of heaven and earth, on that same night, when the angels appeared to the shepherds. This was the beginning of a process that is still on-going, as I speak. It is an old story that is ever new.</span></span></span>



      “Jesus is the same yesterday, today, and always.” With God there is no such thing as time. All of time is totally present to him right now. God’s work among us is always in process, it never comes to an end. In God’s eyes, Christmas is an everyday event, that involves Jesus knocking on the door of my heart, seeking admission. The God-dimension never changes, the offer is always there, the good news is delivered with greater consistency than the morning newspaper. What happens after that is totally dependent on whether I accept the offer, open the door, and make my heart available as a manger.</span></span></span>



      One important point: when the shepherds heard the message they said, “Let us go to Bethlehem and see it for ourselves.” The life of the Christian is a journey of discovery. It involves coming to find out for myself the truth and the reality of what I had been told by my parents, teachers, or preachers in church. I have to cross that bridge. The gospel is in between two phrases. At the beginning, we are invited to “Come and see,” and, at the end, we are instructed to “Go and tell.”</span></span></span>

      “There was no place for them in the inn.” Probably one of the saddest sentences in world literature. Such a sentence is included as part of the joyful news about the birth of the Messiah. Was the birth really joyful? Census, migration, registration, correction, and statistics! Orders by the rulers are to be obeyed by the ordinary citizens. The birth of the Messiah of the universe happens against this background of calamity and confusion, but unnoticed, quietly, and humbly in a manger. Today’s too, Jesus is born into the world in the midst of loneliness, illness, fears, and miseries of millions. Yet, He brings a piece of heaven into this wounded and broken world. While we join Mary and Joseph in joyfully welcoming Jesus, let us not forget that He is inviting us to bend low and visit Him in a manger in a cowshed.</span></span></span>

    body: |
      Anastasia is believed to be one of those who suffered martyrdom on Christmas day under Emperor Diocletian. Named in the Roman Canon of the Mass she is honoured with a special commemoration in the second Mass of Christmas Day.</span></span></span>



      According to tradition, Anastasia was the daughter of a Roman nobleman named Praetextatus and was married to a pagan named Publius, who died while on a mission to Persia (Iran); whilst caring for her fellow Christians, she was arrested and put on a ship along with another Christian and a number of pagan criminals to be abandoned mid-sea but was saved by Saint Theodata. She was then believed to have been martyred on the island of Palmaria—staked to the ground, limbs outstretched, and a huge fire kindled about her. </span></span></span>



      A church was built in her honour and, in the fifth century, her relics were said to have been transferred to the sanctuary of a church in the Byzantine capital by Gennadius, Patriarch of Constantinople. Significantly, in the fourth century, a Roman Basilica was redecorated by Pope Damasus under the title of “Anastasia”, a name which means Resurrection. Cruciform in structure, this is one of the important churches of Rome. </span></span></span>
