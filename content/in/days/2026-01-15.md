      Reflection: 1



      In Jesus’ time lepers were considered unclean and untouchable, and were not allowed to come close to others. A leper picks up courage to comes to Jesus, and with deep humility and faith he kneels in front of Jesus and says to him, “If you will, you can make me clean.” He trusts in Jesus, and Jesus, breaking the social custom, reaches out to him and ouches him, saying, “I will, be clean.” He shows compassion to those who are suffering. If anyone touched a leper, that person would incur uncleanness, but Jesus touches and cleanses the leper. Whatever be the healing we need, let us approach Jesus with humility and trust. Let us also be kind and compassionate to those who are suffering in any way.



      *Fr (Dr) George Kaitholil, SSP*Reflection: 2



      Today’s Gospel describes Jesus touching a man sick with a severe case of leprosy and healing him instantly. In this miracle we have all the essentials for any miracle, says, Rev. Dr. L. Parker. We have a) a leper; b) a disease, leprosy; c) recognition of the disease by the man who has it; d) the presence of Jesus; e) Faith; f) trust, and g) humility enough for the sick man to ask for help from Jesus. Biblical “leprosy” rarely indicated Hansen’s disease (leprosy proper). Mostly, the term referred to skin diseases like ringworm, psoriasis, *leukoderma*, skin cancer, and *vitiligo.*The suffering of lepers in Biblical times was chiefly due to the way they were treated by the religious society of the day (*Interpreter’s Bible*). They were deemed unclean, unfit to be counted among a people who considered themselves “a kingdom of priests, a holy nation” (Ex 19:6). In addition, lepers were treated as sinners who were being punished by God with this contagious disease. The leprosy given by God as punishment to Miriam, the complaining sister of Moses (Nm 12: 1-3, 9-13) , to Gehazi, the greedy servant of the prophet Elisha (2 Kgs 5:22-27), and to the proud king Uzziah of Judah, also called Azariah (2 Kgs 15:3-5), supported the Jewish belief that leprosy was God’s punishment for sins. Finally, “leprosy” was considered a contagious disease, and, hence, its victims were separated from their families and society. The Mosaic Law, as given in Leviticus, demanded that the priest declare the leper unclean and that the leper a) keep his garments rent and his head bare, b) muffle his beard, c) cry out, “Unclean, unclean,” and d) dwell apart, making his abode outside the camp. As a general rule, when a Jewish leper was healed, he had to go to the local priest for confirmation that he was now clean and was permitted to mix with the general public. Here, the healed leper started evangelizing, sharing the Good News of God’s activity in his life and, so, allowing the Holy Spirit to touch the lives of others.



      <span class="subhead">Life Messages:</span> 1) The strong Faith of the sick man prompted him to violate the Mosaic Law prohibiting him from joining a crowd and approaching Jesus. The sympathy and mercy of Jesus prompted Jesus to violate the Mosaic Law which forbade anyone to touch an untouchable leper. Thus, Jesus teaches the lesson that the essence of Christianity is to touch the untouchable, to love the unlovable, and to forgive the unforgivable.

      Today’s healing story consists of four stages: a) the condition of a leper – suffering, marginalised, impure, and an outcast; b) the cry of the leper – sincere and heartfelt (“if you choose, you can make me clean”; v.40); c) the compassion of Jesus – His actions (He was moved with pity, stretched out His hand, touched him) and His words (“I do choose. Be made clean”; v.41) and d) the cleansing of the leper – through the deeds and words of Jesus, he is made clean. This healing event invites us to approach Jesus with great faith and humility, trusting that He can grant us total wellbeing. Do we trust Him enough?

    body: |
      A native of Thebes in Egypt, Saint Paul was orphaned at 15 but grew up to be wealthy and highly educated. Fearing that the tortures of a terrible persecution might endanger his Christian perseverance, he retired to a remote village. But denounced by his brother-in-law, a pagan, he fled to the desert to escape the Decian persecution, trusting that God would provide for his wants—a confidence that was amply rewarded, for at the place where Providence led him he found the fruit of the Palm tree for food, its leaves for clothing and the water of a brook for drink. When the palm no longer provided him with food, a raven brought him his daily bread.



      The persecution fever of Decius having abated, Paul’s first impulse was to return to the world; but having tasted great delight in prayer and penance, he remained there the rest of his life, 90 years to be precise, in prayer and contemplation.



      God miraculously revealed his existence to Saint Anthony, who sought him for three days. Seeing a thirsty wolf run through an opening in the rocks, Anthony followed it in a bid to quench his own thirst, and there found Paul. Even as Anthony was with him, the raven brought a double allowance of bread. Having spent the night in prayer, Paul told Anthony at the break of dawn that he was about to die and requested to be buried in the cloak given to Anthony by Saint Athanasius. Anthony hastened to fetch it and, on his way back, saw Paul’s soul rise to heaven in glory. As for his dead body, he found it kneeling as if in prayer. Two lions came and dug the grave for him.



      Saint Paul, who died at the age of 113, is venerated as the Patron of weavers.
